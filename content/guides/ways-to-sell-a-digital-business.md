## Key takeaway

The route doesn't change what your business is worth. It changes who finds the buyer, who does the work, how long it takes and how much of the price you keep. Choose by the size of the business and by how much of the process you are willing to run yourself.

## Comparison at a glance

| | Broker | Marketplace | Direct sale |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who finds buyers | The broker, from their own buyer list | Buyers browsing the marketplace | You |
| Who does the work | Mostly the broker | You, using the marketplace's tools | You |
| Typical cost | A commission on the sale price | A listing fee, a success fee, or free, depending on the marketplace | None, apart from your own advisers |
| Vetting before listing | Usually strict; many businesses are turned away | Varies from a light review to full verification | None |
| Help with pricing | Yes | Tools and guides; the decision is yours | None |
| Help with negotiation and transfer | Yes | Limited | None |
| Suits | Larger businesses; owners short of time | Smaller businesses; hands-on owners | Sellers who already know a likely buyer |

Fees and requirements differ between providers and change over time. Read the current terms of any broker or marketplace before you commit.

## Selling through a broker

A broker values the business, prepares the sale documents, presents it to buyers they already know, screens enquiries, and manages negotiation and handover. You pay a commission, taken from the sale price when the deal closes.

**Advantages**

- Access to buyers who have bought before and can pay.
- Someone experienced handles pricing, negotiation and paperwork.
- Your time stays on running the business, which protects its value during the sale.

**Disadvantages**

- The commission is the largest cost of any route.
- Many brokers set a minimum size and accept only businesses with clean, well-documented earnings.
- Exclusivity periods are common: you can't sell elsewhere while the agreement runs.

## Selling on a marketplace

You write the listing, set the asking price, answer buyers' questions and agree terms. The marketplace provides the audience and the tools. **Advantages**

- Lower cost than a broker.
- Open to smaller businesses that brokers won't take.
- You control the price, the pace and who you talk to.

**Disadvantages**

- You do the work: preparing figures, replying to enquiries, filtering out unserious buyers.
- You negotiate for yourself.
- You arrange the safeguards, such as an escrow service for the payment and a written sale agreement.

## Selling directly

A direct sale goes to someone who already knows the business: a competitor, a supplier, a customer, an employee or a partner. There is no listing and no intermediary.

**Advantages**

- No fees.
- The buyer already understands the business, which can shorten due diligence.
- The sale stays private.

**Disadvantages**

- One buyer means no competition on price.
- Approaching a competitor reveals that you want to sell, and shows them your numbers.
- Nobody else checks the terms. Use a lawyer and an escrow service.

## How to choose

- **How large is the business?** Larger, well-documented businesses justify a broker's commission and meet their minimums. Smaller ones usually fit a marketplace.
- **How much time can you give?** A marketplace sale takes real hours for several weeks. If the business would suffer, a broker may cost less than the lost value.
- **How clean are your records?** Whichever route you take, buyers will ask for the same evidence. If your figures aren't ready, fix that first; see [how to sell a digital business](/guides/selling-digital-businesses/).
- **Do you already know a buyer?** If so, a direct approach costs nothing to try. Get an independent view of the price first, so you know what a fair offer looks like.

Routes can be combined. Some owners test the price with a marketplace listing and move to a broker if no suitable buyer appears.

## What stays the same on every route

- **Know your number first.** Work out a realistic range before anyone makes an offer. The [valuation tools](/tools/) show how each figure is reached.
- **Evidence decides the price.** At least 12 months of revenue, cost and traffic records, with access a buyer can verify.
- **Use an escrow service** for the payment, and a written agreement that lists exactly what is included.
- **Plan the handover.** Agree how long you will support the buyer after the sale and what that covers.

## Frequently asked questions

### Which route gets the highest price?

No route guarantees it. A broker's buyer list or a marketplace's audience can create competition between buyers, which helps the price. A direct sale to a buyer who gains a lot from the purchase can also do well. What matters most is the quality of your records and how many qualified buyers see the business.

### Can I list on a marketplace and use a broker at the same time?

Only if your broker agreement allows it. Many are exclusive for a fixed period. Check before you list anywhere else.

### Do I need a lawyer if I sell on a marketplace?

It's sensible for anything beyond a very small sale. A lawyer checks the sale agreement and the transfer of assets. A marketplace connects you with buyers; it doesn't act for you in the deal.

### How long does a sale take?

It varies widely with size, price and how ready your records are. Allow several weeks at least for finding a buyer, due diligence and transfer, and longer for larger businesses.
