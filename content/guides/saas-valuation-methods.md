## Key takeaway

A SaaS valuation method is a way of turning subscription figures into a price. The methods differ mainly in whether they pay for revenue, for profit, or for a forecast. A fast-growing product that reinvests everything looks valuable on revenue and nearly worthless on profit, so the method chosen can matter as much as the numbers. This guide compares the methods. For the inputs and a full walkthrough, see [how to value a SaaS business](/guides/saas-valuation/).

## The five methods at a glance

| Method | Starts from | Best used for | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| ARR or MRR multiple | Recurring revenue | Growing SaaS with predictable subscriptions | Ignores costs unless adjusted |
| Profit multiple (SDE or EBITDA) | Annual profit | Mature, owner-run or steady SaaS | Undervalues businesses investing in growth |
| Discounted cash flow | Forecast cash flows | Larger SaaS with stable retention | Very sensitive to assumptions |
| Growth-plus-margin screen | Growth rate and profit margin | Comparing the quality of several businesses | A screen, not a price |
| Comparable sales | Prices of similar businesses | Checking another method's result | Reliable small-deal data is scarce |

## 1. ARR or MRR multiple

**What it is.** [ARR](/glossary/arr/) (annual recurring revenue) multiplied by a figure reflecting growth and risk. Some marketplaces quote the same thing as a multiple of [MRR](/glossary/mrr/); divide by 12 to convert.

**When it's used.** For subscription businesses whose revenue repeats reliably, especially when profit is being spent on growth.

**Strengths.** Recurring revenue is easy to verify from a billing system. The method gives credit for growth that hasn't reached the profit line yet.

**Weaknesses.** Revenue is not what the owner keeps. An ARR multiple applied without looking at [churn](/glossary/churn/) and gross margin treats fragile, expensive revenue the same as durable, cheap revenue. That's why the multiple has to be adjusted.

## 2. Profit multiple (SDE or EBITDA)

**What it is.** Annual profit multiplied by a figure. For an owner-run business the profit is seller's discretionary earnings, [SDE](/glossary/sde/); for one with a management team it is EBITDA. The difference is explained in [SDE vs EBITDA](/guides/sde-vs-ebitda/).

**When it's used.** By buyers who want income from day one, and for SaaS that has stopped growing quickly.

**Strengths.** It prices what the business earns now, with no reliance on a forecast.

**Weaknesses.** A founder who spends on development or marketing lowers profit and so lowers the value, even if that spending is building the business. The result also depends on which costs are treated as add-backs.

## 3. Discounted cash flow

**What it is.** A year-by-year forecast of cash the business will produce, with each year reduced to reflect risk and delay, then added together.

**When it's used.** Mostly for larger SaaS companies with several years of retention data, by professional investors.

**Strengths.** It forces every assumption into the open: growth, churn, costs and the return the buyer requires.

**Weaknesses.** For a small product with two or three years of history, the forecast is the weakest part of the calculation, and the answer swings widely with small changes to the growth or discount rate.

## 4. Growth-plus-margin screens

**What it is.** A rule of thumb some investors use, often called the rule of 40: add the annual revenue growth rate to the profit margin, both in percent. A total of 40 or more is taken as a sign of a healthy balance between growing and earning.

**When it's used.** To compare businesses that make different trade-offs. A product growing 50% with a −10% margin and one growing 10% with a 30% margin both score 40.

**Strengths.** It stops a buyer rewarding growth bought at any cost, or profit achieved by starving the product.

**Weaknesses.** It doesn't produce a price. It is mostly applied to larger software companies, and different people use different margin measures. {{BRAND}} doesn't use it in its tool; treat it as a screen that may shift where in a range a buyer is willing to go.

## 5. Comparable sales

**What it is.** Looking at what similar SaaS businesses sold for and the multiples those prices implied.

**When it's used.** As a check on any figure from the methods above.

**Strengths.** It reflects real decisions by real buyers.

**Weaknesses.** Small SaaS sales are mostly private, and published figures often describe large or venture-backed companies whose multiples don't apply to a small owner-run product. Asking prices are easier to find than final prices. {{BRAND}} doesn't publish market averages it can't source; see [data sources](/data-sources/).

## Worked example: two methods, one business

A hypothetical B2B SaaS product, three years old, with $10,000 MRR and $4,000 monthly net profit. Revenue grew 5% over the last year, monthly churn is 3%, gross margin is 75%, the largest customer is 10% of revenue and the owner works 15 hours a week. Each of those sits in the tool's baseline band, so there is no adjustment.

**ARR multiple.** ARR is $10,000 × 12 = $120,000. At {{BRAND}}'s default assumption:

| | Multiple | Value |
|---|---|---|
| Low | 2.0× | $240,000 |
| Midpoint | 3.5× | $420,000 |
| High | 5.0× | $600,000 |

**Profit multiple.** Annual profit is $4,000 × 12 = $48,000. At the 3× to 5× reference:

| | Multiple | Value |
|---|---|---|
| Low | 3× | $144,000 |
| High | 5× | $240,000 |

The top of the profit range only just meets the bottom of the ARR range. The ARR midpoint of $420,000 is $420,000 ÷ $48,000 = 8.75× annual profit, which the tool rounds to 8.8×.

Neither answer is wrong. A buyer who plans to live off the income will reason from the profit figures and offer near $240,000. A buyer who believes they can grow the product, or cut costs, will reason from ARR. With growth of only 5%, the first buyer has the stronger argument, and a seller should expect offers toward the low end of the ARR range.

As a screen, this business scores 5 (growth) + 40 (profit margin of $4,000 ÷ $10,000) = 45. That says the business is healthy for its growth rate. It says nothing about which of the two prices is right.

## How to combine the methods

- Use the ARR multiple to set a range, adjusted for churn, margin and concentration.
- Calculate the implied profit multiple. If it is far above what an income buyer would pay, the price depends on growth continuing, so check that it is.
- Use a growth-plus-margin screen to compare several candidates, not to price one.
- Leave discounted cash flow to deals large enough to justify the modelling, and test it with pessimistic assumptions.
- Check the result against comparable sales only where you can verify the final price.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should a small SaaS business be valued on ARR or on profit?

Use both. ARR sets the range and profit tests it. The slower the growth, the more weight buyers put on profit.

### Is an MRR multiple different from an ARR multiple?

Only in the unit. A price of 42× MRR is the same as 3.5× ARR, because ARR is MRR × 12. Check which one a listing is quoting before comparing it with another.

### Does the rule of 40 apply to small SaaS businesses?

It can be a useful screen, but it was popularised for larger software companies and it doesn't give a price. A small product can score well and still be risky because of churn, concentration or dependence on its founder.
