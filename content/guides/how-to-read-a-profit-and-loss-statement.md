## The short answer

A profit and loss statement (P&L) shows what a business earned and spent over a period. Read it from the top down: **revenue**, minus the direct costs of making the sale to reach **gross profit**, minus **operating expenses**, to reach **net profit**. Then test each line against a source you can check. The U.S. Small Business Administration lists financial statements and tax returns among the documents a buyer should evaluate, and recommends you "Consider hiring an attorney and an accountant." See its guide to [buying an existing business](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/plan-your-business/buy-existing-business-or-franchise).

## Read it in five steps

1. **Revenue.** Is it before or after refunds? Does it include one-off income?
2. **Direct costs.** These are the costs tied to each sale, such as goods, shipping and payment fees (see [cost of goods sold](/glossary/cost-of-goods-sold/)). Revenue minus direct costs is [gross profit](/glossary/gross-profit/). A falling margin is a warning.
3. **Operating expenses.** Advertising, software, staff, contractors and rent. Look for missing costs, such as ad spend paid personally.
4. **Net profit.** What is left after all costs. See [net profit](/glossary/net-profit/).
5. **Add-backs.** BizBuySell defines add-backs as "expenses added back to a company's bottom-line profits when determining Seller's Discretionary Earnings (SDE) or EBITDA", and warns that "some add-backs can unrealistically overvalue the company." See [add-backs](/glossary/add-backs/).

## A sample P&L with what a buyer should question

This made-up P&L is for a small online business. The right-hand column shows what a careful buyer asks about each line. It is an illustration, not market data.

| Line | Amount | What to question |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $240,000 | Is this before or after refunds? Does it match payout and bank records? |
| Direct costs (hosting, payment fees, product costs) | −$60,000 | Are all fees and refunds counted, or only some? |
| **Gross profit** | **$180,000** | 75% of revenue. Has the percentage moved month to month? |
| Advertising | −$48,000 | Is any paid personally and missing here? Does it keep the revenue coming? |
| Software and tools | −$12,000 | Would a new owner need the same tools? |
| Contractors | −$30,000 | Are they paid on time and under written agreements? |
| Owner's pay | −$36,000 | Real pay, or a token amount? |
| Other costs (includes $5,000 personal costs and a $4,000 one-off) | −$14,000 | Ask for the detail behind both. |
| **Net profit** | **$40,000** | Check: 180,000 − 48,000 − 12,000 − 30,000 − 36,000 − 14,000 = 40,000. |

## From net profit to SDE

Many buyers look at seller's discretionary earnings (SDE). From the sample:

| Step | Amount |
|---|---|
| Net profit | $40,000 |
| + Owner's pay | $36,000 |
| + Personal costs | $5,000 |
| + One-off cost | $4,000 |
| **SDE** | **$85,000** |

Check: 40,000 + 36,000 + 5,000 + 4,000 = 85,000. Each add-back needs an invoice or bank record behind it. Our guide on [how to calculate SDE](/guides/how-to-calculate-sde/) lists every step.

## Red flags in a P&L

| What you see | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Revenue that does not match payout reports | The figures may not come from the accounts |
| Add-backs larger than net profit | The price may rest on adjustments, not earnings |

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I rely on a P&L the seller made?

Treat it as a claim and test it against bank and payment records. See our [financial due diligence checklist](/guides/financial-due-diligence-checklist/).

### How many months should I ask for?

At least twelve, shown month by month.
