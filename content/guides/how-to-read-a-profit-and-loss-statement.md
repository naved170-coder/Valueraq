## The short answer

A profit and loss statement (P&L) shows what a business earned and spent over a period. Read it from the top down: **revenue**, minus the direct costs of making the sale to reach **gross profit**, minus **operating expenses**, to reach **net profit**. Then test each line against a source you can check. The U.S. Small Business Administration lists financial statements and tax returns among the documents a buyer should evaluate, and recommends you "Consider hiring an attorney and an accountant." See its guide to [buying an existing business](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/plan-your-business/buy-existing-business-or-franchise).

## Read it in five steps

1. **Revenue.** Is it before or after refunds? Does it include one-off income? Ask for a split by product or channel.
2. **Direct costs.** These are the costs tied to each sale, such as goods, shipping and payment fees (see [cost of goods sold](/glossary/cost-of-goods-sold/)). Revenue minus direct costs is [gross profit](/glossary/gross-profit/). Watch the percentage over time: a falling margin is a warning.
3. **Operating expenses.** Advertising, software, staff, contractors and rent. Look for costs that are missing, such as ad spend paid personally or work done by the owner for free.
4. **Net profit.** What is left after all costs. See [net profit](/glossary/net-profit/). Many small-business buyers use the owner's earnings, called SDE, which adds back the owner's pay and personal costs.
5. **Add-backs.** BizBuySell defines add-backs as "expenses added back to a company's bottom-line profits when determining Seller's Discretionary Earnings (SDE) or EBITDA", and warns that "some add-backs can unrealistically overvalue the company." Accept only costs a new owner really will not pay. See [add-backs](/glossary/add-backs/).

## A quick example

A hypothetical seller shows one year: revenue $120,000, direct costs $30,000 and operating expenses $54,000.

**Gross profit = $120,000 − $30,000 = $90,000 (75% of revenue).**

**Net profit = $90,000 − $54,000 = $36,000.**

The seller adds back $12,000 for personal costs. Adjusted profit is $48,000. If a buyer finds that only $8,000 of those costs would really stop, adjusted profit is $44,000. At a multiple of 3, that difference of $4,000 changes the price by $12,000. The figures are an illustration, not market data.

## What to check before you trust it

- **Match to the source.** Revenue should agree with payment processor reports and bank deposits.
- **Use at least 12 months,** shown month by month, and watch for [seasonality](/glossary/seasonality/).
- **Look for cash-basis versus accrual differences.** A P&L can recognize income when it is earned or when cash arrives, so ask which one is used.
- **Ask who prepared it.** A statement from an accountant, or one built from tax returns, carries more weight than one typed into a spreadsheet by the seller.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the difference between a P&L and a balance sheet?

A P&L covers a period of time and shows profit. A balance sheet is a snapshot of assets, debts and owner's equity on one date. Buyers usually want both.

### How many months of P&L should I ask for?

Twelve months at minimum, shown month by month. Two years lets you see trends.

### Can I rely on a P&L the seller made?

Treat it as a claim and test it. Compare it with bank statements, payment reports and tax returns, and have an accountant review it if the price is large. Our [financial due diligence checklist](/guides/financial-due-diligence-checklist/) lists the steps.
