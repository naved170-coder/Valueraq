## Key takeaway

Website valuation is the process of estimating the economic value of a website or online business based on factors such as revenue, profit, traffic, growth, risk and business model. For a profitable site, the usual method is **value = annual net profit × multiple**. The skill lies in choosing the multiple: it should reflect how confident a buyer can be that today's profit will continue.

## Why profit, not traffic, is the starting point

A buyer is paying for future income. Profit is the best evidence of that income because it is what remains after the costs of producing it. Two sites with 200,000 monthly pageviews can earn very different amounts: one might monetise with high-value affiliate offers, the other with low-paying display ads. Traffic tells you where profit comes from and how fragile it might be, which is why it adjusts the multiple rather than setting the price.

The profit figure normally used is **seller's discretionary earnings (SDE)**: revenue minus all the costs of running the site, before the owner pays themselves. Buyers usually ask for the average of the last 12 months, sometimes 24.

## How website valuation works, step by step

### 1. Establish trailing profit

Add up revenue and costs for the last 12 months from records a buyer can verify: affiliate and ad-network reports, payment-processor statements and bank deposits. Subtract every business cost, including content, editing, hosting, software, contractors and link-building if it's ongoing. Divide by 12 for the monthly average.

Only add back costs that genuinely won't continue for a new owner, such as a one-off redesign, and keep evidence for each.

### 2. Choose a base multiple

The multiple converts yearly profit into a price. Marketplaces and brokers often quote it in months: 36 months of profit is the same as 3× annual profit. {{BRAND}}'s default starting range for an established website is 2.5× to 4× annual profit (30 to 48 months). That range is a published platform assumption, not a measured market average; see [data sources](/data-sources/).

### 3. Adjust for risk and quality

Buyers pay more for profit they believe will last and that doesn't need much of their time. The main adjustments are:

| Factor | Pushes the multiple up | Pushes it down |
|---|---|---|
| Age | Several years of steady earnings | Less than one or two years of history |
| Trend | Growing profit and traffic | Declining revenue, recent traffic loss |
| Traffic sources | Mix of search, direct, email, social | Most visits from one channel |
| Monetization | Several revenue sources, owned products | One affiliate programme or ad network |
| Owner time | A few hours a week, documented processes | Near full-time owner involvement |
| Transferability | Own content, clean records, contractors staying | Unclear content ownership, messy books |

{{BRAND}} publishes the exact size of each adjustment in its [methodology](/methodology/#adjustments).

### 4. Produce a range, not a single number

Because the multiple is a judgement, an honest valuation is a range. Present a low, midpoint and high figure, and note which evidence would move a buyer toward each end.

## Worked example

A hypothetical affiliate site:

- Four years old, growing about 10% a year.
- $6,000 average monthly revenue and $4,500 average monthly net profit.
- 70% of traffic from organic search.
- About 10 hours of owner time a week.

Annual profit is $4,500 × 12 = $54,000. Starting from the default midpoint of 3.2×, the site's age (+5%) and growth (+8%) push the multiple up, while traffic concentration (−6%) and affiliate dependence (−3%) pull it down. The combined effect is a small increase, and the estimate comes out at roughly $140,000 to $223,000. ## Factors that don't usually add much value

- **Domain authority scores and similar third-party metrics.** They are estimates made by SEO tools, not measures a search engine uses, and buyers look at actual traffic and revenue instead.
- **Social follower counts** that don't produce traffic or sales.
- **Content volume on its own.** A thousand articles that earn nothing are a maintenance cost, not an asset.
- **Money spent building the site.** Buyers pay for what the site earns, not what it cost.

## Common valuation mistakes

1. **Using the best month instead of an average.** Seasonal peaks overstate value.
2. **Leaving out real costs.** Content and link-building costs that keep traffic growing are ongoing expenses.
3. **Ignoring a traffic decline.** If an algorithm update cut traffic three months ago, the trailing 12-month average still includes the good months. Buyers will look at the recent trend.
4. **Confusing monthly and annual multiples.** "40×" almost always means 40 months of profit, which is 3.3× annual profit.
5. **Treating the estimate as the sale price.** Final prices depend on negotiation and deal terms such as earn-outs or payment in instalments.

## Limitations of any website valuation

Every method relies on the accuracy of the figures and on judgement about the future. Search engine changes, affiliate programme changes and new competitors can change a site's earnings quickly, and no model can predict them. Treat any estimate, including {{BRAND}}'s, as a starting point for negotiation and due diligence, not an appraisal.

## Frequently asked questions

### What multiple do websites sell for?

It varies with age, growth, traffic sources, monetization and workload. {{BRAND}}'s default assumption for established sites is 2.5× to 4× annual net profit before adjustments. We don't publish market averages we can't source.

### Can I value a website on traffic alone?

Not reliably. Traffic that doesn't produce revenue has little value to most buyers. Traffic-based estimates are sometimes used for pre-revenue sites, but they are speculative.

### How long should a site be earning before I sell?

Buyers generally want at least 12 months of earnings history, and many prefer two years or more. Younger sites can sell, usually at a lower multiple.

### Does the sale price include the domain and content?

Usually yes. A website sale normally transfers the domain, content, design, accounts and documentation, and the purchase agreement should list every asset.

### How do I increase my website's value before selling?

Diversify traffic and revenue, reduce the hours you spend on it, document your processes, and keep clean monthly records. See [how to sell a digital business](/guides/selling-digital-businesses/).
