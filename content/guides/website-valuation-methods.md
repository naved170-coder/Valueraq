## Key takeaway

A website valuation method is a rule for turning what you know about a site into a price. Different methods start from different facts, so they can give very different answers for the same site. Knowing which method a number came from tells you how much weight to give it. This guide compares the methods. For a step-by-step walkthrough of the main one, see [how to value a website](/guides/website-valuation/).

## The six methods at a glance

| Method | Starts from | Best used for | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Profit (SDE) multiple | Annual net profit | Profitable, established sites | The multiple is a judgement |
| Revenue multiple | Annual revenue | Quick comparison of similar sites | Ignores costs |
| Traffic-based value | Visitors or pageviews | Sites with traffic but little revenue | Traffic isn't income |
| Asset or replacement cost | Cost to rebuild | Loss-making or very young sites; a price floor | Ignores earning power |
| Discounted cash flow | Forecast future profit | Larger sites with stable earnings | Very sensitive to assumptions |
| Comparable sales | Prices of similar sites | Checking a figure from another method | Reliable sale data is scarce |

## 1. Profit (SDE) multiple

**What it is.** Annual net profit multiplied by a figure that reflects risk. The profit used is seller's discretionary earnings, [SDE](/glossary/sde/): revenue minus all running costs, before the owner pays themselves.

**When it's used.** For almost any website with at least a year of steady profit. It's the method most individual buyers and marketplaces use.

**Strengths.** It prices what the buyer actually receives. It's simple, and it lets two sites be compared on the same basis.

**Weaknesses.** Everything depends on the multiple, and on the profit figure being honest. Costs left out of the profit sheet inflate the result directly.

## 2. Revenue multiple

**What it is.** Annual revenue multiplied by a figure, usually well below the profit multiple.

**When it's used.** As a quick screen across sites with a similar business model and similar margins, or when profit figures aren't available yet.

**Strengths.** Revenue is easier to verify than profit and harder to massage with add-backs.

**Weaknesses.** Two sites with the same revenue can keep very different amounts of it. A site that spends heavily on content or paid traffic looks as valuable as one that doesn't. See [revenue vs profit multiples](/guides/revenue-vs-profit-multiples/) for how to convert between the two.

## 3. Traffic-based value

**What it is.** A value per visitor or per thousand pageviews, multiplied by the site's traffic. Some versions estimate what the same visits would cost to buy through advertising.

**When it's used.** For sites with real traffic and little or no revenue, where there is no profit to multiply.

**Strengths.** It gives a starting figure when earnings-based methods give nothing.

**Weaknesses.** It assumes the traffic can be turned into income, which is the part nobody has proved yet. Visitors differ enormously in value depending on the topic and what they came to do. Treat any traffic-only figure as speculative.

## 4. Asset or replacement cost

**What it is.** What it would cost to recreate the site: the domain, the content at a per-article rate, design and development, and the time needed for the site to gain traffic.

**When it's used.** For loss-making, very young or non-earning sites, and as a floor. A buyer who can build the same thing for less than your price will usually do so.

**Strengths.** It rests on costs that can be quoted and checked.

**Weaknesses.** It ignores earning power. A site earning well is worth more than its articles cost to write, and a site earning nothing may be worth less, because money spent is not the same as value created.

## 5. Discounted cash flow

**What it is.** A forecast of profit for each future year, with each year reduced ("discounted") to reflect risk and the wait, then added up.

**When it's used.** For larger sites with long, stable earnings histories, usually by professional buyers.

**Strengths.** It makes the assumptions explicit: how long earnings last, how fast they grow or decline, and how risky they are.

**Weaknesses.** Small changes in the growth rate or discount rate move the answer a long way. For a small site whose traffic could change after one search-engine update, a five-year forecast is mostly guesswork presented precisely.

## 6. Comparable sales

**What it is.** Looking at what similar sites actually sold for, and the multiples those prices implied.

**When it's used.** As a reality check on any other method.

**Strengths.** It reflects what buyers have paid, not what a model says they should.

**Weaknesses.** Most small website sales are private. Asking prices are public far more often than final prices, and the two can differ. Sites that look similar can differ in ways a summary doesn't show, such as traffic sources or owner hours. {{BRAND}} doesn't publish market averages it can't source; see [data sources](/data-sources/).

## Worked example: two methods, one site

A hypothetical ad-funded site, three years old, with flat revenue, 60% of visits from its largest channel and about 10 hours of owner time a week. It earns $5,000 a month in revenue and $3,000 a month in net profit, and has 300 articles.

**Profit multiple.** Annual profit is $3,000 × 12 = $36,000. Every factor sits in its baseline band, so there is no adjustment. At {{BRAND}}'s default assumption:

| | Multiple | Value |
|---|---|---|
| Low | 2.5× | $90,000 |
| Midpoint | 3.2× | $115,200 (shown by the tool as about $115,000) |
| High | 4.0× | $144,000 |

**Replacement cost.** Suppose comparable articles could be commissioned for $150 each, and design, setup and the domain would cost $5,000. These are illustrative figures, not market data.

- Content: 300 × $150 = $45,000
- Design, setup and domain: $5,000
- **Total: $50,000**

The two methods are $40,000 apart at the low end and $94,000 apart at the high end. The gap is what a buyer pays for traffic and income that already exist, and for not waiting years to find out whether a new site would earn anything. If the site earned nothing, the comparison would run the other way: the profit method would give no value at all, and the $50,000 rebuild cost would be a ceiling, not a floor.

## How to combine the methods

- Start with the profit multiple if the site is profitable.
- Use replacement cost as a floor and as a sanity check on small sites.
- Use the revenue multiple only between sites with similar margins.
- Use traffic-based figures only when there's no profit, and label them as speculative.
- Check the result against any comparable sales you can verify.

When two methods disagree widely, work out why before choosing one. The reason is usually the most useful thing you learn.

## Frequently asked questions

### Which website valuation method is the most accurate?

None is accurate in the sense of predicting a sale price. For a profitable site, the profit multiple is the most widely used and the easiest to check. The final price still depends on negotiation and deal terms.

### How do I value a website that has traffic but no revenue?

Use replacement cost and a cautious traffic-based estimate, and expect a low figure. Buyers pay little for income that hasn't been demonstrated.

### Why do online website value calculators give such different numbers?

They use different methods. A calculator working from estimated traffic will rarely agree with one working from profit. Check what a calculator asks for: if it never asks for profit, it can't be measuring it.
