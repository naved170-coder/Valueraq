## Key takeaway

SDE and EBITDA describe the same business from two seats. SDE answers "what would I earn if I ran this myself?" EBITDA answers "what would this earn if I paid someone else to run it?" The difference between them is the market cost of the owner's job. A multiple only means something when you know which of the two it is applied to.

## Comparison at a glance

| | SDE | EBITDA |
|---|---|---|
| Stands for | Seller's discretionary earnings | Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization |
| Owner's pay | Added back (not deducted) | Deducted at a market salary |
| Typical buyer | An individual who will run the business | A company or investor with a manager in place |
| Typical business | Owner-run, usually under about $1 million in annual profit | Larger, with staff or a management team |
| Personal and one-off costs | Added back | Usually adjusted out as well ("adjusted EBITDA") |
| Size of the figure | Higher | Lower, by roughly the cost of replacing the owner |
| Multiple applied | Lower | Higher |

The size threshold is a rule of thumb, not a fixed line. What decides it is whether the business needs its owner to operate.

## How each is calculated

**SDE** starts from net profit and adds back the owner's salary and benefits, interest, depreciation and amortization, and genuine one-off or personal expenses that a new owner wouldn't incur.

**EBITDA** starts from net profit and adds back interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. The owner's pay stays in as a cost. If the owner pays themselves nothing, or far more than the job is worth, the figure is adjusted to a market salary for the role.

So, for the same business:

**EBITDA ≈ SDE − the market cost of a manager to replace the owner**

## A worked example

A content website has $200,000 in revenue and $90,000 in costs before the owner is paid. The owner works about 25 hours a week and takes no formal salary. A capable part-time manager for that work would cost about $40,000 a year.

| | Amount |
|---|---|
| Revenue | $200,000 |
| Costs before owner pay | $90,000 |
| **SDE** | **$110,000** |
| Less market cost of a manager | $40,000 |
| **EBITDA** | **$70,000** |

Suppose the business sells for $330,000. That price is 3.0× SDE and about 4.7× EBITDA. Both statements describe the same deal. Quoting "4.7×" next to another listing's "3.0×" without saying which earnings figure each uses makes one business look far more expensive than it is.

These numbers are illustrative, not market data.

## Which one should you use?

Use **SDE** when:

- One owner runs the business and a buyer would step into that role.
- The owner's pay is mixed in with profit, or isn't recorded as a salary at all.
- You are comparing small digital businesses on a marketplace, where SDE is the usual basis.

Use **EBITDA** when:

- The business has a manager or team and would keep running without the owner.
- The buyer is an investor or company that will not work in the business.
- You are comparing it with larger companies, which report EBITDA.

{{BRAND}}'s website, app, newsletter and ecommerce tools use annual SDE, as described in the [methodology](/methodology/). SaaS and AI tools use ARR, with profit as a cross-check.

## Common mistakes

- **Applying an EBITDA multiple to SDE.** This overstates the value, because the higher multiple assumes the owner's job has already been paid for.
- **Adding back costs that will continue.** A cost only belongs in the add-backs if a new owner genuinely won't pay it. Software the business needs is not an add-back because the owner happens to dislike it.
- **Ignoring the owner's hours.** Two businesses with the same SDE are not worth the same if one needs 10 hours a week and the other needs 50. The second has a much lower EBITDA.
- **Counting an owner's salary twice.** If the accounts already deduct an owner salary, add it back for SDE once, not twice.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is SDE always higher than EBITDA?

For an owner-run business, yes, because SDE adds back the owner's pay and EBITDA doesn't. If the owner does no work in the business and takes no pay, the two figures are the same.

### How do I convert SDE to EBITDA?

Subtract what it would cost to hire someone to do the owner's work, including the time the owner spends that isn't recorded anywhere. To go the other way, add that cost to EBITDA.

### Why are EBITDA multiples higher than SDE multiples?

Because EBITDA is a smaller number for the same business, and because businesses valued on EBITDA tend to be larger and less dependent on one person, which buyers pay more for.

### Which figure do buyers on a marketplace expect?

For small digital businesses, most listings state profit on an SDE basis. A listing should say which it uses. If it doesn't, ask the seller before comparing its multiple with anything else.
