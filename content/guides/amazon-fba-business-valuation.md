## Key takeaway

An Amazon FBA business ("Fulfillment by Amazon", where a seller sends stock to the marketplace and the marketplace picks, packs and ships) is valued like any other online store: **yearly net profit × a multiple, plus sellable stock at cost.** What makes it different is where the risk sits. Almost all of the sales usually come through one marketplace that sets the fees, the rules and the visibility, and the owner has no control over any of them.

This guide shows how to work out the profit, how the multiple is built, and how to weigh the risks that are particular to FBA. Figures are worked examples from {{BRAND}}'s published default assumptions. They are estimates, not records of real sales. For the general method, see [how to value an ecommerce business](/guides/ecommerce-valuation/), and for a store you run on your own site, [how to value a Shopify store](/guides/shopify-store-valuation/).

## Step 1: Work out the real profit

Marketplace sales reports flatter a business, because the payout is not the profit. Build the figure from the last 12 months.

1. **Net sales**, after refunds and returns.
2. **Cost of goods**: the product, packaging and freight to the warehouse. See [cost of goods sold](/glossary/cost-of-goods-sold/).
3. **Marketplace fees**: the referral fee on each sale, the fulfillment fee, storage fees and any removal or disposal fees.
4. **Advertising**: on-marketplace ad spend and any outside traffic you pay for.
5. **Other costs**: software, prep and inspection, accounting, any virtual assistant or agency.
6. **Add back** the owner's pay and genuinely one-off costs.

Two items are often missed. The first is storage and aged-stock fees, which can rise sharply in the busy season. The second is the cost of inventory that never sold. If last year's profit was helped by selling off old stock at a discount, that is not a repeatable result. Use [SDE](/glossary/sde/) as the profit measure, and treat every add-back with care: BizBuySell warns that "some add-backs can unrealistically overvalue the company."

## Step 2: Apply the multiple

{{BRAND}}'s default range for an ecommerce business is **2.0 to 3.5 times yearly profit**, midpoint 2.7. This is a published platform assumption, not market data; the reasoning is in the [methodology](/methodology/). There is no complete public record of private marketplace-business sales, so treat any single "FBA multiple" quoted online with caution.

![Default profit multiple range for an ecommerce business: Low 2x, Midpoint 2.7x, High 3.5x](/static/img/articles/amazon-fba-business-valuation-default-profit-multiples.svg){: width="720" height="226" loading="lazy" }

### A worked example

An FBA business earns $10,000 a month in profit after all costs, before the owner is paid.

- **Yearly profit:** $10,000 × 12 = $120,000.
- **At 2.0 times:** $120,000 × 2.0 = $240,000.
- **At 2.7 times:** $120,000 × 2.7 = $324,000.
- **At 3.5 times:** $120,000 × 3.5 = $420,000.

That is the range before adjustments and stock.

## Step 3: Weigh channel reliance, the main FBA risk

{{BRAND}}'s default rules reduce the multiple when a large share of sales comes from one channel.

| Share of sales from the largest channel | Default adjustment |
|---|---|
| Under 60% | No adjustment |
| Over 60% to 80% | −6% |
| Over 80% | −12% |

A typical FBA business sells over 80% of its units through the marketplace itself, so the larger reduction often applies. Take the example above with the −12% adjustment.

- **Adjusted multiples:** 2.0 × 0.88 = 1.76, 2.7 × 0.88 = 2.376, 3.5 × 0.88 = 3.08.
- **Value range:** $120,000 × 1.76 = $211,200, $120,000 × 2.376 = $285,120, and $120,000 × 3.08 = $369,600.

![Midpoint price on $120,000 of yearly profit: Baseline $324,000, Over 80% from one channel $285,120](/static/img/articles/amazon-fba-business-valuation-effect-of-channel-reliance.svg){: width="720" height="180" loading="lazy" }

The midpoint falls by $38,880. A seller who also sells on its own website, through email or on other marketplaces reduces that discount, and a buyer will often pay more for that spread. Ask for sales by channel for every month of the year, not just the total.

## Step 4: Look at the risks specific to marketplace sellers

- **Account health.** The seller's account is the business. Ask about warnings, suspended listings, intellectual property complaints and any past suspension. Find out how the account would pass to a new owner under the marketplace's own rules, and do not assume it can be transferred.
- **Product concentration.** If one product earns most of the profit, a stock-out, a bad review run or a copycat competitor can change the picture quickly. Ask for profit by product.
- **Supplier dependence.** Who makes the product, on what terms, and does the buyer get an introduction? A supplier who only trusts the current owner is a risk.
- **Advertising dependence.** If sales fall away when ad spend is cut, the profit is partly bought. Compare organic sales with sales from ads.
- **Rank and reviews.** Listing history and reviews stay with the product listing, but they are earned over time and can be damaged. Check the review trend.
- **Price and fee changes.** The marketplace can raise its fees or change its rules. A thin margin has little room to absorb that.
- **Product safety and compliance.** Some products carry rules for labels, testing or safety. Ask for evidence and take advice.
- **Brand ownership.** Confirm who owns the trademark, the listing rights and the product designs.

## Step 5: Add the stock

As with other stores, inventory is paid for separately at cost, after a count at closing, rather than multiplied. Count what can be sold.

- **Stock at the warehouse.** Ask for the marketplace's own inventory report, not the seller's list.
- **Stock in transit.** Agree who owns goods that are on their way and how they are counted.
- **Aged or stranded stock.** Slow-moving units and units that cannot be sold are worth less than cost. Agree a discount or leave them out.
- **Reserved or returned units.** Check that these are counted correctly.

In the example, the business holds $45,000 of sellable stock at cost. At the adjusted midpoint, the price is $285,120 + $45,000 = $330,120. Write how stock will be counted and valued into the offer, because it is the most common cause of a price dispute. The [working capital](/glossary/working-capital/) page covers how to treat stock, cash and unpaid supplier bills.

## Step 6: Check the evidence

Before you accept a valuation as a buyer, or rely on it as a seller, test it against the source documents.

- Match the marketplace's payment reports to bank deposits for the whole year.
- Ask for read-only access to the seller account's reports, not screenshots.
- Compare the profit figure with supplier invoices and freight bills.
- Ask for the tax records that support the sales figures. Sales tax and import rules depend on where you sell and ship, so ask an accountant.
- Confirm that no money is owed for chargebacks, returns or fees that have not yet been taken.

The U.S. Small Business Administration advises a "thorough, objective investigation" before buying a business and suggests hiring an attorney and an accountant. The [digital business due diligence checklist](/guides/digital-business-due-diligence/) lists the questions in more detail.

## What a buyer will want to see

A prepared seller answers these questions before they are asked. Expect a buyer to request monthly profit and loss statements for at least 12 months, sales and units by product and by channel, the advertising spend and what it returned, the inventory report with ages, supplier contracts or order history, and a list of anything that went wrong in the last year, such as a stock-out, a listing removal or a quality complaint.

A seller who can show clean figures and explain the bad months is more believable than one who only shows the good ones. Buyers also read the pattern. A business whose profit rises every month before the listing date raises the question of whether ad spend was cut, or stock was run down, to flatter the numbers. Explain any such move in writing.

## How to improve the valuation before you sell

| Action | Why it helps |
|---|---|
| Sell through a second channel | Lowers channel reliance and the adjustment that goes with it |
| Spread sales across more products | Reduces dependence on one listing |
| Keep clean monthly accounts | Makes the profit easy to prove |
| Clear aged stock before listing | Removes arguments about stock value |
| Write down the supplier process | Helps the buyer take over without you |
| Cut the owner's weekly hours | A low workload supports a higher multiple |
| Stay steady in the months before the sale | A falling trend pulls the multiple down |

## Frequently asked questions

### How much is an Amazon FBA business worth?

Take yearly net profit before the owner's pay, apply 2.0 to 3.5 on {{BRAND}}'s default assumptions, reduce it for reliance on one channel and other risks, and add sellable stock at cost. A business earning $120,000 a year falls between $240,000 and $420,000 before adjustments and stock.

### Is an FBA business valued on revenue or profit?

Profit. Marketplace fees, advertising and product costs take a large share of each sale, so revenue says little about what the owner actually keeps.

### Is inventory included in the price?

Usually not within the multiple. Sellable stock is normally added at cost after a count at closing, and the offer should say so.

### Can an Amazon seller account be sold?

That depends on the marketplace's own rules and on how the deal is structured. Do not assume it can be transferred. Read the marketplace's current seller terms and ask a lawyer before you agree a price.

### Does selling on only one marketplace lower the value?

On {{BRAND}}'s default rules, yes: more than 80% of sales from one channel reduces the multiple by 12%. The reduction is a judgment about risk, not a penalty, and spreading sales across channels reduces it.
