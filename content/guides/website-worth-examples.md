## Key takeaway

Two websites with the same profit can be worth very different amounts. In the examples below, the multiple applied to yearly profit runs from 2.40 for an affiliate site in decline to 3.92 for a mature site with several income sources. That gap is the difference between about 29 months of profit and about 47 months of profit for the same dollar earned.

Every figure on this page comes from the [website valuation tool](/tools/website-valuation/) using {{BRAND}}'s published default assumptions. They are estimates, not appraisals and not records of real sales.

## The method in three lines

1. **Yearly profit** = average monthly net profit × 12. Profit here means [SDE](/glossary/sde/): revenue minus all costs, before the owner pays themselves.
2. **Multiple** = a default range of 2.5 (low), 3.2 (midpoint) and 4.0 (high) for websites, moved up or down by fixed rules.
3. **Value** = yearly profit × multiple, shown as a low, midpoint and high figure.

Valuing a business from a multiple of its earnings is one form of what the U.S. Small Business Administration calls the market approach, which "compares your business to other similar businesses that have recently sold." The SBA lists it beside an income approach and an assets approach. For small websites the earnings multiple is the usual starting point because it needs only figures the owner already has. The full step-by-step method is in the [website valuation guide](/guides/website-valuation/); this page shows it applied.

## What moves the multiple

The tool adjusts the multiple with five rules for a website. Each rule adds or subtracts a percentage, and the percentages are multiplied together. The total adjustment can't go below 0.5 or above 1.4.

| Factor | What lowers the multiple | What raises it |
|---|---|---|
| Business age | Under 1 year: −20%. 1 to 2 years: −10% | 4 years or more: +5% |
| Revenue trend, last 12 months | Down more than 20%: −25%. Down 5% to 20%: −12% | Up 5% to 25%: +8%. Up 25% to 75%: +15%. Above 75%: +20% |
| Owner hours a week | Over 40: −10%. 20 to 40: −5% | Under 10: +5% |
| Share of traffic from the largest source | Over 80%: −12%. 60% to 80%: −6% | No bonus; 60% or less is the baseline |
| How the site earns | Affiliate: −3% | Own products or mixed income: +3% |

These are {{BRAND}} editorial assumptions, published in the [methodology](/methodology/). They are not averages from a sales database.

## Seven worked examples

### 1. A display-ad content site

A four-year-old recipe site earns $3,200 a month from display ads and keeps $2,400 after hosting and freelance writers. Revenue is up 3% on last year. The owner spends 8 hours a week on it. Google search sends 70% of visits.

- Yearly profit: $2,400 × 12 = **$28,800**
- Adjustments: age +5%, trend 0%, owner hours +5%, one source at 70% −6%, display ads 0%
- Adjustment factor: 1.05 × 1.05 × 0.94 = 1.036
- Multiple: 2.59 low, 3.32 midpoint, 4.15 high
- **Value: $75,000 to $119,000, midpoint $96,000**

### 2. An affiliate site in decline

A three-year-old product review site earns $6,000 a month in affiliate commission and keeps $5,000. Revenue is down 10% on last year. The owner works 12 hours a week. Search sends 85% of visits.

- Yearly profit: $5,000 × 12 = **$60,000**
- Adjustments: age 0%, trend −12%, owner hours 0%, one source at 85% −12%, affiliate −3%
- Adjustment factor: 0.88 × 0.88 × 0.97 = 0.751
- Multiple: 1.88 low, 2.40 midpoint, 3.00 high
- **Value: $113,000 to $180,000, midpoint $144,000**

This site earns more than twice as much as the recipe site, yet its midpoint is only 1.5 times higher. Falling revenue and heavy reliance on one traffic source cost it about a quarter of its multiple.

### 3. A lead-generation site

A five-year-old site collects enquiries for home-improvement contractors and sells them for $9,000 a month. Profit is $5,500. Revenue is flat. The owner spends 25 hours a week on sales calls and account management. No traffic source is above 55%.

- Yearly profit: $5,500 × 12 = **$66,000**
- Adjustments: age +5%, trend 0%, owner hours −5%, concentration 0%, lead generation 0%
- Adjustment factor: 1.05 × 0.95 = 0.997
- Multiple: 2.49 low, 3.19 midpoint, 3.99 high
- **Value: $165,000 to $263,000, midpoint $211,000**

The age bonus is cancelled by the owner's workload. A buyer has to do those 25 hours or pay someone to.

### 4. A site that sells its own products

A site that is two and a half years old sells its own digital templates. Revenue is $7,000 a month and profit is $4,200. Revenue is up 20%. The owner works 15 hours a week. The largest traffic source is 45%.

- Yearly profit: $4,200 × 12 = **$50,400**
- Adjustments: age 0%, trend +8%, owner hours 0%, concentration 0%, own products +3%
- Adjustment factor: 1.08 × 1.03 = 1.112
- Multiple: 2.78 low, 3.56 midpoint, 4.45 high
- **Value: $140,000 to $224,000, midpoint $179,000**

### 5. A site under one year old

A ten-month-old affiliate site already earns $1,200 a month and keeps $900. Revenue is up 60% on its early months. The owner works 15 hours a week. Search sends 90% of visits.

- Yearly profit: $900 × 12 = **$10,800**
- Adjustments: age −20%, trend +15%, owner hours 0%, one source at 90% −12%, affiliate −3%
- Adjustment factor: 0.80 × 1.15 × 0.88 × 0.97 = 0.785
- Multiple: 1.96 low, 2.51 midpoint, 3.14 high
- **Value: $21,000 to $34,000, midpoint $27,000**

Fast growth doesn't make up for a short history. With less than a year of records, a buyer can't rule out a seasonal peak or a one-off spike, so this result carries more uncertainty than the others.

### 6. A mature site with mixed income

A nine-year-old hobby site earns $15,000 a month from ads, affiliate links and a paid membership, and keeps $11,000. Revenue is up 8%. The owner works 5 hours a week because editors run it day to day. The largest traffic source is 40%.

- Yearly profit: $11,000 × 12 = **$132,000**
- Adjustments: age +5%, trend +8%, owner hours +5%, concentration 0%, mixed income +3%
- Adjustment factor: 1.05 × 1.08 × 1.05 × 1.03 = 1.226
- Multiple: 3.07 low, 3.92 midpoint, 4.91 high
- **Value: $405,000 to $648,000, midpoint $518,000**

### 7. A site that loses money

A two-year-old site earns $2,000 a month and costs $2,200 a month to run. The tool returns no estimate. A multiple of earnings can't value a business with no earnings; a loss multiplied by anything is still a loss.

Sites like this do sell, but on other grounds: the domain, the content, the email list or the traffic. Those are judged case by case, and no calculator can do it reliably. If your site is close to break-even, the more useful question is what it would earn after cutting costs a buyer wouldn't need.

## What a calculator can't see

A multiple is quick, and that is also its weakness. The Corporate Finance Institute notes that multiples reduce complex information to a single value and consider a company's position only for a certain period. For a website, the things a multiple misses are usually these:

- **Quality of the records.** Profit you can prove from ad network and bank statements is worth more to a buyer than profit in a spreadsheet.
- **Where the traffic really comes from.** Two sites at "70% from search" differ a great deal if one relies on three pages and the other on three hundred.
- **Costs left out.** Unpaid family help, a personal software licence or the owner's own writing all become real costs for a buyer.
- **One-off spikes.** A single viral month lifts a 12-month average without lifting what the site will earn next year.

Treat the result as a starting range for a conversation, then check it against what buyers ask about in [due diligence](/guides/digital-business-due-diligence/).

## How to get your own number

1. Add up net profit for the last 12 months and divide by 12. Leave your own pay out of the costs.
2. Compare the latest three months of revenue with the same three months a year ago to get the trend.
3. Check your analytics for the share of visits from your largest source.
4. Count the hours you really spend each week.
5. Enter the figures in the [website worth calculator](/calculators/website-worth-calculator/) for a quick answer, or in the full [website valuation tool](/tools/website-valuation/) to see every adjustment.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much is a website that makes $1,000 a month worth?

If $1,000 is monthly profit, yearly profit is $12,000. On {{BRAND}}'s default range of 2.5 to 4.0 times yearly profit that is $30,000 to $48,000 before adjustments. A young site or one that relies on a single traffic source would come out lower, and an older, growing site higher.

### Is a website valued on revenue or on profit?

Content, affiliate, ad-funded and lead-generation websites are valued on profit, because costs vary so much from one site to the next. Revenue multiples are mainly used for subscription software. The guide to [revenue and profit multiples](/guides/revenue-vs-profit-multiples/) explains when each applies.

### Why do some people quote a monthly multiple, such as 35 times?

It is the same idea in different units. A multiple of 35 times monthly profit equals about 2.9 times yearly profit. To convert, divide a monthly multiple by 12.

### Can I value a website that has traffic but no income?

Not with an earnings multiple. A site with no profit is priced on its assets, such as the domain, content and audience, and that depends on what a particular buyer would do with them.

### How accurate is a website worth calculator?

It is as accurate as the figures entered and no more. It applies fixed rules to your numbers and can't check your records, your traffic quality or the market on a given day. Use it to set expectations, not to set a final price.
