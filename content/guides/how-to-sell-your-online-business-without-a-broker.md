## Key takeaway

Selling without a broker means you do the broker's work yourself. **You keep the commission, but you spend your own time on pricing, paperwork, buyer questions and the handover.** It suits businesses with clean records and owners who can give a few hours a week for a few weeks.

This guide lists what a broker does, shows each job as a task you can do, and works through a made-up example so you can see how much a commission is worth against your own costs. The figures are examples, not market data.

## What a broker actually does

A [business broker](/glossary/business-broker/) usually prepares the sale, finds and screens buyers, manages questions and negotiation, and works with the lawyers and the escrow provider until closing. Brokers are normally paid a commission from the sale price when the deal closes. Terms differ, so read any agreement before you sign.

Selling alone means each of those jobs becomes yours. The table shows the swap.

|---|---|---|
| Set the price | Value the business and be ready to explain the figure | [Website Valuation Tool](/tools/website-valuation/) and the [methodology](/methodology/) |
| Prepare the records | Collect 12 months of revenue, costs and traffic proof | [What documents buyers ask for](/guides/what-documents-do-buyers-ask-for/) |
| Write the sales summary | Write a clear, honest listing | [How to write a listing](/guides/how-to-write-a-business-for-sale-listing/) |

## Step 1: Decide whether going alone fits

Selling alone fits best when:

- Your records are clean and you can prove revenue and traffic.
- The business is small enough that a commission would be a large cut of what you keep.
- You can reply to buyers within a day.
- You are comfortable negotiating.

A broker may fit better when the business is large, the records are messy, or you simply do not have the time. Our guide to [broker, marketplace or direct](/guides/ways-to-sell-a-digital-business/) compares the routes side by side.

## Step 2: Put a price on it

The U.S. Small Business Administration advises owners to "use business valuation to set a monetary value" ([SBA](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/manage-your-business/close-or-sell-your-business)). It describes three approaches: income, market and assets.

For a small online business the shortcut is a multiple of yearly profit. {{BRAND}}'s default range for a website is 2.5× low, 3.2× midpoint and 4.0× high (see the [methodology](/methodology/)). These are VALUERAQ's default assumptions, not market data. A site earning $45,000 a year would land at $112,500, $144,000 and $180,000 on those three multiples.

Run the real tool with your own figures, then set an asking price you can defend with the numbers. See [asking price](/glossary/asking-price/).

## Step 3: Gather your records

Without a broker, nobody builds the sales package for you. Collect:

- 12 months of revenue and costs, month by month.
- Payment processor and bank statements that match them.
- Analytics showing traffic by source.
- A list of every account, login and contract that moves to the buyer.

The SBA says to value "intangible assets like brand presence, intellectual property, customer information, and projection of future revenue" ([SBA](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/manage-your-business/close-or-sell-your-business)). A buyer will want proof of each. A neat [data room](/glossary/data-room/) answers most questions before they are asked.

## Step 4: Write the listing

State what the business is, how it earns, the last 12 months of revenue and profit, how many hours you work, and why you are selling. Disclose weak spots such as a traffic drop. Buyers find them in [due diligence](/glossary/due-diligence/) anyway, and an honest listing keeps their trust.

## Step 5: Put it in front of buyers

You have three ways to find a buyer without a broker:

1. **A marketplace.** List the business where buyers already look. On {{BRAND}}, listings are reviewed before they go live and buyers contact you through the site. See [fees](/fees/) for what is free and what is paid, and the [marketplace rules](/marketplace-rules/).
2. **Your own network.** Competitors, suppliers, customers and people in your industry may already know the business.
3. **A direct approach.** Contact a small number of likely buyers yourself.

## Step 6: Screen buyers and answer questions

Many first messages are not serious. Before you share private figures, ask who the buyer is, what they have bought before and how they will pay. A signed [non-disclosure agreement](/glossary/non-disclosure-agreement/) and [proof of funds](/glossary/proof-of-funds/) are common requests. Never share logins before the price and terms are agreed and payment is secured.

## Step 7: Agree terms in writing

Settle more than the price. A [letter of intent](/glossary/letter-of-intent/) should cover:

- The price and how it is paid, including any [earnout](/glossary/earnout/) or [seller financing](/glossary/seller-financing/).
- What is included: domain, accounts, content, customer lists.
- How long you will help after the sale.
- Any [non-compete](/glossary/non-compete-agreement/).

The SBA recommends an attorney review the sales agreement. That is a cost you take on yourself when there is no broker, and it is worth paying.

## Step 8: Close through escrow and hand over

Use an independent [escrow](/glossary/escrow/) service so the buyer's money is held until you transfer the assets, and you are not asked to hand over a website on a promise. Then follow a transfer checklist: domain, hosting, code, payment accounts, email list and social accounts. Our guide to [how escrow works](/guides/how-escrow-works-for-website-sales/) walks through the order of events.

## Worked example: commission vs doing it yourself

This is an illustration with made-up numbers. The 10% commission is an assumed rate chosen for the arithmetic, not a market average and not a VALUERAQ fee. Your own broker terms will differ.

A seller sets an asking price of $150,000. Doing it alone, the seller pays an assumed $1,500 for a lawyer to review the agreement and an assumed $1,500 for escrow fees. These costs are also placeholders.

| Route | Sale price | Commission | Other costs | Money kept |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Broker | $150,000 | $15,000 | $0 | **$135,000** |
| Alone, full price | $150,000 | $0 | $3,000 | **$147,000** |
| Alone, price 5% lower | $142,500 | $0 | $3,000 | **$139,500** |

Check the numbers:

- Broker: $150,000 × 10% = $15,000; $150,000 − $15,000 = $135,000.
- Alone, full price: $150,000 − $3,000 = $147,000.
- Alone, 5% lower: $150,000 × 0.95 = $142,500; $142,500 − $3,000 = $139,500.

![Money kept by the seller in the example: broker at 10% $135,000, sold yourself at full price $147,000, sold yourself 5% lower $139,500](/static/img/articles/how-to-sell-your-online-business-without-a-broker-net-to-seller.svg){: width="720" height="226" loading="lazy" }

**Break-even:** to keep the same $135,000 on your own, you need a sale price of $135,000 + $3,000 = $138,000. That is $12,000 below the asking price, or 8% lower ($12,000 ÷ $150,000).

![Asking price $150,000 against the lowest price that still beats the broker route, $138,000](/static/img/articles/how-to-sell-your-online-business-without-a-broker-break-even.svg){: width="720" height="180" loading="lazy" }

In this example, selling alone comes out ahead unless the lack of a broker costs you more than 8% of the price, through a weaker price or a sale that falls through. Add your own time to the other side of the sum, and remember a broker may reach buyers you cannot.

## Scam and payment-fraud checklist

Most scams target sellers who work alone. Check every line before you hand anything over.

- The buyer will pay through escrow, and you can open the escrow service's own site yourself.
- You never receive a cheque, overpayment or "refund me the difference" request.
- No transfer of the domain or account happens before the money is confirmed as held.
- The buyer has shown proof of funds, and the name matches the contract.
- The buyer will not ask for passwords, only for view-only access during checks.
- You are not rushed by a deadline or a "final offer" that expires in hours.

If any line fails, stop and ask for the step to be fixed or walk away. See [how escrow works](/guides/how-escrow-works-for-website-sales/).

## Confidentiality: NDA steps

1. Share only a short summary, with no name, domain or exact figures, until a buyer shows proof of funds.
2. Ask the buyer to sign a [non-disclosure agreement](/glossary/non-disclosure-agreement/) before you share the name and records.
3. Share records in stages: summary figures first, then reports, then account access.
4. Keep a list of who has seen what.
5. Have an attorney check the agreement, since enforcement differs by country.

## When a broker is worth it

| Your situation | Selling alone fits | A broker may help |
|---|---|---|
| Time | You can answer buyers within a day | You cannot spare the hours |
| Deal size | Small and simple | Large, or several moving parts |
| Records | Tidy and complete | Complex, with several entities |
| Buyer reach | You have a network or audience | You have no route to buyers |
| Negotiation | You are comfortable negotiating | You prefer someone else to do it |

Fees and services differ, so compare in writing before choosing.

## Common mistakes when selling alone

Sellers who skip the broker tend to trip over the same few things:

- **Pricing from hope, not figures.** An asking price with no arithmetic behind it invites a low offer or silence. Show how you got there.
- **Sharing too much too soon.** Give a serious buyer the full numbers only after an NDA and proof of funds. Login details come last, at closing.
- **Letting the business slide.** The sale takes weeks. If revenue falls while you answer emails, the buyer will ask for a lower price. Keep running the business as normal.
- **Agreeing on a handshake.** Anything that matters, such as who keeps the domain or how long you will help, belongs in the written agreement.
- **Skipping the adviser.** A short lawyer review costs far less than a dispute after the sale.

## How long to give it

Plan for the work to come in bursts: a few days to prepare records, a few hours a week answering buyers, then a more intense stretch around the letter of intent and closing. If you cannot protect that time while still running the business, a broker's fee may be buying you something real.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it legal to sell a business without a broker?

In most places an owner may sell their own business without a broker. Rules on contracts, tax and transfers depend on the country, so ask a lawyer and an accountant where you live.

### Where is the best place to sell a small business without a broker?

It depends on the business. A marketplace reaches buyers you do not know, your own network reaches people who already understand the business, and a direct approach works if you already have a likely buyer. Many sellers use more than one. See [broker, marketplace or direct](/guides/ways-to-sell-a-digital-business/).

### How long does it take to sell on your own?

It varies with price, records and buyer interest. Our guide on [how long it takes to sell an online business](/guides/how-long-to-sell-an-online-business/) explains what speeds it up or slows it down.

### Do I still need a lawyer?

Yes, for the sales agreement at least. The SBA recommends an attorney review it.

### Is it safe to sell without a broker?

Yes, if you use escrow, check proof of funds and never hand over accounts before payment is confirmed.

### Do I need an NDA?

Use one before you share the business name, the domain or exact figures, and have it reviewed by an attorney.
