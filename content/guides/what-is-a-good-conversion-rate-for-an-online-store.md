## Key takeaway

Searching for "a good conversion rate" usually turns up one tidy percentage. The honest answer is that **there is no single number that is good for every store.** A shop selling $15 accessories and a shop selling $900 furniture should not expect the same share of visitors to buy, and a store with loyal email customers will show a different rate from one that buys all its traffic from ads.

What matters is whether **your** rate is stable or rising, whether you understand why, and whether you can show a buyer the numbers behind it. This guide gives you the formula, a worked example with the arithmetic, a way to read your rate by traffic source, and what a half-point change can mean for the price of a store.

Every figure below is a made-up example for teaching. None of it is market data and none is a promise of results.

## The formula

**Conversion rate = orders ÷ sessions × 100**

An example store had 40,000 sessions in a month and 800 orders. That is 800 ÷ 40,000 × 100 = **2.0%**.

Before you compare any rate, settle three things, because each changes the answer:

- **The base.** Sessions, unique visitors and product page views give different percentages for the same orders. Our [conversion rate](/glossary/conversion-rate/) entry shows the same store at 2.0%, 3.2% and 5.0% depending on the base.
- **What counts as a conversion.** Completed orders are the cleanest choice for a store. Add-to-cart or email sign-ups are useful, but they are different measures.
- **The time window.** A single week can mislead. Use full months, and compare with the same month a year earlier if the store is seasonal.

## Why one "good" number does not exist

Several things move a store's rate without the store getting better or worse:

1. **Price and purchase size.** A low-priced item is an easier decision than an expensive one that people research for days.
2. **Traffic source.** Visitors who already know the brand, such as email subscribers, usually behave differently from visitors who clicked an ad once.
3. **Device.** Phone and desktop visitors often convert at different rates for the same store. Check yours separately.
4. **New or returning customers.** Returning customers have already decided they trust you.
5. **Season and promotions.** A sale lifts the rate for a few days and can pull sales forward from later weeks.
6. **How traffic is measured.** Bots, internal visits and tracking gaps change the number of sessions, and therefore the rate.

Because of this, an average from another store, or from a report that mixes thousands of different shops, tells you very little about whether your store is healthy.

## Worked example: one store, five traffic sources

The overall rate hides large differences. Here is the same example store from above, split by where visitors came from:

| Source | Sessions | Orders | Conversion rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Email | 4,000 | 160 | 4.0% |
| Direct | 6,000 | 168 | 2.8% |
| Organic search | 14,000 | 280 | 2.0% |
| Paid social | 12,000 | 168 | 1.4% |
| Referral | 4,000 | 24 | 0.6% |
| **Total** | **40,000** | **800** | **2.0%** |

![Example store: conversion rate by traffic source: Email 4%, Direct 2.8%, Organic search 2%, Paid social 1.4%, Referral 0.6%](/static/img/articles/what-is-a-good-conversion-rate-for-an-online-store-by-source.svg){: width="720" height="318" loading="lazy" }

The store's 2.0% is an average of five very different groups. It is not "good" or "bad" until you ask what each group is doing and what it costs to reach.

### When the rate falls but the business improves

Next month the owner spends more on paid social and sends 20,000 paid sessions instead of 12,000. Every source converts at the same rate as before:

| Source | Sessions | Orders |
|---|---|---|
| Email | 4,000 | 160 |
| Direct | 6,000 | 168 |
| Organic search | 14,000 | 280 |
| Paid social | 20,000 | 280 |
| Referral | 4,000 | 24 |
| **Total** | **48,000** | **912** |

Overall conversion rate: 912 ÷ 48,000 × 100 = **1.9%**. The headline rate dropped by a tenth of a point, yet the store took 112 more orders. Nothing on the site got worse. The mix of visitors changed.

This is why a buyer who sees a falling conversion rate will ask which sources changed before drawing a conclusion. Whether the extra orders were worth it depends on what the ads cost, which you can check with [return on ad spend](/glossary/return-on-ad-spend/).

## Look at revenue per visitor as well

Conversion rate says how many visitors buy. It does not say how much they spend. Combine it with [average order value](/glossary/average-order-value/):

**Revenue per session = conversion rate × average order value**

| | Store A | Store B |
|---|---|---|
| Sessions | 40,000 | 40,000 |
| Conversion rate | 3.0% | 1.5% |
| Average order value | $30 | $90 |
| Orders | 1,200 | 600 |
| Revenue | $36,000 | $54,000 |
| [Revenue per visitor](/glossary/revenue-per-visitor/) | $0.90 | $1.35 |

Store A converts twice as often, but Store B earns more from the same traffic. Judging either store by conversion rate alone would point the wrong way.

## What a half-point change can be worth

Take the first example store again: 40,000 sessions a month, an average order value of $60 and a 2.0% conversion rate.

1. Orders: 40,000 × 2.0% = 800. Revenue: 800 × $60 = **$48,000 a month.**
2. Suppose a better product page lifts the rate to 2.5%. Orders: 40,000 × 2.5% = 1,000. Revenue: **$60,000 a month**, which is $12,000 more.
3. Assume 30% of each extra sale is left as profit after product cost, shipping, payment fees and ads (an assumption for this example; use your own figure). Extra profit: $12,000 × 0.30 = **$3,600 a month, or $43,200 a year.**

A buyer values an ecommerce business mainly as a multiple of annual profit. VALUERAQ's default range for ecommerce is 2.0x to 3.5x, with 2.7x in the middle (see the [methodology](/methodology/)). That is our own editorial default, not market data. Applied to the extra $43,200 of yearly profit:

| Multiple | Extra profit | Value added |
|---|---|---|
| 2.0x (low) | $43,200 | $86,400 |
| 2.7x (middle) | $43,200 | $116,640 |
| 3.5x (high) | $43,200 | $151,200 |

![Value added by +0.5 points, in dollars, at three multiples: Low 2.0x 86400, Mid 2.7x 116640, High 3.5x 151200](/static/img/articles/what-is-a-good-conversion-rate-for-an-online-store-value-at-multiples.svg){: width="720" height="226" loading="lazy" }

Read this as the *ceiling* of what an improvement could be worth, not a forecast. A buyer will only pay for a gain they believe will last. A rate that jumped for one month because of a sale usually earns no extra value. A rate that has risen steadily over six months, with clean records behind it, often does.

## What a buyer reads into your conversion rate

When a store is for sale, a careful buyer looks at conversion rate for three things. The SBA tells buyers to carry out a "thorough, objective investigation" of an existing business, and the rate is part of that.

- **Stability.** Months that move within a narrow range suggest a predictable business. Wild swings invite questions.
- **Source of growth.** A rise driven by repeat customers and organic search looks more durable than one driven by a short discount campaign.
- **Fit with the story.** If the listing says "growing", but the rate has slid while ad spend rose, the buyer will notice. Our guide to [ecommerce valuation](/guides/ecommerce-valuation/) covers what else buyers check.

## Checklist: find out whether your rate is good

1. Export at least 12 months of sessions and orders from your store and analytics.
2. Calculate the rate for each full month. Mark months with sales or outages.
3. Split by traffic source and by device.
4. Split new customers from returning ones if your tools allow it.
5. Calculate revenue per session, not only the rate.
6. Compare each month with the same month a year earlier.
7. Write down what changed in any month the rate moved by more than a few tenths of a point.
8. Keep the reports. A buyer's due diligence will ask to see them.

## Common mistakes

- **Comparing with a number you cannot trace.** If you cannot tell what base, period and store type it covers, it is not a fair comparison.
- **Chasing the rate and ignoring margin.** A discount can lift conversion and lower profit.
- **Mixing bases.** Do not compare an order-per-session rate with an order-per-visitor rate.
- **Judging a week.** Small samples are noisy. A store with 200 sessions a day can swing by a full point from luck alone.
- **Ignoring tracking.** If your analytics changed, an old and new rate may not be comparable.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a good conversion rate for an ecommerce store?

It depends on your product, price and traffic. A good rate for your store is one that is stable or rising over full months, and that holds up when you split it by traffic source. Comparing with your own history is more reliable than comparing with other stores.

### How do I calculate my conversion rate?

Divide orders by sessions and multiply by 100. For example, 800 orders from 40,000 sessions is 2.0%. State whether you used sessions or visitors every time you quote it.

### Does a higher conversion rate always mean a more valuable store?

No. A store can convert well and earn little profit if its margins are thin or its orders are small. Buyers mostly price stores on profit, so look at conversion rate together with average order value and margin.

### Can I just buy more traffic to grow?

You can, but extra paid traffic often converts less well than traffic that already knows you, so the overall rate may dip while orders grow, as in the example above. Check whether each extra order still earns a profit after ad costs.
