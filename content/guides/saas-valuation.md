## Key takeaway

SaaS valuation is the process of estimating what a subscription software business is worth, usually by applying a multiple to its recurring revenue and adjusting for how durable and profitable that revenue is. The formula is simple, **value = ARR × multiple**, but the multiple depends on a handful of metrics every serious buyer will ask for.

## Why SaaS is valued on recurring revenue

Subscription revenue repeats. A customer paying $50 a month today will probably pay next month too, which makes SaaS revenue more predictable than one-off sales or ad income. That predictability is why SaaS is often valued on revenue, while content sites and stores are usually valued on profit.

For small, owner-run SaaS businesses, many buyers look at both. An [ARR multiple](/guides/saas-valuation-multiples/) captures growth potential; a profit multiple shows what the business earns today. {{BRAND}}'s tool calculates the ARR-based value and shows the profit multiple it implies.

## The metrics that set the multiple

### Annual recurring revenue (ARR)

[ARR](/glossary/arr/) is the yearly value of active subscriptions, usually [MRR](/glossary/mrr/) × 12. Exclude one-off fees and services. Buyers want to see MRR month by month, split into new, expansion, contraction and churned MRR.

### Growth

Growth tells a buyer what ARR is likely to be next year. Measure it by comparing recent MRR with the same point a year earlier. Consistent growth is worth more than one strong quarter.

### Churn

[Churn](/glossary/churn/) is the share of customers (or revenue) lost each month. It compounds: 3% monthly churn loses about 31% of customers a year; 8% loses about 63%. High churn means growth has to replace lost revenue before it can add any.

### Gross margin

[Gross margin](/glossary/gross-margin/) is revenue minus direct costs (hosting, third-party APIs, payment fees, sometimes support) divided by revenue. Software typically keeps most of each dollar. Products with heavy infrastructure or API costs keep less.

### Customer concentration

If one customer provides a large share of revenue, losing them would hurt badly. Buyers discount for [customer concentration](/glossary/customer-concentration/), especially above roughly 15–30% from one account.

### Founder dependence

If the founder does sales, support and development, the buyer inherits that workload or must hire for it. Documented processes and a team or contractors who stay reduce the discount.

## The metrics buyers check, at a glance

| Metric | What it measures | How to calculate it | Why buyers care |
|---|---|---|---|
| MRR / ARR | Recurring revenue | Active subscriptions per month; ARR = MRR × 12 | Sets the starting number |
| Growth | Direction of ARR | (MRR now − MRR a year ago) ÷ MRR a year ago | Predicts next year's ARR |
| Churn | Customers or revenue lost | Customers lost in a month ÷ customers at its start | Decides how much growth is wasted |
| Net revenue retention | Revenue kept and expanded from existing customers | Revenue now from last year's customers ÷ their revenue then | Above 100% means customers spend more over time |
| Gross margin | What is left after direct costs | (Revenue − direct costs) ÷ revenue | Shows how much of each dollar can become profit |
| Customer concentration | Dependence on a few accounts | Largest customer's revenue ÷ total revenue | A single loss can sink the business |
| Rule of 40 | Growth plus profit | Growth rate % + profit margin % | A score of 40% or more is the usual target, a rough check only |

Stripe defines churn as "the percentage of total customers who discontinue using a product or service during a given period" and net revenue retention as "the percentage of revenue that is retained from existing customers over a given period of time." The Corporate Finance Institute describes the Rule of 40 as a back-of-the-envelope calculation that "oversimplifies a company's financial prospects by focusing on just two metrics." Use it to compare, not to decide.

## ARR multiple or profit multiple?

Buyers of small SaaS use two methods, and the choice follows the business.

| | ARR multiple | Profit multiple (SDE) |
|---|---|---|
| Starts from | Annual recurring revenue | Yearly profit before the owner's pay |
| Best when | Growing, low churn, margins still improving | Stable, owner-run, profit is already high |
| Rewards | Growth and retention | Cash the buyer receives from day one |
| Risk | Price can run ahead of profit | Ignores growth potential |

In the worked example below, ARR is $240,000 and annual profit is $9,000 × 12 = $108,000. Every 1× of profit multiple is worth $108,000, so a buyer who thinks in profit terms and pays 4× is paying $432,000. The ARR method gives $480,000 to $1,200,000 at the default 2× to 5×, and the gap between the two is what a buyer is paying for growth. A seller who can show that growth is real should anchor on ARR. A seller whose growth has flattened should expect buyers to lean on profit.

## How the valuation is calculated

1. **ARR** = current MRR × 12.
2. **Base multiple**: {{BRAND}}'s default assumption for small SaaS is 2× to 5× ARR (midpoint 3.5×). It's a published editorial assumption; see [data sources](/data-sources/).
3. **Adjustments** for growth, churn, gross margin, customer concentration, age and owner time. The size of each is published in the [methodology](/methodology/#adjustments).
4. **Range** = ARR × adjusted low, mid and high multiples.
5. **Cross-check** against profit: divide the midpoint by annual profit. If the implied profit multiple is very high, the price relies on growth continuing.

## Worked example

A hypothetical B2B SaaS business:

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| MRR | $20,000 (ARR $240,000) |
| Monthly net profit | $9,000 |
| Growth, last 12 months | 30% |
| Monthly churn | 3% |
| Gross margin | 82% |
| Largest customer | 8% of revenue |
| Age | 3 years |
| Owner time | 25 hours a week |

Fast growth adds 15% to the multiple and a gross margin above 80% adds 5%, while 25 hours of owner time takes off 5%. Churn, concentration and age sit in their baseline bands. The combined adjustment is about +15%, giving an adjusted multiple of roughly 2.3× to 5.7× ARR: around $551,000 to $1,380,000, with a midpoint near $964,000. At the midpoint, that's about 8.9× annual profit, which tells a profit-focused buyer the price depends on growth continuing. The [SaaS Valuation Tool](/tools/saas-valuation/) opens with these figures.

Rule of 40 for the same business: growth of 30% plus a profit margin of 45% ($108,000 ÷ $240,000) is 75%, comfortably above the usual 40% target. That is one reason this example sits at the upper half of the range.

## How to raise a SaaS valuation before selling

- **Cut churn first.** Find why customers leave, fix the top reason and show the new trend for at least three months.
- **Spread the revenue.** Lower the share from your largest customer; each point moves the discount.
- **Document the work.** Write down deployment, support and billing so a buyer can run it without you.
- **Reconcile the books.** MRR in your billing system should tie to bank deposits in the profit and loss statement.
- **Clear technical debt** a code review would flag: outdated dependencies, missing backups, undocumented access.

See also the [documents buyers ask for](/guides/what-documents-do-buyers-ask-for/).

## What buyers will ask for

- Billing-system exports showing MRR movements and customer counts by month.
- Churn by month and by plan, and cohort retention if available.
- A profit and loss statement reconciled to bank deposits.
- Hosting and third-party service invoices, to confirm gross margin.
- A code and infrastructure review: dependencies, security, documentation, deployment.
- Customer contracts and terms of service, to check they can be transferred.

## Limitations

- Venture-backed SaaS companies are often valued on forward revenue and market size, which this approach doesn't capture.
- Expansion revenue and net revenue retention can justify a higher multiple; they aren't modelled in {{BRAND}}'s tool.
- The default multiple is an assumption. Actual prices depend on buyer type, deal structure and market conditions.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a typical SaaS valuation multiple?

It depends heavily on size, growth, churn and profitability, and widely quoted figures often describe large public or venture-backed companies. For small SaaS, {{BRAND}} starts from 2× to 5× ARR and adjusts.

### Is ARR or profit more important?

For small SaaS, both. ARR shows scale and potential; profit shows what the buyer earns from day one. Many buyers of owner-run SaaS put heavy weight on profit.

### How do annual plans affect MRR?

Divide the annual plan's price by 12 and include it in MRR for each month of the contract. Counting the full payment in the month it arrives overstates MRR.

### Does high churn make a SaaS business unsellable?

No, but it lowers the multiple. A buyer who understands why customers leave and can fix it may still pay a fair price. If you are deciding where to sell my SaaS company, see [where to sell a SaaS company](/guides/how-to-sell-a-saas-business/).

### What is a good churn rate for SaaS?

There is no universal number, and published benchmarks differ by customer size and price. Buyers care more about the trend and the reason: falling churn with a clear fix is worth more than a low number nobody can explain.

### What is net revenue retention and why does it matter?

It is the share of revenue kept and grown from existing customers over a period. Above 100% means upgrades outweigh cancellations, so the business grows even with no new customers.

### Should I value my SaaS on ARR or on profit?

Use both and compare. If the two answers are far apart, the difference is the price of growth, and you need evidence to defend it.

### How long should a SaaS business operate before selling?

Buyers want at least 12 months of billing history, and more is better. Younger products can sell, usually on a lower multiple.

### How is SaaS valuation different from website valuation?

SaaS is usually valued on recurring revenue with an emphasis on churn and growth; websites on profit, with an emphasis on traffic sources.
