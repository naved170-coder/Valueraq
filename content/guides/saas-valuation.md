## Key takeaway

SaaS valuation is the process of estimating what a subscription software business is worth, usually by applying a multiple to its recurring revenue and adjusting for how durable and profitable that revenue is. The formula is simple, **value = ARR × multiple**, but the multiple depends on a handful of metrics every serious buyer will ask for.

## Why SaaS is valued on recurring revenue

Subscription revenue repeats. A customer paying $50 a month today will probably pay next month too, which makes SaaS revenue more predictable than one-off sales or ad income. That predictability is why SaaS is often valued on revenue, while content sites and stores are usually valued on profit.

For small, owner-run SaaS businesses, many buyers look at both. An ARR multiple captures growth potential; a profit multiple shows what the business earns today. {{BRAND}}'s tool calculates the ARR-based value and shows the profit multiple it implies.

## The metrics that set the multiple

### Annual recurring revenue (ARR)

[ARR](/glossary/arr/) is the yearly value of active subscriptions, usually [MRR](/glossary/mrr/) × 12. Exclude one-off fees and services. Buyers want to see MRR month by month, split into new, expansion, contraction and churned MRR.

### Growth

Growth tells a buyer what ARR is likely to be next year. Measure it by comparing recent MRR with the same point a year earlier. Consistent growth is worth more than one strong quarter.

### Churn

[Churn](/glossary/churn/) is the share of customers (or revenue) lost each month. It compounds: 3% monthly churn loses about 31% of customers a year; 8% loses about 63%. High churn means growth has to replace lost revenue before it can add any.

### Gross margin

[Gross margin](/glossary/gross-margin/) is revenue minus direct costs (hosting, third-party APIs, payment fees, sometimes support) divided by revenue. Software typically keeps most of each dollar. Products with heavy infrastructure or API costs keep less.

### Customer concentration

If one customer provides a large share of revenue, losing them would hurt badly. Buyers discount for [customer concentration](/glossary/customer-concentration/), especially above roughly 15–30% from one account.

### Founder dependence

If the founder does sales, support and development, the buyer inherits that workload or must hire for it. Documented processes and a team or contractors who stay reduce the discount.

## How the valuation is calculated

1. **ARR** = current MRR × 12.
2. **Base multiple**: {{BRAND}}'s default assumption for small SaaS is 2× to 5× ARR (midpoint 3.5×). It's a published editorial assumption; see [data sources](/data-sources/).
3. **Adjustments** for growth, churn, gross margin, customer concentration, age and owner time. The size of each is published in the [methodology](/methodology/#adjustments).
4. **Range** = ARR × adjusted low, mid and high multiples.
5. **Cross-check** against profit: divide the midpoint by annual profit. If the implied profit multiple is very high, the price relies on growth continuing.

## Worked example

A hypothetical B2B SaaS business:

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| MRR | $20,000 (ARR $240,000) |
| Monthly net profit | $9,000 |
| Growth, last 12 months | 30% |
| Monthly churn | 3% |
| Gross margin | 82% |
| Largest customer | 8% of revenue |
| Age | 3 years |
| Owner time | 25 hours a week |

Fast growth adds 15% to the multiple and a gross margin above 80% adds 5%, while 25 hours of owner time takes off 5%. Churn, concentration and age sit in their baseline bands. The combined adjustment is about +15%, giving an adjusted multiple of roughly 2.3× to 5.7× ARR: around $551,000 to $1,380,000, with a midpoint near $964,000. At the midpoint, that's about 8.9× annual profit, which tells a profit-focused buyer the price depends on growth continuing. ## What buyers will ask for

- Billing-system exports showing MRR movements and customer counts by month.
- Churn by month and by plan, and cohort retention if available.
- A profit and loss statement reconciled to bank deposits.
- Hosting and third-party service invoices, to confirm gross margin.
- A code and infrastructure review: dependencies, security, documentation, deployment.
- Customer contracts and terms of service, to check they can be transferred.

## Limitations

- Venture-backed SaaS companies are often valued on forward revenue and market size, which this approach doesn't capture.
- Expansion revenue and net revenue retention can justify a higher multiple; they aren't modelled in {{BRAND}}'s tool.
- The default multiple is an assumption. Actual prices depend on buyer type, deal structure and market conditions.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a typical SaaS valuation multiple?

It depends heavily on size, growth, churn and profitability, and widely quoted figures often describe large public or venture-backed companies. For small SaaS, {{BRAND}} starts from 2× to 5× ARR and adjusts.

### Is ARR or profit more important?

For small SaaS, both. ARR shows scale and potential; profit shows what the buyer earns from day one. Many buyers of owner-run SaaS put heavy weight on profit.

### How do annual plans affect MRR?

Divide the annual plan's price by 12 and include it in MRR for each month of the contract. Counting the full payment in the month it arrives overstates MRR.

### Does high churn make a SaaS business unsellable?

No, but it lowers the multiple. A buyer who understands why customers leave and can fix it may still pay a fair price.

### How is SaaS valuation different from website valuation?

SaaS is usually valued on recurring revenue with an emphasis on churn and growth; websites on profit, with an emphasis on traffic sources. See [website vs SaaS valuation](/guides/website-vs-saas-valuation/).
