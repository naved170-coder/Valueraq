## Key takeaway

This page is about buying an existing SaaS **business**, with customers and revenue, or building a new one from scratch. It is not about choosing software tools for your own company.

The two routes fail in different places. A build fails when nobody wants the product. A purchase fails when you paid for revenue that does not survive the handover. Buying moves the risk from *will anyone want this* to *is what I am being shown real*, and the second question is the easier one to answer with evidence.

## Comparison at a glance

| | Buy | Build |
|---|---|---|
| Upfront cost | A multiple of revenue or profit — {{BRAND}}'s default assumption is 2× to 5× ARR | Low cash, high time |
| Time to first revenue | Immediate | Usually 6 to 18 months, often never |
| What you are buying | Customers, revenue history, code, brand | An idea and your own effort |
| Main risk | Overpaying, or revenue that leaves with the seller | Nobody wants the product |
| What you need | Capital, and the ability to assess a business | Time, product skill, and tolerance for no income |
| Evidence available before you commit | Billing data, cohorts, code, support history | None — you find out after building |
| Hardest part | Due diligence | Distribution |

The multiple range is a published platform assumption, not a measured market statistic. See the [methodology](/methodology/).

## Worked example: a $5,000 MRR product

All figures below are invented to show the arithmetic. The multiple range is {{BRAND}}'s default for SaaS, which is **2.0 to 5.0 times ARR** with 3.5 as the midpoint (see the [methodology](/methodology/)).

**Buying.** A product with $5,000 in [monthly recurring revenue](/glossary/mrr/) has an [ARR](/glossary/arr/) of $5,000 × 12 = $60,000.

| Multiple | Calculation | Price |
|---|---|---|
| 2.0 (low) | $60,000 × 2.0 | $120,000 |
| 3.5 (midpoint) | $60,000 × 3.5 | $210,000 |
| 5.0 (high) | $60,000 × 5.0 | $300,000 |

**Building.** Suppose you need 12 months to reach $5,000 MRR and your living costs are $3,000 a month, with $300 a month for hosting and tools. Cash cost: 12 × ($3,000 + $300) = $39,600. That is far below the purchase price, but it assumes the product reaches $5,000 MRR in 12 months, which is the uncertain part. If it takes 24 months, the same arithmetic gives $79,200. If it never gets there, you have spent the money and own no revenue.

| | Buy (midpoint) | Build |
|---|---|---|
| Cash out | $210,000 | $39,600 if 12 months, $79,200 if 24 |
| Revenue on day one | $5,000 a month | $0 |
| Outcome known before you spend | Mostly | No |

The comparison does not decide the question. It shows that building trades a lower cash cost for a longer wait and a risk of no revenue.

## What buying actually costs

The purchase price is the visible part. Budget for four more:

1. **Due diligence.** A developer to review the code and a few hours of your own time reading billing exports. Skipping this is how people buy businesses whose revenue is concentrated in one customer who is about to leave.
2. **Transition.** The seller's involvement for a handover period, usually agreed in the deal. Without it you inherit a codebase nobody can explain.
3. **The things the seller did for free.** Owner support, owner sales, owner content. Price the cost of replacing their hours, because the profit figure you were shown probably does not include them.
4. **Deferred work.** Dependency upgrades, security fixes and technical debt the seller postponed while preparing to sell.

A business bought at 3× ARR with a year of neglected maintenance behind it is not a 3× purchase.

## What building actually costs

The cash cost is small. The real cost is the months spent before you know whether anyone will pay, and the fact that most of that work is not the part people expect.

Writing the product is the easier half. Finding customers is the hard half, and it is the half a purchase skips entirely — an existing SaaS business comes with an acquisition channel that already works, however modestly.

Building makes sense when you have something a purchase cannot give you: a specific unmet need you understand from the inside, usually from having lived it in a job or another business.

## When buying wins

- You have capital and want income sooner than a build can produce it.
- You can assess a business properly — or pay someone who can.
- You want to improve something that works rather than discover whether anything will.
- You are buying into a market you already understand, so you can tell whether the seller's growth story is plausible.

## When building wins

- You have a specific problem you have personally hit and know others hit too.
- You have the time, and income from elsewhere while you spend it.
- Nothing on the market at your budget is close to what you want to own.
- The product you have in mind depends on an insight that would be hard to buy — a relationship, a dataset, a distribution channel you already control.

## The middle path

A third option sits between them: buy a small, working product and rebuild it toward what you wanted to build. You inherit customers, revenue and a distribution channel, then change the product underneath them.

This works when the existing customers are close to the ones you want. It fails when they are not, because you end up supporting a product you do not want while building one nobody has asked for yet.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it cheaper to build than to buy?

In cash, almost always. In total cost, often not — a year of your time has a value, and a build that fails returns nothing for it. A purchase that disappoints still owns customers and revenue.

### What multiple should I expect to pay for a small SaaS business?

Under {{BRAND}}'s default assumptions, 2× to 5× ARR before adjustments for growth, churn, margin, concentration, age and owner workload. Check a specific business with the [SaaS Valuation Tool](/tools/saas-valuation/).

### Can I buy a SaaS business with no technical skills?

You can, but you need a technical person you trust — for the code review before the purchase and for maintenance afterwards. Budget for them as a running cost, not a one-off.

### Is a $5,000 MRR SaaS business worth $210,000?

Not necessarily. That figure is the midpoint of a published default range applied to an invented product. Churn, growth, margin, concentration and owner workload move the range. Enter real figures into the [SaaS Valuation Tool](/tools/saas-valuation/).

### How long does buying take?

Weeks to months, depending on the size of the deal and how organised the seller's records are. Most of that time is due diligence and transfer, not negotiation.
