## Key takeaway

App valuation is the process of estimating what a mobile app business is worth from the profit it earns and the risks attached to that profit. The usual method is **value = annual net profit × multiple**. Downloads and active users explain where the profit comes from, but a buyer pays for earnings that are likely to continue after the app changes hands.

## Why apps are valued on profit

App stores take a commission on sales, and many apps spend on advertising to get installed. Revenue therefore says little about what the owner keeps. Small apps are normally priced on annual net profit before the owner's pay, also called [SDE](/glossary/sde/), measured after store commissions, ad spend, servers and contractors.

Use the amounts the stores actually paid out, not gross sales shown in the store dashboards.

## The factors that move the multiple

### Revenue model

Subscriptions renew, so next month's revenue is partly known in advance. In-app purchases depend on users continuing to buy. Advertising depends on ad rates and on user volume, neither of which the owner controls. Paid downloads depend on a constant flow of new buyers.

### Platforms

An app on both iOS and Android reaches more users and depends less on one store's rules.

### Channel concentration

Many apps get most of their installs from one place: store search, one ad network or one social platform. A ranking change or a rise in ad prices can then cut installs quickly. Buyers treat this the same way they treat a website that relies on one traffic source.

### Retention and churn

For subscription apps, [churn](/glossary/churn/) shows how long subscribers stay. For every app, retention (the share of new users still active after 1, 7 and 30 days) shows whether people find it useful. {{BRAND}}'s app tool does not model retention directly, but buyers will ask for it.

### Technical condition and owner workload

Both stores change their operating systems and policies every year, and an app that is not kept up to date can break or be removed. A buyer will check the code, third-party libraries and how much weekly work the app needs.

## How the valuation is calculated

1. **Annual profit** = average monthly net profit × 12.
2. **Base multiple**: {{BRAND}}'s default assumption for mobile apps is 2.0× to 3.5× annual profit (midpoint 2.7×). It is a published editorial assumption, not measured market data; see [data sources](/data-sources/).
3. **Adjustments** published in the [methodology](/methodology/#adjustments):

| Factor | {{BRAND}}'s default adjustment |
|---|---|
| Subscriptions | +5% |
| In-app purchases | Baseline (0%) |
| Advertising | −5% |
| Paid download | −5% |
| iOS and Android | +3% |
| One platform only | Baseline (0%) |
| Over 60% to 80% of installs or sales from one channel | −6% |
| Over 80% from one channel | −12% |

Revenue trend, age and owner hours are adjusted in the same way as for every other business type. The adjustments are multiplied together, and the combined effect is capped at −50% and +40%.

4. **Range** = annual profit × the adjusted low, mid and high multiples.

## Worked example

A hypothetical subscription app:

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Monthly revenue | $9,000 |
| Monthly net profit | $5,500 (annual profit $66,000) |
| Platforms | iOS and Android |
| Revenue model | Subscriptions |
| Growth, last 12 months | 5% |
| Largest channel | 65% of installs |
| Monthly active users | 40,000 |
| Age | 3 years |
| Owner time | 12 hours a week |

Subscription revenue adds 5% to the multiple and being on both stores adds 3%. Getting 65% of installs from one channel takes off 6%. Growth of 5% counts as roughly flat, and age and owner time sit in their baseline bands. Multiplied together (1.05 × 1.03 × 0.94), the combined adjustment is about +1.7%, giving an adjusted multiple of roughly 2.0× to 3.6× annual profit: around $134,000 to $235,000, with a midpoint near $181,000. These numbers are illustrative, not market data.

## Common mistakes

- **Valuing downloads.** Total downloads include people who deleted the app years ago. Active users and paying users matter more.
- **Using gross store sales as revenue.** Store commissions and refunds come out before you are paid.
- **Leaving out ad spend.** If installs are bought, the cost of buying them belongs in the profit figure. Compare [CAC](/glossary/cac/) with what a user is worth.
- **Ignoring maintenance.** An app that has not been updated for a long time carries a repair bill the buyer will deduct.
- **Assuming the app can simply be handed over.** Store transfers have conditions, and third-party accounts, keys and licences have to move too.

## What buyers will ask for

- Store payout reports and, for subscription apps, active subscriptions and cancellations by month.
- Analytics showing active users and retention.
- Ad network statements, for ad-funded apps and for paid acquisition.
- Access to the source code repository for a technical review.
- A list of third-party services, licences and accounts the app depends on.

The [due diligence guide](/guides/digital-business-due-diligence/) covers the wider checklist.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is an app valued on downloads or on profit?

On profit. Downloads and active users help explain the profit and show how the app is trending, but {{BRAND}} and most buyers of small apps start from annual net profit. The default assumption is 2.0× to 3.5×.

### Are subscription apps worth more than ad-funded apps?

For the same profit, usually yes, because subscription revenue is more predictable. {{BRAND}}'s default adds 5% to the multiple for subscriptions and takes off 5% for advertising.

### Can I value an app that isn't profitable yet?

Not with an earnings method. An app with users but no profit is usually priced on its code, audience or strategic fit for a specific buyer, which the tool does not estimate.
