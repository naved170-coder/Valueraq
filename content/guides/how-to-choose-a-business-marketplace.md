## Key takeaway

There is no single best place to sell or buy an online business. The right marketplace depends on the size of the deal, the type of business and how much of the process you want to run yourself. A small site with a few thousand dollars in yearly profit and a six-figure ecommerce store need different things from a marketplace.

Judge any marketplace on six points: **buyers, cost, vetting, deal support, payment protection and fit**. The sections below explain what to ask under each, how to compare costs with real arithmetic, and a short checklist you can use on any site, including ours.

## Start with your deal size and how hands-on you are

Before comparing sites, answer two questions. They narrow the field faster than any ranking.

| Your situation | What usually fits | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Small business, you are happy to do the work | Open marketplace with low or no fees | Fees stay low and you keep control of price and pace |
| Mid-sized business, limited time | Marketplace with deal support, or a broker | Someone else handles buyer screening and paperwork |
| Larger or complex business | Broker or a curated, vetted marketplace | A smaller, qualified buyer pool and managed negotiation |
| A buyer already in mind | Direct sale, with a lawyer and escrow | No intermediary needed |

Brokers, marketplaces and direct sales are compared in [broker vs marketplace vs selling directly](/guides/ways-to-sell-a-digital-business/). This guide assumes you have decided a marketplace is part of your plan and want to choose one.

## The six checks

### 1. Who the buyers are

A listing is only as good as the people who see it. Ask the marketplace, or look for yourself:

- Do buyers have to register or show funds before they can contact you?
- Are the buyers mostly first-time hobby buyers, or experienced acquirers?
- Does the marketplace carry the type of business you have (content site, ecommerce store, app, SaaS, newsletter)? A site full of stores is a poor home for a newsletter.
- Can you see how many people viewed, saved or messaged about a listing?

Sellers should be wary of any claim about "thousands of qualified buyers" that is not backed by numbers they can check. Buyers should ask the same question the other way round: how many listings are real and current?

### 2. What it costs you

Marketplaces charge in different ways. The common models are:

- **A success fee or commission**, taken from the sale price when the deal closes.
- **A listing fee**, paid up front whether or not the business sells.
- **A subscription**, often for buyers who want extra access or sellers who want extra promotion.
- **Nothing for a basic listing**, with paid extras.

Read the current fee page of every site you consider. Fees change, and a figure you saw in an old article may be out of date. Also count the costs that sit outside the marketplace: an escrow service, lawyers, accountants, bank transfer charges and any tax due on the sale. 

### 3. How listings are vetted

Vetting protects buyers from fake listings and sellers from time-wasting. Look for what the marketplace actually checks:

- Is each listing reviewed by a person before it goes live?
- Can a seller prove ownership of the domain, store or app?
- Are revenue and traffic claims verified, or just stated by the seller?

Heavy vetting usually means fewer listings and sometimes a minimum size. Light vetting means more choice and more risk, which pushes work onto the buyer. Buyers can reduce that risk with the checks in [how to spot a fake website listing](/guides/how-to-spot-a-fake-website-listing/) and [how to verify website revenue](/guides/how-to-verify-website-revenue/).

### 4. What help you get with the deal

A marketplace that only shows listings leaves negotiation, paperwork and transfer to you. A marketplace with deal support may offer messaging inside the site, document sharing, templates or a person who can help. Ask which of these exist and which are extra:

- Messaging that keeps a record of what was agreed.
- A way to share financial documents safely (see the [data room checklist](/guides/data-room-checklist/)).
- Help with the sale agreement and the asset transfer.

The U.S. Small Business Administration advises anyone buying an existing business to carry out a "thorough, objective investigation" and to "consider hiring an attorney and an accountant." That applies on every marketplace. Good deal support makes the work easier, but it does not replace professional advice. ([SBA](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/plan-your-business/buy-existing-business-or-franchise))

### 5. How the payment is protected

For online businesses, the biggest risk is paying and not receiving the assets, or handing over the assets and not being paid. Ask whether the marketplace works with an escrow service, who holds the money, and when it is released. Our guide on [how escrow works for website sales](/guides/how-escrow-works-for-website-sales/) explains the steps. A marketplace that tells you to pay a seller directly, before any protection is in place, is a warning sign.

### 6. How well it fits your business

Last, check the practical fit:

- Does the marketplace accept your business size and type?
- How long do listings usually stay up, and how many sell?
- Can you list in several places at once, or does it require exclusivity?
- Does it work for sellers and buyers in your country? Tax rules and payment options differ between the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada and Australia.

## A worked example: what you keep from a $100,000 sale

Fees look small until you turn them into dollars. The figures below are **illustrative fee models, not the prices of any real marketplace**. They show how the structure of the fee changes what you keep from a $100,000 sale.

| Fee model (illustrative) | Calculation | Fee | You keep |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10% commission | $100,000 × 0.10 | $10,000 | $90,000 |
| 5% success fee | $100,000 × 0.05 | $5,000 | $95,000 |
| $300 flat listing fee | fixed | $300 | $99,700 |
| No fee | none | $0 | $100,000 |

![Money you keep from a $100,000 sale (illustrative fee models): 10% commission 90000 dollars, 5% success fee 95000 dollars, $300 flat fee 99700 dollars, No fee 100000 dollars](/static/img/articles/how-to-choose-a-business-marketplace-net-proceeds.svg){: width="720" height="272" loading="lazy" }

Three lessons follow from the table:

1. **A percentage fee grows with the price.** At $10,000 a 10% commission is $1,000. At $100,000 it is $10,000. The difference matters most on larger sales.
2. **A flat fee is cheap on a large sale but can be hard to justify on a very small one.** A $300 fee on a $2,000 sale is 15% of the price.
3. **The cheapest option is not always the best.** If a higher fee brings a stronger buyer pool or someone who handles the paperwork, it may leave you with a higher price or a sale that actually closes. Compare what you get, not only what you pay.

Subtract the outside costs too. If an escrow service, a lawyer and bank charges together cost you $1,500 on that sale, then with no marketplace fee you keep $98,500, and with a 10% commission you keep $88,500.

## Worked example for buyers: the cost of a wrong choice

Buyers pay in a different way: with their time and their risk. Suppose you plan to buy a site that earns $20,000 a year in profit and the seller asks for a multiple of 3 on that profit: $20,000 × 3 = $60,000. VALUERAQ's default range for a website is 2.5 to 4.0 times yearly profit (see the [methodology](/methodology/)), so $50,000 to $80,000 would be the range to test against.

If a marketplace verifies nothing and the seller overstates profit by 25%, true profit is $16,000, and the same multiple of 3 gives $48,000. Paying $60,000 would mean paying $12,000 too much. A marketplace that verifies ownership and figures, or lets you verify them before paying, directly protects that $12,000.

## Questions to ask any marketplace

Copy these into an email or a chat before you list or make an offer:

- What does it cost me at listing, at closing and per month? Is any of it refundable?
- Who reviews listings, and what proof of ownership and revenue is required?
- Which payment protection do you support, and who pays for it?
- Can I speak to a person if something goes wrong in a deal?
- Do I have to list exclusively, and for how long?
- What happens to my data and the documents I upload?

A marketplace that answers plainly in writing is easier to trust than one that avoids the question.

## Frequently asked questions

### Where is the best place to sell an online business?

It depends on the size and type of the business and on how much help you want. Smaller businesses usually suit an open marketplace where you handle the sale yourself. Larger ones often justify a broker or a vetted marketplace. Use the six checks above and compare at least two options before committing.

### Is it safe to buy a business from a marketplace?

It can be, if you do the checks. Verify ownership and figures yourself, use an escrow service, and have a professional review the sale agreement. No marketplace removes the need for due diligence; see [digital business due diligence](/guides/digital-business-due-diligence/).

### Should I list on more than one marketplace?

Often yes, unless one demands exclusivity. More places can mean more buyers, but each extra listing needs its figures kept consistent. Take a listing down everywhere once you sign with a buyer.

### Are marketplace fees negotiable?

Sometimes, especially for larger deals or when you list on one site only. Ask before you list, and get the answer in writing.

### How do I know a marketplace's numbers about buyers and sales are real?

Ask how they are measured and over what period. Be cautious about averages without a source. The [BizBuySell website benchmarks](https://www.bizbuysell.com/learning-center/valuation-benchmarks/websites-ecommerce/) are a published example of how sold-listing data can be presented, though they cover only that site's listings.
