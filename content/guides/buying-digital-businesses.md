## Key takeaway

Buying an online business trades build time for purchase price. You get existing revenue, customers and systems immediately, but you also take on the risk that earnings fall after the sale. A careful process, focused on verifying figures and understanding why the business earns what it does, reduces that risk.

## Step 1: Define what you're looking for

Before you browse listings, decide:

- **Budget**, including a reserve for working capital, improvements and the unexpected.
- **Business model** you understand: content, SaaS, apps, newsletters or ecommerce each need different skills.
- **Time you can commit** each week.
- **Risk you'll accept**, for example dependence on search traffic, one marketplace or one model provider.
- **Your plan to grow it.** Know what you'd do differently from the current owner.

## Step 2: Shortlist and sense-check prices

Compare listings on the same basis. For each one, calculate the [implied multiples](/calculators/business-multiple-calculator/): asking price ÷ annual profit, and ÷ annual revenue. {{BRAND}} shows these on every listing page. Then run the figures through the [valuation tool](/tools/) for the business type to see which risk factors apply.

A multiple far above the usual range needs a clear reason, such as fast verified growth. A very low multiple can mean a bargain, or a problem the listing doesn't mention.

## Step 3: Make initial contact

Ask the seller focused questions before requesting documents:

1. Why are you selling?
2. How many hours a week do you spend, and on what?
3. Where does revenue come from, by source, over the last 24 months?
4. What has changed recently in traffic, customers or costs?
5. What's included in the sale, and what isn't?

Many sellers will ask you to sign a non-disclosure agreement before sharing details. That's normal.

## Step 4: Due diligence

Due diligence means checking that the business is what the seller says it is. Verify revenue against payment-processor and bank records, traffic against analytics access, and ownership of every asset. The full checklist is in the [due diligence guide](/guides/digital-business-due-diligence/). For larger deals, bring in an accountant and a lawyer, and a developer for SaaS or apps.

## Step 5: Negotiate price and terms

Price is one part of the deal. Terms can matter as much:

- **Asset sale or share sale.** Most small online deals buy the assets, not the company. The choice affects liabilities and tax. Take professional advice.
- **Payment structure.** Full payment at closing, instalments, seller financing or an **earn-out** (part of the price paid later if the business hits agreed targets). Earn-outs shift some risk back to the seller.
- **Transition support.** How long the seller helps after closing, and how.
- **Non-compete.** An agreement that the seller won't start a competing business for a period.
- **Inventory**, for ecommerce, usually paid at cost after a stock count.

## Step 6: Close through escrow

Use a reputable escrow service: the buyer's money is held until the assets have transferred, then released to the seller. Get a written purchase agreement listing every asset, account, liability and condition. {{BRAND}} doesn't hold funds or act as escrow.

## Step 7: Take over carefully

Plan the handover asset by asset: domain, hosting, code repositories, payment accounts, marketplace and app-store accounts, email lists, social accounts, supplier and contractor agreements. Change passwords and access keys once the transfer is complete. Avoid big changes in the first months, so you can tell whether any change in results comes from the handover or from your changes.

## Financing a purchase

Many small online acquisitions are paid in cash. Some buyers use seller financing, and in the US some use loans backed by the Small Business Administration; each lender sets its own criteria, and not every online business qualifies. Discuss options with a lender early.

## Common mistakes buyers make

- Relying on screenshots instead of direct access to accounts.
- Ignoring a recent downward trend because the 12-month average looks good.
- Underestimating the time the business really needs.
- Skipping a written agreement on smaller deals.
- Paying outside escrow.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much does it cost to buy an online business?

Prices range from a few thousand dollars for small sites to millions for established SaaS. Price follows earnings and risk; see [how digital businesses are valued](/guides/business-valuation/).

### Is it safer to buy than to build?

Buying gives you proven revenue but no guarantee it continues. Building costs less upfront but takes time, with no guarantee of success. The right choice depends on your capital, skills and time.

### How long does buying an online business take?

Small deals can close in a few weeks; larger ones with thorough due diligence often take a few months.

### What should I do if the seller won't give analytics or payment access?

Treat it as a warning sign. Read-only access is a reasonable request, and screenshots can be edited.
