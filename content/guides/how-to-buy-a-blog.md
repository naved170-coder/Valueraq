## Key takeaway

A blog earns money from readers who arrive through search, social media or email. When you buy one, you are buying that flow of readers, the content that attracts it and the accounts that turn it into income. The price you can justify depends on the profit left after paying for the work, and on how likely the traffic is to keep coming.

Most bad purchases share the same causes: the buyer trusted screenshots, ignored the unpaid hours the owner puts in, or did not notice that one page brought in half the visitors. This guide shows how to check each of those before you pay.

In the worked example below, a blog is listed at $100,000. Once the owner's unpaid editing is counted, it earns $31,200 a year, so the ask is about 3.2 times profit. VALUERAQ's default range for a website is 2.5 to 4.0 times profit, so the price sits inside the range. Whether it is a good deal depends on the checks, not on the multiple alone.

For the general process that applies to any site, see [how to buy a website](/guides/how-to-buy-a-website/). This guide covers what is specific to blogs.

## What you are actually buying

Write down what is on offer before you talk price. A blog sale can include:

- The domain name and the website files or hosting account.
- The content: posts, images, downloads and any custom design.
- The email list, if there is one, and the tool it sits in.
- Affiliate and advertising accounts that pay the blog's income.
- Social media accounts and the brand name.
- Any writers, editors or tools the owner uses.

Anything not listed in writing is not included. See [what is included when you buy a website](/guides/what-is-included-when-you-buy-a-website/) for a fuller list.

## Step 1: Decide what kind of blog you want

Blogs earn in different ways, and the way changes how steady the profit is.

| Income source | How it works | What to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Affiliate links | Commission when readers buy through your links | Which programs pay, and how much comes from the top three |
| Display ads | A network pays per visitor or per view | Which network, and what happens if it ends the account |
| Sponsored posts | Companies pay to be featured | How many repeat sponsors, and under what terms |
| Own products | Courses, templates or services | How many buyers each month, and refund rate |
| Email list | Income from messages sent to subscribers | List size, open rate and how people joined |

A blog with several income sources is usually safer than one that depends on a single program. Also decide how much time you will give it.

## Step 3: Work out what you would pay

Small blogs are normally priced on annual net profit before the owner's pay, known as [SDE](/glossary/sde/). The important word is real: profit after the cost of everything the blog needs to keep running.

Start from the seller's figures, then correct them:

1. Take the 12-month average of profit shown in the bank and payout records.
2. Subtract any work the owner does for free that you would have to pay for, such as editing, publishing or answering emails.
3. Subtract costs the seller left out, such as tools paid from a personal card.
4. Multiply by 12 for the yearly figure.

### Worked example: is $100,000 a fair ask?

A seller lists a blog for **$100,000**. Over the last 12 months it brought in an average of $4,500 a month, with $1,500 of costs for writers, hosting and tools. The seller reports **$3,000 a month in profit**.

The seller edits and publishes every post, about 10 hours a week, and pays nothing for it. You would hire an editor for $400 a month.

**Seller's annual profit = $3,000 × 12 = $36,000** (asking multiple: $100,000 ÷ $36,000 = 2.8 times)

**Your monthly profit = $3,000 − $400 = $2,600**

**Your annual profit = $2,600 × 12 = $31,200**

**Asking multiple on your profit = $100,000 ÷ $31,200 = 3.2 times**

VALUERAQ's default range for a website is 2.5 to 4.0 times profit, midpoint 3.2:

- Low: $31,200 × 2.5 = **$78,000**
- Midpoint: $31,200 × 3.2 = **$99,840**
- High: $31,200 × 4.0 = **$124,800**

![Asking price vs default value range, $000: Ask 100k, Low 78k, Midpoint 99.8k, High 124.8k](/static/img/articles/how-to-buy-a-blog-ask-vs-range.svg){: width="720" height="272" loading="lazy" }

The ask sits at the midpoint, so it is neither cheap nor high. Now test the risk. Suppose the blog gets 40% of its visitors from one article and a search update pushes it down, so traffic and revenue both fall 30%:

**Revenue after the drop = $4,500 × 0.70 = $3,150**

**Costs stay at $1,900** ($1,500 plus your $400 editor)

**Monthly profit = $3,150 − $1,900 = $1,250, or $15,000 a year**

**Payback = $100,000 ÷ $15,000 = about 6.7 years**

![Years to earn back $100,000 at three profit levels: As reported 2.8 yrs, After editor cost 3.2 yrs, After 30% traffic drop 6.7 yrs](/static/img/articles/how-to-buy-a-blog-payback.svg){: width="720" height="226" loading="lazy" }

The figures here are illustrative, not market data. They show why costs and traffic risk matter: a 30% fall in traffic more than doubles the time it takes to earn your money back, because costs do not fall with it.

Treat the result as a starting point for negotiation. It is not an appraisal. VALUERAQ's multiples are [editorial assumptions](/methodology/), not a record of sales.

## Step 4: The 12 checks before you pay

Ask for evidence on every line. If the seller cannot provide it, lower your offer or walk away.

| # | Check | What to ask for | Red flag |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Income | Payout statements from each program and network, for 12 months | Screenshots only |
| 2 | Bank match | Deposits that match the payouts | Totals that do not reconcile |
| 3 | Traffic | Read-only access to the analytics account | Reports sent as images |
| 4 | Search data | Read-only access to the site's search performance report | The seller will not share it |
| 5 | Top pages | The 10 pages with the most visits and their share | One page above 30% to 40% |
| 6 | Traffic sources | Search, social, email, direct and referral shares | One source above 70% |
| 7 | Income by page | Which pages earn the commission or ad revenue | Income depends on a few posts |
| 8 | Content ownership | Who wrote it and a written transfer of rights | Freelance writers with no agreement |
| 9 | Content quality | Is it accurate, current and original? | Copied or auto-generated pages |
| 10 | Costs | Writers, hosting, tools, subscriptions | Costs missing or "paid by the owner" |
| 11 | Owner workload | Hours per week and who does what | Owner is the only person who can publish |
| 12 | Penalties and warnings | Any manual action notice or policy warning | Warnings ignored or hidden |

The U.S. Small Business Administration advises buyers to carry out a "thorough, objective investigation" of any business and to look at financial statements, tax returns, contracts and cash flow. For a blog, payout statements and analytics take the place of much of that. Our [due diligence checklist](/guides/digital-business-due-diligence/) covers the same ground in more detail.

### Verify traffic yourself

Ask the seller to add you as a read-only user on the analytics account and on the search performance report, and look at them on a call. Check three things: the trend over 12 to 16 months, how much traffic arrives through search compared with other sources, and whether any sharp drops line up with a Google update. [How to verify website traffic](/guides/how-to-verify-website-traffic/) walks through each step.

### Verify income yourself

Income should match three records: the program or network dashboard, the payout statement and the bank deposit. If the three do not agree, ask why before you go further. [How to verify website revenue](/guides/how-to-verify-website-revenue/) explains the process.

## Step 5: Judge the risk of a Google update

Many blogs depend on search. A search update can lift or lower rankings with little warning, so a blog that has grown for a year can lose a third of its visits in a month. You cannot remove this risk, but you can measure it.

- **Look at past updates.** Compare traffic in the weeks around known updates. A blog that kept its traffic through several is steadier than one that dipped each time.
- **Check concentration.** Divide traffic from the top page by total traffic. The higher the share, the more one ranking decides your income.
- **Check content depth.** Pages written for people, with real experience and sources, tend to hold up better than thin pages written only to rank. This is a judgment, not a rule.
- **Ask about recovery.** If traffic fell, what did the owner change, and did traffic come back? A seller who can explain a drop with evidence is more trustworthy than one who cannot.

A falling blog is not worthless, but it should be priced lower. See [how to value a website with falling traffic](/guides/how-to-value-a-website-with-falling-traffic/) and [platform risk](/glossary/platform-risk/).

## Step 6: What transfers and what does not

Be specific about each account before you sign.

| Item | Usually transfers | Check |
|---|---|---|
| Domain and files | Yes, by registrar and hosting transfer | Owner can prove they control the domain |
| Content and images | Yes | Written transfer of rights from each writer |
| Email list | Often | The list's rules and the privacy promises made to subscribers |
| Affiliate accounts | Varies | Ask each program in writing whether the account can move |
| Ad network account | Varies | Ask the network whether you can take over or must apply |
| Social accounts | Often | Login and recovery details move safely |

Affiliate and advertising programs set their own rules, and some do not allow an account to change hands. If the income sits in an account that cannot move, ask the program what you must do, and agree who handles it with the seller before you pay. Our [guide to transferring a website](/guides/how-to-transfer-a-website-to-a-new-owner/) explains the technical side.

## Step 7: Agree terms in writing

Once the checks are done, the usual order is a [letter of intent](/glossary/letter-of-intent/), a period of due diligence, then a purchase agreement. Settle these points:

- **Price and payment.** All at closing, or part later through [seller financing](/glossary/seller-financing/) or an [earnout](/glossary/earnout/).
- **What is included.** Use the list from the start of this guide.
- **Transition period.** Ask the seller to stay on for a few weeks to answer questions. See [transition period](/glossary/transition-period/).
- **Seller promises.** A clause that the figures given are true and the seller owns the content.
- **Non-compete.** Stop the seller from launching a similar blog next month.
- **Holdback.** Keep part of the price back until agreed conditions are met. See [holdback](/glossary/holdback/).

The SBA also says to "consider hiring an attorney and an accountant." For negotiating tactics, read [how to negotiate buying an online business](/guides/how-to-negotiate-buying-an-online-business/).

## Step 8: Pay through escrow

Pay through an independent [escrow](/glossary/escrow/) service rather than sending money straight to the seller. Release the money once the domain, files and accounts are in your name and you have confirmed the blog loads and publishes. [How escrow works for website sales](/guides/how-escrow-works-for-website-sales/) explains the steps.

For more warning signs, see [red flags when buying a website](/guides/red-flags-when-buying-a-website/).

## Your first 90 days

The first three months decide whether the traffic stays. A simple plan:

**Days 1 to 7: secure everything.** Change every password and key the seller knew. Turn on two-step sign-in. Make a full backup. Confirm that analytics, search reports and income dashboards now report to you.

**Days 8 to 30: change little.** Publish on the same schedule the seller used. Do not redesign, change the address structure or remove posts. Spend the time learning which pages bring readers and income, and ask the seller your questions while they are still on hand.

**Days 31 to 60: fix the obvious.** Update out-of-date posts on your top pages, repair broken links, and answer reader comments. If the owner's unpaid work needs a person, hire them now.

**Days 61 to 90: add, not replace.** Write new posts on topics the blog already ranks for, and try one new income source on pages that already get steady traffic. Compare your results against the profit you used to set the price.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much does it cost to buy a blog?

It depends on profit, age, traffic and risk. VALUERAQ's default range for a website is 2.5 to 4.0 times annual net profit, midpoint 3.2. That is an editorial assumption, not a record of sales. Use the free [Website Valuation Tool](/tools/website-valuation/) for a range based on a specific blog.

### Is it worth buying an existing blog instead of starting one?

An existing blog comes with traffic, content and income history, so you skip the years of building. You pay for that and take on the risk that the traffic falls. If you have more time than money, starting new may suit you better.

### Can I buy a blog that makes no money?

Yes, but a profit multiple cannot price it. You would be paying for the traffic, content or domain, and offers are usually low and uncertain. The tool does not estimate value for sites with zero or negative profit.

### Is it safe to buy a blog from a stranger online?

It can be, with care. Check the income and traffic yourself, use escrow, and never accept a login instead of a proper transfer. Our [buyer safety](/buyer-safety/) page explains common problems.
