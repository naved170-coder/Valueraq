## Key takeaway

This is not a question about which asset class is better. It is a question about which failure you are equipped to handle. A website can lose most of its traffic in a week and there is often nothing you can do about it. A SaaS business rarely collapses overnight, but it demands continuous technical work, and neglect shows up months later as churn you cannot reverse.

Pick the one whose bad day you can survive and whose weekly work you will actually do.

## Comparison at a glance

| | Content website | SaaS product |
|---|---|---|
| Typical price per dollar of annual profit | Lower — {{BRAND}}'s default is 2.5× to 4× profit | Higher — 2× to 5× ARR, often well above 4× profit |
| Skills you need | SEO, content, affiliate and ad relationships | Product, support, and a developer you trust |
| Weekly hours | Content planning and publishing | Support, bug fixes, releases |
| How income behaves | Varies with traffic and ad rates | Repeats monthly until a customer cancels |
| Biggest risk | A search algorithm update | Churn and competition |
| How the risk arrives | Suddenly, often without warning | Gradually, usually with warning |
| Capital at risk if it fails | Lower entry price | Higher entry price |
| Resale | Buyers are plentiful at small sizes | Fewer buyers, but higher multiples |

Default ranges are published platform assumptions, not measured market statistics. See the [methodology](/methodology/) and [website vs SaaS valuation](/guides/website-vs-saas-valuation/) for how the two are priced.

## The case for a website

**Lower entry price for the same profit.** A website earning $3,000 a month in profit costs meaningfully less than a SaaS business earning the same, because buyers discount traffic-dependent revenue.

**You can learn the work.** SEO, content commissioning and affiliate management are skills a motivated non-technical owner can pick up. A SaaS codebase is not.

**Failure is usually partial.** Traffic falls, revenue falls, and you still own a site that earns something. It is rarely zero.

The real exposure is concentration: a site that gets most of its visitors from one search engine and most of its revenue from one affiliate programme has two single points of failure, and both belong to someone else.

## The case for SaaS

**The revenue repeats.** A customer who paid last month will probably pay this month. That predictability is what buyers pay the higher multiple for, and it is what makes planning possible.

**Problems announce themselves.** Churn rises before revenue falls. You get warning, which a website rarely does.

**Price increases are available.** A SaaS business with genuinely useful software has pricing power. A content website's income is largely set by advertisers and affiliate programmes.

The exposure is that it needs continuous work. Dependencies age, security issues appear, customers expect support. Skip a quarter and you will not notice for two more — then churn climbs and the cause is already behind you.

## Which risk can you actually manage?

| If this happens | On a website | On a SaaS product |
|---|---|---|
| Traffic halves overnight | Revenue halves. Little short-term recourse | Mostly unaffected — existing customers stay |
| Your developer disappears | Minor inconvenience | Serious. Releases and fixes stop |
| A big competitor launches | Slow erosion in search results | Direct pressure on churn and pricing |
| You ignore it for three months | Rankings drift, revenue drifts | Churn rises, often permanently |

The question answers itself differently for different people. A non-technical buyer who can write and commission content is better placed with a website even though the asset is riskier. A buyer with technical capacity is better placed with SaaS even though it costs more.

## What to check before buying either

Both need the standard work set out in [due diligence](/guides/digital-business-due-diligence/). Two checks matter disproportionately:

- **For a website:** traffic sources over at least 24 months, from analytics you are given access to — not a screenshot. One search update in that window tells you more than any seller's narrative.
- **For a SaaS product:** monthly churn by revenue, not by customer count, over at least 12 months. Churn by customer count hides the loss of a single large account.

## Frequently asked questions

### Which one makes more money?

Neither reliably. At the same purchase price, a website usually produces more current profit, and a SaaS business usually produces more predictable profit with a better resale multiple.

### Which is safer for a first acquisition?

A website is cheaper to enter, so less capital is exposed, but its revenue is less stable. A small SaaS business is steadier week to week but costs more and needs technical support. Safety here means matching the asset to your own skills, not picking the category.

### Can I own both?

Yes, and the risks are largely uncorrelated — a search update that hits a content site does not touch a subscription base. That only helps if you have time for both, since the weekly work is entirely different.

### Do these multiples apply to AI tools too?

AI businesses are valued on their own basis, with different risks around model dependency and defensibility. See [AI business valuation](/guides/ai-business-valuation/).
