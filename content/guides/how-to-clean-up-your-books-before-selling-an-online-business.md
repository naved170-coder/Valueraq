## Key takeaway

A buyer pays for the profit they can verify, not the profit you say you make. Messy books make buyers discount the number, ask for more proof, or walk away. Clean books let your real profit show up in the price.

Cleaning up your books before a sale means five things: **reconcile** every payout to the bank, **separate** personal and business spending, **record** refunds, chargebacks and fees on their own lines, **build** a clean monthly profit and loss statement for the last 12 months, and **document** each add-back with proof. This guide walks through each step, shows a worked example where cleaner books lift the verified profit from $58,900 to $66,900, and gives a timeline you can follow from 12 months out to listing day.

This guide is about bookkeeping. For the wider job of writing down how the business runs, see [how to document your business before selling](/guides/how-to-document-your-business-before-selling/).

## Why buyers care so much about your books

A buyer of an online business is buying a stream of profit. Almost every offer starts from one number, your annual profit, multiplied by a figure that reflects risk. VALUERAQ's default range for an ecommerce business is 2.0x to 3.5x annual profit, and for a website 2.5x to 4.0x. These are VALUERAQ's own default ranges, not market data; see the [methodology](/methodology/) for how they are set.

Every dollar of profit you can prove is worth the multiple: at 2.7x, an extra $1,000 of verified yearly profit adds $2,700 to the price. A dollar a buyer cannot verify is worth little, because buyers who have to guess, guess low.

The U.S. Small Business Administration tells buyers to carry out a "thorough, objective investigation" of a business before buying, and lists financial statements and tax returns among the documents to evaluate ([SBA, Buy an existing business or franchise](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/plan-your-business/buy-existing-business-or-franchise)). Your books are the first thing that investigation tests. Anything unclear becomes a question in due diligence, and questions cost you time and price. The questions buyers ask are covered in the [financial due diligence checklist](/guides/financial-due-diligence-checklist/).

## What "clean books" actually means

Clean books are not perfect books. They are books where a stranger can follow the money from a sale to the bank account without asking you to explain. In practice that means:

| Clean books have | Messy books have |
|---|---|
| One business bank account and card | Business and personal spending mixed |
| Every payout matched to the bank | Revenue typed in from dashboard screenshots |
| Refunds and chargebacks shown separately | Refunds ignored or netted away |
| Processor and platform fees as a line | Fees hidden inside "revenue" |
| Expenses sorted into clear categories | A large "miscellaneous" line |
| Months that add up to the year | Totals that change depending on the report |
| Proof for every unusual item | Add-backs with no receipts |

## Step 1: Separate personal and business spending

This is the first fix because everything else depends on it. If personal costs run through the business, your profit is understated, and a buyer cannot tell which costs will continue after the sale.

Work through the last 12 months of the business bank account and card statements and tag each line as one of three kinds:

- **Business, ongoing:** costs that continue under a new owner, such as hosting, software, ad spend and contractors.
- **Personal:** costs that belong to you, such as a family phone plan, personal meals or a car that is not used for the business.
- **Business, one-time:** costs that will not repeat, such as a rebrand, a legal fee for a one-off matter or a big equipment purchase.

From now on, use one business account and one business card for the business only. Move personal spending to your own accounts. A clean split going forward matters even if you cannot fully repair the past, because buyers weigh the most recent months most.

## Step 2: Reconcile every payment platform to the bank

Reconciliation means proving that the money your platforms say you earned is the money that reached your bank account. For an online business, the work usually involves three layers:

| Layer | What to match | Common gap |
|---|---|---|
| Sales platform report | Gross sales for the month | Differs from payouts because of refunds and fees |
| Payment processor payouts | Net deposits, usually grouped in batches | Payout dates fall in a different month from the sale |
| Bank statement | The deposits you can see | Includes transfers, loans or personal deposits |

For each month, build a small bridge from gross sales to the bank deposits:

> Gross sales − refunds − chargebacks − processor and platform fees = net deposits (± timing differences)

If your bridge does not close, find the difference before a buyer does. The usual causes are payouts that straddle two months, a refund booked in the wrong month, a fee that was never recorded, or a deposit that was not sales at all (a transfer from your own account, for example).

Keep each month's bridge in a spreadsheet. It answers a buyer's first question before they ask it.

## Step 3: Record refunds, chargebacks and fees on their own lines

Many small online businesses book revenue as whatever landed in the bank. That hides three things buyers care about.

- **Refunds** show how happy customers are with the product.
- **Chargebacks and disputes** show payment risk. See the [chargeback](/glossary/chargeback/) definition for how they work.
- **Processor and platform fees** are a real, recurring cost of running the business.

Showing them separately feels like admitting a weakness, but a buyer who sees a small, steady refund line trusts the rest of the numbers.

Do the same for **ad spend**. Record it by channel (for example search, social and email tools) rather than as one lump. A buyer will want to know what it costs to get a customer, and a channel split lets them see which spend is working.

## Step 4: Choose one accounting method and stick to it

Pick either [cash-basis accounting](/glossary/cash-basis-accounting/) (record money when it moves) or [accrual-basis accounting](/glossary/accrual-basis-accounting/) (record income and costs when earned or owed) and apply it to all 12 months. The important thing is consistency. A business that switches methods halfway through the year produces months that do not compare.

## Step 5: Build a clean 12-month profit and loss statement

Buyers usually look at the last 12 months, often called the trailing twelve months. See [trailing twelve months](/glossary/trailing-twelve-months/) for how that is calculated through a partial year. Build one profit and loss statement with a column for each month and a total, using the same categories every month. Our guide to [reading a profit and loss statement](/guides/how-to-read-a-profit-and-loss-statement/) explains the layout.

Here is a simple template you can copy into a spreadsheet:

| Line | Jan | Feb | … | Dec | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gross sales | | | | | |
| Refunds | | | | | |
| Chargebacks | | | | | |
| **Net sales** | | | | | |
| Cost of goods sold | | | | | |
| Processor and platform fees | | | | | |
| Ad spend (by channel) | | | | | |
| Software and hosting | | | | | |
| Contractors | | | | | |
| Accounting and legal | | | | | |
| Other operating costs | | | | | |
| **Operating profit** | | | | | |
| Owner pay and personal items | | | | | |
| One-time items | | | | | |
| **Profit for valuation (SDE)** | | | | | |

The last three rows are where the recast happens. Keep them below the operating profit line, so a buyer can see both the profit as booked and the profit after your adjustments.

## Step 6: Recast the statements and document every add-back

Recasting financial statements means restating your profit so it shows what the business earns for a new owner. The tool is the [add-back](/glossary/add-backs/): an expense that belongs to you or happens once, added back to profit. [BizBuySell's guide to add-backs](https://www.bizbuysell.com/learning-center/article/add-backs-sde/) puts it this way: "Add-backs are expenses added back to a company's bottom-line profits when determining Seller's Discretionary Earnings (SDE) or EBITDA." It also warns that "some add-backs can unrealistically overvalue the company."

That warning is your guide. For each add-back, ask whether a skeptical buyer would agree. Good add-backs are:

- **Personal spending** that ran through the business, with the statement line as proof.
- **One-time costs** that will not repeat, with the invoice.
- **Owner pay**, when you are using SDE, since the buyer will replace or become the owner. The method is in [how to calculate SDE](/guides/how-to-calculate-sde/).

Weak add-backs are costs that are really part of running the business, such as a contractor you will still need, or a "one-time" cost that appears every year. Keep a table of add-backs with the date, amount, reason and the document that proves it.

## Worked example: a messy store recast

An ecommerce store owner has books that show profit by deposits. The store sold $240,000 in the last 12 months. Here is what the books look like before and after cleaning.

**What the messy books show**

The owner booked revenue as the payouts that reached the bank and typed in expenses from the card statement:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Revenue (deposits) | $222,900 |
| Cost of goods sold | $96,000 |
| Ad spend | $38,000 |
| Software | $6,000 |
| Contractors | $12,000 |
| Personal items on the business card | $4,800 |
| One-time rebrand | $3,200 |
| Accounting and miscellaneous | $4,000 |
| **Total expenses** | **$164,000** |
| **Profit as booked** | **$58,900** |

**Step A: show sales, refunds, chargebacks and fees on their own lines**

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Gross sales | $240,000 |
| Refunds | −$7,200 |
| Chargebacks | −$1,800 |
| **Net sales** | **$231,000** |
| Processor and platform fees | $8,100 |
| Net deposits to the bank ($231,000 − $8,100) | $222,900 |

The deposits match the first table. Profit does not change in this step, but revenue rises from $222,900 to $231,000 and fees now show as a cost. The bridge ties to the bank, which is what a buyer wants to see.

**Step B: recast the profit**

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Profit as booked | $58,900 |
| Add back personal items on the business card | + $4,800 |
| Add back one-time rebrand | + $3,200 |
| **Verified profit** | **$66,900** |

As a check, the clean operating costs are $96,000 + $8,100 + $38,000 + $6,000 + $12,000 + $4,000 = $164,100, and $231,000 − $164,100 = $66,900.

![Annual profit a buyer can verify: Messy books $58,900, Clean books $66,900](/static/img/articles/how-to-clean-up-your-books-before-selling-an-online-business-profit-messy-vs-clean.svg){: width="720" height="180" loading="lazy" }

**What it does to the price**

Using VALUERAQ's default ecommerce range of 2.0x to 3.5x annual profit, with a midpoint of 2.7x:

| Multiple | Messy books ($58,900) | Clean books ($66,900) | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.0x | $117,800 | $133,800 | $16,000 |
| 2.7x | $159,030 | $180,630 | $21,600 |
| 3.5x | $206,150 | $234,150 | $28,000 |

![Price at the 2.7x ecommerce midpoint: Messy books $159,030, Clean books $180,630](/static/img/articles/how-to-clean-up-your-books-before-selling-an-online-business-price-at-midpoint.svg){: width="720" height="180" loading="lazy" }

An $8,000 improvement in verified profit moves the price by $16,000 to $28,000 across that range. This is an illustration with made-up figures, not a prediction of a sale price. A buyer will check each add-back, and if they reject one the number drops. The same cleanup can also cut your profit: if refunds or fees were never booked, the true profit can be lower than you thought. Better to learn that before a buyer does.

## A timeline: 12, 6 and 1 month before listing

You do not need to do everything at once. A plan across the year before you list works better:

| When | What to do |
|---|---|
| 12 months before | Open a business-only bank account and card. Stop paying personal costs from the business. Pick one accounting method. Start a monthly reconciliation. |
| 6 months before | Close each month within a few weeks. Tag expenses as ongoing, personal or one-time. Start your add-back table with receipts. |
| 3 months before | Build the clean 12-month profit and loss statement. Fix gaps in the bridge from sales to bank. Review it with an accountant. |
| 1 month before | Freeze the numbers. Collect bank statements, payout reports and tax returns in one folder. Prepare a short note explaining any unusual month. |
| Listing day | Share only what the buyer is entitled to see, after an NDA. See [what documents buyers ask for](/guides/what-documents-do-buyers-ask-for/) and the [data room checklist](/guides/data-room-checklist/). |

If you cannot start 12 months out, start now. A few clean months beat none, and you can tell buyers when the cleanup began.

## Checklist: before you share your numbers

- Business and personal spending are separated going forward.
- Each month has a bridge from gross sales to bank deposits.
- Refunds, chargebacks and fees are recorded on their own lines.
- One accounting method is used for all 12 months.
- The 12-month profit and loss statement adds up to the yearly total.
- Every add-back has a date, amount, reason and proof.
- Bank statements and payout reports match the profit and loss statement.
- An accountant has reviewed the numbers. The SBA's advice to buyers applies to sellers too: "Consider hiring an attorney and an accountant."
- You can explain every month that looks unusual.

## Frequently asked questions

### How far back should I clean my books?

Start with the last 12 months, because that is the period most buyers look at first. If you can also tidy the previous year, comparing the two years shows the trend. If your records go back further than your cleanup, say so; buyers prefer honesty to gaps they find themselves.

### Do I need an accountant to clean up my books?

You can do the first pass yourself: separating spending and building the sales-to-bank bridge. An accountant is worth paying for the review, especially for the recast and for tax questions, which vary by country and by how your business is set up. This guide is not tax or accounting advice.

### Will clean books raise my price?

They can, because buyers pay a multiple of the profit they can verify, as the worked example shows. They can also lower your profit if costs were missing, so treat cleanup as a way to know your real number. The [free valuation tools](/tools/) let you see how different profit figures change the range.

### What if I cannot prove an add-back?

Leave it out of the headline number, or show it separately as "profit with unproven add-backs" so the buyer can decide. An add-back you cannot support can make the rest of your numbers look suspect.
