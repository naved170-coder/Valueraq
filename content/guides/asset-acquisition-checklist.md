## Key takeaway

In an asset acquisition you buy a list of things, not a company. **That makes the list the most important document in the deal: whatever is not on it, you do not own.** A good checklist makes sure every asset you are paying for is named, proven, transferable and priced, and that nothing you did not want comes with it.

This guide is for buyers of small online businesses such as websites, stores, apps and newsletters, where an [asset sale](/glossary/asset-sale/) is the normal structure. It gives nine steps, a table of what to ask for on each asset, and a worked example with the arithmetic shown. All figures in the example are made up. Multiples come from VALUERAQ's default ranges on the [methodology](/methodology/) page. They are the tool's starting assumptions, not market data and not a promise of a price.

If you want the wider buying process from first look to day 100, read the [acquiring a business checklist](/guides/acquiring-a-business-checklist/). This page goes deeper on one part of it: the assets themselves.

## Asset acquisition vs buying the company

| | Asset acquisition | Buying the company (share sale) |
|---|---|---|
| What you get | The assets named in the contract | The whole company, with everything inside it |
| Past debts and claims | Generally stay with the seller, unless the contract says otherwise | Generally stay in the company you now own |
| Contracts and accounts | Moved or re-signed one by one | Stay in the company's name |
| Your main job | Get the list right and move each item | Investigate everything the company has ever done |
| Typical for | Small websites, stores, apps, newsletters | Larger businesses with staff and many contracts |

This is a general description. What transfers depends on the contract and on local law, so have a lawyer read the agreement. Our [stock sale](/glossary/stock-sale/) page covers the other structure.

## Step 1: Write the asset list first

Before you talk price, ask the seller for a schedule: one line per asset, with an owner's name beside it. For a typical online business the list is longer than people expect.

- **Core assets:** domain names, website files, content, theme or code, brand name and logo.
- **Accounts:** hosting, payment processor, ad networks, email service, analytics, app store or marketplace accounts, social profiles.
- **Audience:** email list, subscriber list, customer records, where the privacy policy and local law allow them to be transferred.
- **Commercial assets:** supplier agreements, affiliate and sponsor relationships, product listings, inventory.
- **Know-how:** written processes, passwords list (shared safely), templates, contractor details.

The U.S. Small Business Administration notes that when valuing a business you should value all property tied to it, and that this can include [intangible assets such as brand presence, intellectual property and customer information](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/manage-your-business/close-or-sell-your-business). The same assets are the ones you should be able to name in the contract.

Checkpoint: a schedule you could hand to a lawyer without explaining what any line means.

## Step 2: Prove the seller owns each asset

A seller can only sell what they own and are allowed to transfer. Match each line to proof.

| Asset | Ask for | Red flag |
|---|---|---|
| Domain name | Registrar account access, the owner name on the record | Domain registered to a freelancer or a past partner |
| Website and code | Hosting login, code repository access | Theme or plugin licenses held in someone else's name |
| Content | Proof the seller or the business wrote it or paid for it | Copied or loosely licensed text and images |
| Brand and logo | Registration papers if any, original design files | Design made by a contractor with no written transfer |
| Payment and ad accounts | Account holder details, a plan for how they move | Account that cannot be transferred under its own terms |
| Email list | How subscribers joined, the privacy policy | Purchased or scraped list |
| Inventory | Stock count, supplier invoices | Count the seller cannot back up |
| Supplier contracts | Signed copies and whether they can be assigned | Contract that ends when ownership changes |

Our guide on [digital business due diligence](/guides/digital-business-due-diligence/) goes through the checks one area at a time, and [what documents buyers ask for](/guides/what-documents-do-buyers-ask-for/) lists the paperwork.

Checkpoint: every line on the schedule has either proof attached or a note saying what is missing.

## Step 3: Say what you are not buying

The other half of the list is the exclusions. In an asset deal these normally stay with the seller: their company, its bank accounts and cash, past tax bills, debts, and claims that arose before the sale. Write them down anyway. A short "excluded" section stops arguments later.

Also decide how to treat gray areas: a loan the seller used to buy stock, customer prepayments for orders not yet shipped, subscriptions already paid for the coming months. These are real obligations to customers. Decide in writing who delivers on them and who bears the cost.

Checkpoint: a written list of excluded items and a rule for each gray area.

## Step 4: Value the assets as a business

The price should rest on what the business earns, not on a sum of parts. For small online businesses the common starting point is a multiple of yearly profit. Read [SDE](/glossary/sde/) and [how to calculate SDE](/guides/how-to-calculate-sde/) for the measure. VALUERAQ's default ranges are 2.5x to 4.0x for a website or newsletter, and 2.0x to 3.5x for an ecommerce business or app. For ecommerce, inventory is valued separately, at cost, and added.

Use asset values as a second opinion. The SBA describes the assets approach as one that "subtracts total business liabilities from the total value of all assets." If the earnings price is far above what the assets could fetch, the gap is [goodwill](/glossary/goodwill/): you are paying for the business to keep earning. See [asset-based valuation](/glossary/asset-based-valuation/) for how the method works.

Checkpoint: a low, middle and high price, and a number above which you walk away.

## Worked example: pricing and splitting an asset deal

The figures are made up. A seller offers an ecommerce store. After adjusting for owner pay and one-off costs, average monthly profit is $3,000, and inventory at cost is $14,000.

**Annual profit:** $3,000 × 12 = **$36,000**

**Default range for an ecommerce business, 2.0x to 3.5x:**

- Low: $36,000 × 2.0 = **$72,000**
- Midpoint: $36,000 × 2.7 = **$97,200**
- High: $36,000 × 3.5 = **$126,000**

![Default value range, $36,000 profit (example): Low 72000, Midpoint 97200, High 126000](/static/img/articles/asset-acquisition-checklist-value-range.svg){: width="720" height="226" loading="lazy" }

Suppose you agree the midpoint plus inventory at cost.

**Total price:** $97,200 + $14,000 = **$111,200**

Now the contract needs to say what each part of the price pays for. A possible split:

| Asset group | Amount | Share of total |
|---|---|---|
| Inventory at cost | $14,000 | 14,000 ÷ 111,200 = 12.6% |
| Domain, store, content and brand | $70,000 | 70,000 ÷ 111,200 = 63.0% |
| Customer and email list | $12,000 | 12,000 ÷ 111,200 = 10.8% |
| Supplier agreements, accounts and handover help | $15,200 | 15,200 ÷ 111,200 = 13.7% |
| **Total** | **$111,200** | **100%** (rounded) |

![Example split of a $111,200 price: Domain and store 70000, Supplier and handover 15200, Inventory 14000, Customer list 12000](/static/img/articles/asset-acquisition-checklist-price-split.svg){: width="720" height="272" loading="lazy" }

The split is an example, not a rule. How a price is divided between assets can change each side's tax, so settle it with an accountant. Our [asset sale](/glossary/asset-sale/) page explains how the schedule works.

One check to run: the inventory line should match a count you or someone you trust made at the date of transfer. If the real count is $11,000 at cost, the price should fall by $3,000, to $108,200.

## Full checklist by asset type

Tick each line before you pay. A line you cannot tick is a question for the seller.

**Core digital assets**
- [ ] Domain names, with registrar account access or a transfer code
- [ ] Website files, theme or code, and the repository
- [ ] Content, images and downloads, with proof the seller can sell them
- [ ] Hosting, store platform or app store account

**Audience and marketing assets**
- [ ] Email list, with how and when people agreed to receive messages (consent rules differ by country, so ask an attorney)
- [ ] Social media accounts and handles
- [ ] Advertising accounts and their history
- [ ] Analytics and Search Console access

**Supplier and operating assets**
- [ ] Supplier accounts and agreed terms
- [ ] Inventory, with a count and location
- [ ] Software and tool licenses that transfer
- [ ] Contracts with writers, developers or support staff

**Brand assets**
- [ ] Business name and logo
- [ ] Trademarks or applications, if any

## What stays with the seller

In an asset sale, these normally remain with the seller unless the contract says otherwise: their company, its bank accounts, debts, past tax bills and claims that began before the sale. Confirm each in writing, and ask the seller to say which customer prepayments or open orders you take on.

This checklist is a planning aid, not legal advice. Have an attorney review the asset purchase agreement.

## Step 5: Build the contract around the list

A lawyer should draft or review the [purchase agreement](/glossary/purchase-agreement/). Check that it contains:

- the schedule of assets from step 1, attached to the contract;
- the exclusions from step 3;
- the seller's [representations and warranties](/glossary/representations-and-warranties/): written statements, for example that they own the assets and that the figures given are accurate;
- a [non-compete agreement](/glossary/non-compete-agreement/) so the seller cannot open a copy next door;
- the price split from the example above;
- the [transition period](/glossary/transition-period/): how long the seller helps, and how.

If part of the price is deferred, name the tool: a [holdback](/glossary/holdback/), [seller financing](/glossary/seller-financing/) or an [earnout](/glossary/earnout/).

## Step 6: Plan the transfer order

Assets do not move together, and some cannot move on the day you sign. Write a dated transfer list, in this order of importance:

1. Domain name and DNS.
2. Hosting and code repository.
3. Payment processor, ad and email accounts, or new accounts opened in your name.
4. App store or marketplace accounts, where the platform has a transfer process.
5. Social profiles and the email list.
6. Supplier contracts and inventory.

Our guide on [how to transfer a website to a new owner](/guides/how-to-transfer-a-website-to-a-new-owner/) covers the technical steps. Some items, such as a payment account that is tied to the seller's identity, are not transferred at all. You open your own and the seller closes theirs.

## Step 7: Pay through a safe route

Tie the payment to the transfer list. In [escrow](/glossary/escrow/), a neutral party holds the money until both sides confirm the assets have moved. Our guide on [how escrow works for website sales](/guides/how-escrow-works-for-website-sales/) lists the steps. A common mistake is paying part of the price before a single asset has moved, in a way you cannot reverse.

## Step 8: Confirm by checking, not by trust

On transfer day, test each item yourself:

- Log in to every account with your own credentials. Change the passwords.
- Confirm the domain record shows your name.
- Place a test order or sign-up to see the store or app work.
- Compare the inventory count with the schedule.
- Download a backup of the site and store it where the seller cannot reach it.

Checkpoint: every line on the transfer list is ticked by you, not by the seller.

## Step 9: The first week

Control comes first. Remove the seller's access that is no longer needed, turn on two-step sign-in, set up backups, and write down where everything lives. Do not redesign anything yet. Track the same monthly numbers the seller gave you so you can see whether the business matches how it was described. If it does not, your written warranties are where you start, and a lawyer is who you call.

## Common mistakes

- **Buying "the business" without a list.** Say what you are buying in lines, not in a general phrase.
- **Skipping exclusions.** An unwritten exclusion is a future argument.
- **Paying for the sum of parts.** The price comes from earnings. Asset values are the cross-check.
- **Letting accounts transfer later.** If an asset is not under your control at closing, hold back money against it.
- **Skipping the professionals.** The SBA suggests buyers [consider hiring an attorney and an accountant](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/plan-your-business/buy-existing-business-or-franchise).

## Frequently asked questions

### What is an asset acquisition?

It is a purchase in which you buy specific assets of a business, such as its domain, website, brand and customer list, rather than the company that owns them. Debts and past problems generally stay with the seller unless the contract says otherwise.

### What should an asset acquisition checklist include?

The asset list with proof of ownership, the exclusions, a price for each asset group, the contract terms, a transfer order, a safe payment method and a plan for the first week.

### Do I need a lawyer for an asset purchase?

For anything beyond a very small deal, yes. A lawyer checks that the contract matches the list, and an accountant advises on how the price is split. The SBA recommends considering both when you buy an existing business.

### Can I take over the seller's payment account?

Often not. Many payment and ad accounts are tied to the seller's identity and cannot be handed over under their own rules, so plan to open your own and have the seller close theirs.

### How do I know the price is fair?

Value the business on its adjusted yearly profit with a defensible range, then use asset values as a second view. The free [website valuation tool](/tools/website-valuation/) gives a default range in about a minute.

### How do I order the transfers?

Domain and DNS first, then hosting and code, then store or app accounts, then ad and email accounts. Release payment only as each is confirmed.
