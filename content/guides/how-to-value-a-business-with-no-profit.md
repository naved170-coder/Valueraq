## Why the usual method fails

Most small businesses are valued as yearly profit × a multiple. A loss multiplied by any number is still a loss, so the method gives no answer. {{BRAND}}'s tools say so instead of showing a figure.

## Four ways to value it instead

| Approach | How it works | Use it when |
|---|---|---|
| Assets minus debts | Add up what the business owns, subtract what it owes | It holds stock, equipment, a strong domain or content |
| Revenue multiple | Yearly revenue × a multiple | Revenue repeats by subscription and is growing |
| Cost to rebuild | What a buyer would spend in time and money to build the same thing | It has an audience, code or rankings that took years |
| Profit after fixes | The profit once costs a buyer wouldn't carry are removed | The loss comes from one-off or owner-specific spending |

The U.S. Small Business Administration describes the assets approach as one that "subtracts total business liabilities from the total value of all assets." The Corporate Finance Institute notes that revenue multiples are used when a company has no positive earnings, while warning that they ignore profitability.

## A worked example

A newsletter has 20,000 subscribers and earns $3,000 a month in sponsorships, but spends $3,400 a month on writers and paid growth.

- **Profit:** −$400 a month. No earnings multiple applies.
- **Profit after fixes:** $1,200 of the spending is paid subscriber growth a buyer could stop. Profit would then be $800 a month, or $9,600 a year.
- **At 2.5 times** that adjusted profit, the low end of {{BRAND}}'s default range for newsletters, the figure is $24,000. Treat it as a ceiling, since it assumes growth spending can stop without losing sponsors.
- **Assets:** the list, the archive and the brand. Their value depends on what a particular buyer would do with them.

## What a buyer will ask

- Why does the business lose money, and what changes under a new owner?
- Which costs are for growth and which are needed just to stand still?
- How long until it breaks even, and on what assumptions?

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a loss-making business sell for a high price?

Yes, when a buyer values the audience, technology or market position more than the current earnings. Those prices reflect that buyer's plans, not a formula.

### Should I wait until it is profitable before selling?

Usually yes, if you can get there. A business with even a small, steady profit can be valued on earnings, which most buyers find easier to judge.
