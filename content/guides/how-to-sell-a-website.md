## Key takeaway

Selling a website is mostly a matter of proof and handover. A buyer of a content, affiliate or ad-funded site wants to see where every dollar and every visit comes from, and wants to know the site will still work the day after it changes hands. This guide covers the steps that are specific to websites. For the general process that applies to any online business, see [how to sell a digital business](/guides/selling-digital-businesses/).

## Step 1: Pull together 12 months of records

Start here, because the records decide the price. For each of the last 12 months, collect:

- **Revenue by source.** Reports from each ad network, affiliate programme, sponsor or product checkout, matched to the deposits in your bank or payment account.
- **Costs.** Writers, editors, hosting, themes and plugins, software subscriptions, link-building, and any contractor you pay.
- **Traffic.** Analytics and search-console data by month and by channel: organic search, direct, email, social and referral.
- **Your time.** A plain list of what you do each week and roughly how long it takes.

Put the revenue and costs into one monthly profit and loss sheet. The bottom line is your net profit before paying yourself, known as [SDE](/glossary/sde/). If a buyer can trace each figure on that sheet to a report they can open themselves, you have done the hardest part.

If you have 24 months of records, keep them ready. A buyer comparing the same months across two years can see seasonality, which protects you from a low offer based on a quiet month.

## Step 2: Clean up the site and the accounts

Fix the things a buyer will find anyway:

- **Ownership.** Make sure the domain is registered in your name or your company's, and that you can show who wrote the content and where images came from. Keep contractor agreements and image licences in one folder.
- **Accounts.** Move the site off shared logins. Analytics, search console, hosting and email tools should sit in accounts that can be handed over without exposing your other projects.
- **The site itself.** Update the software, remove unused plugins, fix broken links and broken affiliate links, and check that the privacy policy and affiliate or advertising disclosures are present and accurate.
- **Documentation.** Write short instructions for the recurring work: how articles are commissioned and published, how links are updated, how the email list is sent.

Avoid big changes just before you list. A redesign or a new monetization method makes recent figures harder to compare with the past year, and buyers discount what they can't compare.

## Step 3: Price it with the tool

Enter your average monthly revenue and profit in the [Website Valuation Tool](/tools/website-valuation/). {{BRAND}}'s default assumption for a website is 2.5× to 4.0× annual net profit, then adjusted for age, revenue trend, how much traffic comes from one channel, monetization type and your weekly hours. The adjustments multiply together and are capped at −50% and +40%. The range is a published platform assumption, not a measured market average; the reasoning is in the [methodology](/methodology/) and the [how-to guide on website valuation](/guides/website-valuation/).

Look at which adjustments pull your multiple down. If 85% of your visits come from one search engine, or the site needs 30 hours of your week, a buyer will raise it. Decide whether to fix it before listing or to price it in.

Set an asking price you can defend from your own sheet. A price inside the range the tool gives is easier to justify than one based on your best month.

## Step 4: Write the listing

A website listing should let a buyer decide in two minutes whether to ask for more. State:

1. What the site covers and who reads it.
2. Its age and how it earns money, with each revenue source's share.
3. Average monthly revenue and net profit for the last 12 months.
4. Monthly visits and the share from each channel.
5. Your weekly hours and what you do.
6. What is included: domain, content, email list, social accounts, contractor relationships.
7. Why you are selling.

## Step 5: Handle buyer questions

Buyers message sellers privately through the listing. Expect questions on:

- What happened in any month where traffic or revenue moved sharply.
- Whether the site has ever had a manual action or a traffic drop after a search-engine update.
- How links to the site were acquired.
- Who writes the content and whether they will keep working for a new owner.
- Whether affiliate rates or ad rates have changed recently.

Answer plainly, including the awkward ones. Before sharing the site's address or account access, ask the buyer about their budget and experience, and consider a non-disclosure agreement. When you do share data, give read-only access to analytics and revenue dashboards. Screenshots can be edited, and experienced buyers know it. The [due diligence checklist](/guides/digital-business-due-diligence/) shows what a careful buyer will ask for.

## Step 6: Agree terms in a letter of intent

Once a buyer is serious, the main terms go into a [letter of intent](/glossary/letter-of-intent/): the price, how it is paid, what is included, how long the buyer has to check your figures, and how long you will help after the sale. It is usually not binding on price, but it stops both sides negotiating from memory.

The final terms go into a purchase agreement. Most small website sales are an [asset sale](/glossary/asset-sale/): the buyer acquires the domain, content and accounts, not your company. The agreement should list every asset by name. The structure affects tax and liability, so take professional advice before you sign.

## Step 7: Close through escrow

Use an independent [escrow](/glossary/escrow/) service. The buyer pays the escrow provider, you transfer the assets, the buyer confirms they have them, and the provider releases the money to you. Agree in advance who pays the escrow fee and how long the buyer's inspection period lasts.

{{BRAND}} is not a broker or an escrow service and does not handle the purchase price. You and the buyer choose the escrow provider. Don't transfer the domain before the money is confirmed as held.

## Step 8: Migrate and hand over

Agree a written order of transfer. A common one:

| Asset | How it usually moves | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Domain | Transfer to the buyer's account at the same registrar, or to their registrar | Registrar transfers can take several days, and some domains are locked for a period after registration or a recent transfer |
| Hosting and files | Full backup restored on the buyer's hosting, or the hosting account handed over | Test the copy before changing where the domain points |
| Content and design | Included in the backup; licences for themes and plugins reassigned | Licences tied to your personal account |
| Analytics and search console | Buyer added as owner, then you are removed | Keep history intact; don't create a new property |
| Email list | Export with consent records, or transfer of the sending account | Data-protection rules on transferring subscriber data |
| Affiliate and ad accounts | Often not transferable; the buyer applies for their own and links are swapped | A gap in earnings while the buyer waits for approval |
| Social accounts | Login and recovery details changed to the buyer's | Each platform's terms on account transfers |

Affiliate and ad accounts cause most delays. Encourage the buyer to apply to each programme early, during the inspection period if possible, and check each programme's terms. After the transfer, stay available for the support period you agreed, and remove your own access once the buyer confirms everything works.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much is my website worth?

It depends on its profit and its risks. {{BRAND}}'s default assumption is 2.5× to 4.0× annual net profit before adjustments. Run your own figures through the [Website Valuation Tool](/tools/website-valuation/) for a range.

### Can I sell a website that makes very little money?

Yes, but expect a small price. A buyer of a site with little profit is paying for the content, the domain and the traffic, and will compare your price with the cost of building something similar.

### Do my affiliate and ad accounts transfer to the buyer?

Often they don't. Many programmes tie the account to the person or company that applied, so the buyer opens their own accounts and replaces the links and ad code. Check each programme's terms before you promise anything in the listing.
