## Key takeaway

An affiliate website is usually valued the same way as other small websites: yearly net profit multiplied by a multiple. What makes affiliate sites different is where the risk sits. The income comes from programs the owner does not control, and the traffic often comes from one search engine. Both can change without warning, so buyers look closely at them.

In {{BRAND}}'s default method, the affiliate label alone takes only 3% off the multiple. A heavy reliance on one traffic source (up to 12% off) and a falling revenue trend (up to 25% off) matter far more. The multiples in this guide are {{BRAND}}'s default assumptions, described in the [methodology](/methodology/). They are not market data, and a real buyer may pay more or less.

## What makes an affiliate website different

An affiliate site earns commission when a visitor clicks a link and buys from another company. The owner does not hold stock, ship products or handle customer service. That is the appeal: low costs and a simple business. See [affiliate marketing](/glossary/affiliate-marketing/) for the basic terms.

The same setup creates three risks that a buyer has to price:

1. **Program risk.** The merchant sets the commission rate, the cookie window and the rules. It can change them or close the program. {{BRAND}}'s default rules note that affiliate programs "can change commission rates or close."
2. **Traffic risk.** Many affiliate sites rely on organic search. If one search engine sends most of the visits, a ranking change can cut revenue quickly.
3. **Concentration inside the income.** One merchant may produce most of the commission. Losing it would hurt more than losing one of several.

None of these makes an affiliate site a bad business. They are the reasons a buyer asks more questions than for, say, a site that sells its own products.

## Step 1: Work out yearly net profit

The base figure is annual net profit, also called [SDE](/glossary/sde/) (seller's discretionary earnings): profit before the owner pays themselves. For a website, {{BRAND}} uses average monthly net profit over the last 12 months, multiplied by 12.

For an affiliate site the main costs are usually:

- Writers or editors
- Hosting and software (SEO tools, email service)
- Paid traffic, if any
- Freelancers for links, design or technical work

Use a full year, not the best few months. A site that earns $9,000 in one strong month and $2,000 in the rest has a very different profit from one that earns $3,500 every month. The [trailing twelve months](/glossary/trailing-twelve-months/) view smooths out seasons and one-off spikes.

Be careful with add-backs. BizBuySell warns that "some add-backs can unrealistically overvalue the company" ([BizBuySell](https://www.bizbuysell.com/learning-center/article/add-backs-sde/)). If you add back a cost, a buyer will ask for the document behind it. See [add-backs](/glossary/add-backs/).

## Step 2: Start from the default multiple range

{{BRAND}}'s default range for a website is 2.5 to 4.0 times yearly profit, with a midpoint of 3.2. This is an editorial assumption, not an average from a transaction dataset. Corporate Finance Institute describes the multiples approach as one that "utilizes different financial metrics from comparable companies to value a target company" ([CFI](https://corporatefinanceinstitute.com/resources/valuation/multiples-analysis)), and also lists oversimplification and comparability as weaknesses. Two sites with the same profit can deserve different multiples.

That is why the range is then adjusted for the facts of each site.

## Step 3: Adjust for the factors that matter

These are the adjustments in {{BRAND}}'s default website rules that affect an affiliate site most. Effects multiply together.

| Factor | Band | Effect on multiple |
|---|---|---|
| Monetization | Affiliate commissions | −3% |
| Monetization | Display ads or lead generation | 0% |
| Monetization | Own products, or mixed income with no source over 60% | +3% |
| Channel concentration | Over 80% of traffic from one source | −12% |
| Channel concentration | Over 60% to 80% | −6% |
| Channel concentration | 60% or less | 0% |
| Revenue trend | Down more than 5% to 20% | −12% |
| Revenue trend | Down more than 20% | −25% |
| Revenue trend | Within ±5% | 0% |
| Revenue trend | Up more than 5% to 25% | +8% |
| Business age | 4 years or more | +5% |
| Owner involvement | Under 10 hours a week | +5% |

The combined adjustment is limited to between ×0.5 and ×1.4. The full list is in the [methodology](/methodology/).

Notice the size of each effect. Going from affiliate to mixed income moves the multiple by about 6 percentage points between the two bands. Moving from 85% of traffic in one channel to 55% moves it by 12. A site whose revenue is falling loses more again.

## A worked example

A content website earns $6,000 a month in revenue and $3,500 a month in profit, so yearly profit is $42,000 ($3,500 × 12). It is four years old, revenue has been flat, the owner works about 15 hours a week, and all income is affiliate commission.

The table changes one thing at a time. Profit is held at $42,000 in every row, which is an assumption, since real changes usually cost money or time.

| Case | What differs | Combined factor | Midpoint multiple | Midpoint value |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A (base) | 70% of visits from one source | 1.05 × 0.94 × 0.97 = 0.957 | 3.06× | $129,000 |
| B | 85% of visits from one source | 1.05 × 0.88 × 0.97 = 0.896 | 2.87× | $120,000 |
| C | 55% of visits from one source | 1.05 × 1.00 × 0.97 = 1.019 | 3.26× | $137,000 |
| D | 55% from one source and mixed income | 1.05 × 1.00 × 1.03 = 1.082 | 3.46× | $145,000 |
| E | As A, but revenue down 10% | 1.05 × 0.88 × 0.94 × 0.97 = 0.842 | 2.70× | $113,000 |

Check case A: the midpoint multiple is 3.2 × 0.957 = 3.06, and $42,000 × 3.06 = $128,700, shown as $129,000. The full range for case A runs from about $101,000 to $161,000, which comes from the low (2.5) and high (4.0) base multiples.

## What buyers ask about an affiliate site

Expect these questions in [due diligence](/guides/how-to-verify-website-revenue/):

- **Which programs pay you, and how much from each?** Have a table by merchant for the last 12 months.
- **Do you have written terms or notices from each program?** A buyer wants to know whether commissions have changed and whether the account can move to a new owner. Read each program's rules on transfers before you list.
- **Where does the traffic come from?** Analytics and search console screenshots help. See [how to verify website traffic](/guides/how-to-verify-website-traffic/).
- **Which pages earn the most?** If three articles produce most of the revenue, say so. A buyer will find out anyway.
- **What happens if a program closes?** A short replacement plan shows you have thought about it.

The U.S. Small Business Administration advises buyers of existing businesses to carry out a "thorough, objective investigation" and to consider hiring an attorney and an accountant ([SBA](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/plan-your-business/buy-existing-business-or-franchise)). Sellers who prepare for that investigation tend to have smoother deals.

## Ways to improve an affiliate site's value

Most of these take months, so start well before you list.

1. **Add programs for the same readers.** If the main merchant is 70% of commission, a second and third reduce the damage from one change.
2. **Widen your traffic.** An email list, a newsletter or a second search source reduces the traffic concentration penalty. See [how to increase website value before selling](/guides/increase-website-value-before-selling/).
3. **Add something you own.** A digital product or a paid tool turns part of the income into revenue you control.
4. **Document your tasks.** Fewer owner hours make a handover easier and add 5% to the multiple under 10 hours a week.
5. **Keep records clean.** Twelve months of statements from each network, and your expenses in one place.

If traffic is already falling, find out why before you list. See [how to value a website with falling traffic](/guides/how-to-value-a-website-with-falling-traffic/).

## Common mistakes when valuing an affiliate site

- **Using the best month.** Use 12 months of profit, not a peak.
- **Ignoring program rules.** If a program's rules limit transfers, say so before a buyer finds out.
- **Counting revenue as profit.** Commission paid to you is revenue. Writers, tools and links come out before profit.
- **Treating a multiple as a promise.** A multiple is a starting point for talks. Deal terms such as earn-outs or seller financing can change what you receive.
- **Comparing with a different kind of site.** A site with its own products and an affiliate site with the same profit are not the same risk.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are affiliate websites worth less than other websites?

In {{BRAND}}'s default rules, only slightly: affiliate income takes 3% off the multiple compared with display ads or lead generation. The larger effects come from traffic concentration and the revenue trend. A buyer may weigh program risk more heavily, so treat the tool's result as an estimate.

### What multiple do affiliate websites sell for?

{{BRAND}}'s default range for websites is 2.5 to 4.0 times yearly profit, adjusted for the site's facts. That range is an editorial assumption and not a statistic from completed sales.

### Can I sell my affiliate accounts to a buyer?

It depends on each program's own terms. Read them and ask the merchant or network about a change of account owner before you list.

### How many months of records do I need?

Twelve months is the normal minimum. Include statements from every network and payment account, and your expense records.

### Does the tool guarantee a sale price?

No. It gives a low, midpoint and high estimate from the figures you enter. The price a buyer accepts depends on the buyer, the deal terms and the market.
