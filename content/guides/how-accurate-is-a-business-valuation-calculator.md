## Short answer

A business valuation calculator is accurate enough to give you a starting range, and not accurate enough to name the price a buyer will pay. Treat the result as a way to frame a conversation, not as an appraisal.

Two things set how close it gets: how true your inputs are, and how well the calculator's multiples fit a business like yours.

## What limits accuracy

| Limit | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Your inputs | A calculator cannot check them. Wrong profit in means wrong value out. |
| Default multiples | They are assumptions. Real prices vary with buyer type, deal structure and negotiation. |
| What is left out | Brand strength, technical quality, legal issues, seasonality and deal terms such as an [earnout](/glossary/earnout/) are hard to model. |
| Business type | A multiple built for profit-based businesses fits poorly for loss-making or pre-revenue ones. |

VALUERAQ states these limits in its [methodology](/methodology/): inputs are not verified, default multiples are editorial assumptions rather than averages from a transaction dataset, and results are not appraisals or advice. Each result also carries a confidence rating that describes how much information it rests on, not the chance of selling at that price.

## Worked example: how an input error becomes a value error

Using VALUERAQ's default 3.2x midpoint for a profit-based business (an assumption, not market data):

| Monthly profit entered | Annual profit | Value at 3.2x |
|---|---|---|
| $4,000 (true figure) | $48,000 | $153,600 |
| $5,000 (rounded up, add-backs not backed by records) | $60,000 | $192,000 |

A $1,000 monthly error is a 25% error in profit, and it moves the midpoint by $38,400. Multiple ranges add more spread: at 2.5x to 4.0x, $48,000 of profit gives $120,000 to $192,000. The input matters at least as much as the formula. See [valuation mistakes](/guides/valuation-mistakes/).

## How to get a more reliable result

- Use twelve months of profit from bank or payment records, not a best month.
- Count only add-backs you can document.
- Run the calculator twice, with a cautious and a confident set of inputs, and look at the gap.
- Compare the result with a second method, such as [revenue vs profit multiples](/guides/revenue-vs-profit-multiples/).
- For a deal worth real money, ask an accountant or a professional appraiser to review it.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a calculator result the same as an appraisal?

No. An appraisal is a professional opinion based on the specific business and its records. A calculator applies fixed rules to the figures you type. The U.S. Small Business Administration advises using business valuation to set a monetary value and lists income, market and assets approaches; a calculator covers a simple version of one.

### Why do two calculators give different answers?

They use different multiples, different adjustments and sometimes different earnings measures. Compare what each one assumes, then see [how to find out what a business is worth](/guides/how-to-find-out-what-a-business-is-worth/).

### Can a calculator value a business with no profit?

Not well. A profit multiple cannot value a loss, so VALUERAQ's profit-based tools return no estimate in that case. Such businesses are often priced on assets, audience or strategic value.
