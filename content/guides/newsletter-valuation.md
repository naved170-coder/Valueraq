## Key takeaway

Newsletter valuation is the process of estimating what an email publication is worth from the profit it earns and how likely that profit is to continue under a new owner. The usual method is **value = annual net profit × multiple**. A large list helps only if readers open, click and buy, so buyers look past the subscriber count to engagement and to where the money comes from.

## Why newsletters are valued on profit

A newsletter's assets are its subscriber list, its archive, its sponsor relationships and its reputation with readers. None of those has a price on its own. What a buyer can measure is the profit they produce, so small newsletters are normally priced on annual net profit before the owner's pay, also called [SDE](/glossary/sde/).

"Price per subscriber" figures are sometimes quoted, but they vary so widely by topic and engagement that they are a weak guide. Two lists of 40,000 people can earn very different amounts. {{BRAND}} records subscribers in the report for context and bases the estimate on profit.

## The factors that move the multiple

### Open rate and engagement

[Open rate](/glossary/open-rate/) is the share of delivered emails that are recorded as opened. It is the quickest signal of whether a list is alive. It is also imprecise: privacy features in mail apps, such as Apple Mail Privacy Protection, load emails automatically and record opens that never happened. Buyers therefore ask for click rates, replies and unsubscribe rates as well.

### Revenue model

Paid subscriptions are recurring and come directly from readers. Sponsorship revenue has to be sold again every issue or every quarter. A mix of both spreads the risk.

### Sponsor concentration

If one sponsor pays a large share of the bills, losing them would cut profit sharply. This is a form of [customer concentration](/glossary/customer-concentration/), and buyers discount for it.

### Revenue trend and age

A growing newsletter with several years of history gives a buyer more confidence than a new one or a shrinking one. List growth matters less than revenue growth, because lists can be grown cheaply with low-quality signups.

### Dependence on the writer

Many newsletters are read because of one person's voice. If that person leaves at the sale, readers may leave too. A documented editorial process, guest writers or a brand that is bigger than its author all make the business easier to transfer.

## How the valuation is calculated

1. **Annual profit** = average monthly net profit × 12.
2. **Base multiple**: {{BRAND}}'s default assumption for newsletters is 2.5× to 4.0× annual profit (midpoint 3.2×). It is a published editorial assumption, not measured market data; see [data sources](/data-sources/).
3. **Adjustments** published in the [methodology](/methodology/#adjustments):

| Factor | {{BRAND}}'s default adjustment |
|---|---|
| Open rate under 25% | −8% |
| Open rate 25% to 45% | Baseline (0%) |
| Open rate above 45% | +5% |
| Revenue from paid subscriptions | +5% |
| Mixed revenue | +3% |
| Sponsorships and ads | Baseline (0%) |
| Largest sponsor over 15% to 30% of revenue | −6% |
| Largest sponsor over 30% of revenue | −12% |

Revenue trend, age and owner hours are adjusted in the same way as for every other business type. The adjustments are multiplied together, and the combined effect is capped at −50% and +40%.

4. **Range** = annual profit × the adjusted low, mid and high multiples.

## Worked example

A hypothetical sponsor-funded newsletter:

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Monthly revenue | $7,000 |
| Monthly net profit | $6,000 (annual profit $72,000) |
| Subscribers | 45,000 |
| Open rate | 42% |
| Revenue model | Sponsorships and ads |
| Growth, last 12 months | 15% |
| Largest sponsor | 20% of revenue |
| Age | 3 years |
| Owner time | 15 hours a week |

Steady growth adds 8% to the multiple. One sponsor providing 20% of revenue takes off 6%. Open rate, revenue model, age and owner time all sit in their baseline bands. Multiplied together (1.08 × 0.94), the combined adjustment is about +1.5%, giving an adjusted multiple of roughly 2.5× to 4.1× annual profit: around $183,000 to $292,000, with a midpoint near $234,000. ## Common mistakes

- **Valuing the list instead of the profit.** A subscriber count says little without engagement and revenue behind it.
- **Taking open rate at face value.** Automatic opens inflate it. Check clicks and replies, and compare like with like over the same 90 days.
- **Ignoring the cost of the writer.** If the owner writes every issue, a buyer must do that work or pay someone to. Count those hours honestly.
- **Treating booked sponsorships as recurring.** A strong quarter of ad sales is not the same as subscription revenue. Show how many sponsors rebook.
- **Counting unengaged subscribers.** A list that has never been cleaned looks bigger and performs worse. Buyers will ask when inactive addresses were last removed.

## What buyers will ask for

- Read-only access to the email platform, showing list size, opens, clicks and unsubscribes by issue.
- Where subscribers came from: organic signups, referrals, paid ads or list swaps.
- Sponsor invoices and a list of sponsors with how often each has rebooked.
- For paid newsletters, payment-processor exports showing subscriber counts and cancellations by month.
- A profit and loss statement reconciled to bank deposits.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much is a newsletter worth per subscriber?

There is no reliable per-subscriber figure, because what a subscriber is worth depends on the topic, the engagement and how the list earns money. {{BRAND}} values newsletters on annual profit, starting from a default assumption of 2.5× to 4.0×, and treats subscriber count as context.

### Can a newsletter with no profit be valued?

Not with an earnings method. A loss-making or unmonetized newsletter is usually priced on its audience or strategic value to a specific buyer, which the tool does not estimate.

### Does a high open rate raise the value?

A little. {{BRAND}}'s default adds 5% to the multiple above a 45% open rate and takes off 8% below 25%. Because privacy features inflate reported opens, buyers will want click data before giving credit for a high figure.
