## Key takeaway

There is no honest single answer to "how long does it take to sell an online business?" Any average you read without a named source should be treated with caution, and this page does not quote one. What can be said is that every sale passes through the same six stages, and each stage has a short list of things that make it slower.

Most of those delays are in the seller's hands. Missing records, an asking price the numbers cannot support and slow replies to a buyer's questions account for much of the time a sale drags. The buyer's funding and the legal paperwork are the parts you control least.

The timeline later in this guide is an invented example, built to show how delays add up. It is not a statistic and not a promise about your sale.

## The six stages of a sale

| Stage | What happens | What slows it down |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Prepare | Gather records, agree an asking price, write the listing | Messy books, no clear price, unclear ownership of accounts |
| 2. Find a buyer | List the business, answer enquiries, screen buyers | A price above the range, thin listing, slow replies |
| 3. Letter of intent | Agree price and main terms in writing | Gaps between what each side expects, deal structure |
| 4. Due diligence | Buyer checks the figures, accounts and assets | Missing documents, figures that do not match |
| 5. Contract and escrow | Sign the sale agreement, fund escrow | Legal edits, buyer funding, payment method |
| 6. Handover | Move accounts, domain, code and know-how | Accounts not in the seller's name, no written process |

The stages overlap a little in real sales, but they happen in this order. A delay early in the list is passed on to every stage after it.

## Stage 1: Prepare

Preparation is the stage a seller controls completely, and it is the one most often rushed.

The U.S. Small Business Administration (SBA) tells owners selling a business to "Use business valuation to set a monetary value." Do that first. A price you can explain with figures avoids weeks of back-and-forth later. A free estimate from a [valuation tool](/tools/) gives a low, midpoint and high figure to anchor on, and the [methodology](/methodology/) shows how it was reached.

Then gather the evidence a buyer will ask for. The SBA's guide for buyers lists financial statements and tax returns among the documents to evaluate, and says to ask about contracts, leases, existing cash flow and inventory. Having them ready before you list is the single biggest time-saver. The [data room checklist](/guides/data-room-checklist/) sets out what to collect, folder by folder.

Check who owns what. If the domain, the app store account, the payment processor or the hosting sits in a freelancer's name, fix that now. Transfers take as long as the slowest account.

## Stage 2: Find a buyer

Once the listing is live, the pace depends on how many suitable buyers see it and how quickly you answer them.

A listing is slower when it hides the facts a buyer needs, such as how the business earns money, how many hours it takes each week, and where its traffic or sales come from. It is also slower when the price is out of line with the figures. Buyers who run their own numbers will compare the asking price with annual profit before they write to you.

Your choice of route changes this stage most. A broker, a marketplace and a direct sale all reach buyers differently, as [broker vs marketplace vs selling directly](/guides/ways-to-sell-a-digital-business/) explains. If you list on a marketplace, check its rules for how long a review takes before a listing goes live. On {{BRAND}}, listings are reviewed before they appear, and the [marketplace rules](/marketplace-rules/) describe the checks.

Reply to messages promptly. A buyer comparing three businesses tends to move forward with the one that answers first and answers fully.

## Stage 3: Letter of intent

When a buyer is serious, the two sides usually put the main terms in a [letter of intent](/glossary/letter-of-intent/): price, how it is paid, what is included, and a period during which you deal only with that buyer. That last part is an [exclusivity period](/glossary/exclusivity-period/), and it matters for timing because it pauses other offers. Keep it short, and make sure it ends if the buyer stalls.

This stage is quick when both sides agree on the price and slow when they do not. If the gap is about growth that has not yet happened, a structure such as an [earnout](/glossary/earnout/) may close it, but it adds terms to negotiate. The [deal structures guide](/guides/deal-structures-for-online-business-sales/) compares the common options.

## Stage 4: Due diligence

[Due diligence](/glossary/due-diligence/) is the buyer's check of what you have told them. The SBA describes it as a "thorough, objective investigation", and it names the letter of intent, a confidentiality agreement, the sales agreement and any purchase price adjustment as documents that professionals can help with.

This is usually where a sale slows down most, because it is where claims meet records. Three things stretch it:

- **Figures that do not match.** Revenue in the listing, in the payment processor and in the tax return must agree, or you must be able to explain why not.
- **Aggressive add-backs.** BizBuySell warns that "some add-backs can unrealistically overvalue the company". Expect a buyer to question every one, so keep a receipt or a statement behind each.
- **Slow answers.** Each question left open for a few days adds days to the whole sale.

The [due diligence guide](/guides/digital-business-due-diligence/) shows what buyers check and in what order.

## Stage 5: Contract and escrow

After due diligence, the lawyers or the two parties turn the letter of intent into a sale agreement. The SBA recommends that an attorney review the sales agreement, and it also suggests hiring an accountant. Both take time to schedule, so book them while due diligence is running, not after.

The buyer then pays into [escrow](/glossary/escrow/), an independent holder who releases the money when the agreed handover is complete. [How escrow works for website sales](/guides/how-escrow-works-for-website-sales/) covers the steps. Delays here usually come from the buyer's funds: a bank transfer that needs approval, or financing that is not yet in place. Ask for proof of funds early in the process.

## Stage 6: Handover

Handover is the move of the domain, hosting, code, social accounts, email lists and payment accounts, plus the training the buyer needs. It is quicker when each account already sits in your name, and when you have written down how the business runs. See [how to transfer a website to a new owner](/guides/how-to-transfer-a-website-to-a-new-owner/) for the checklist.

Agree a [transition period](/glossary/transition-period/) in the contract so both sides know how long you will help after the money has moved.

## Worked example: how delays add up

The numbers below are invented to show the arithmetic. They are not averages.

A content website earns $4,000 a month in net profit. That is $4,000 × 12 = $48,000 a year. {{BRAND}}'s default range for a website is 2.5x to 4.0x annual profit, before adjustments, so the unadjusted range is:

- Low: $48,000 × 2.5 = $120,000
- Midpoint: $48,000 × 3.2 = $153,600, shown as about $154,000
- High: $48,000 × 4.0 = $192,000

These are {{BRAND}}'s own default assumptions, not market data. The multiples are set out in the [methodology](/methodology/).

**Plan A: a prepared seller.** Records are ready and the price sits inside the range.

| Stage | Weeks |
|---|---|
| Prepare | 3 |
| Find a buyer | 4 |
| Letter of intent | 1 |
| Due diligence | 4 |
| Contract and escrow | 2 |
| Handover | 1 |
| **Total** | **15** |

3 + 4 + 1 + 4 + 2 + 1 = 15 weeks.

![Example plan: weeks spent on each stage: Prepare 3 wk, Find a buyer 4 wk, Letter of intent 1 wk, Due diligence 4 wk, Contract and escrow 2 wk, Handover 1 wk](/static/img/articles/how-long-to-sell-an-online-business-weeks-by-stage.svg){: width="720" height="364" loading="lazy" }

**Plan B: records are missing.** The seller needs 2 extra weeks to rebuild the books before listing, and buyers then need 2 extra weeks in due diligence to get answers. 15 + 2 + 2 = 19 weeks.

**Plan C: records missing and the price too high.** The seller asks $250,000. That is $250,000 ÷ $48,000 = 5.2 times annual profit, above the 4.0x top of the default range. Buyers hesitate, and the listing needs 6 extra weeks to attract a serious offer. 19 + 6 = 25 weeks.

![Example plan: total weeks from start to handover: Prepared seller 15 wk, Records missing 19 wk, Records missing and price too high 25 wk](/static/img/articles/how-long-to-sell-an-online-business-total-weeks-by-scenario.svg){: width="720" height="226" loading="lazy" }

The extra 10 weeks between Plan A and Plan C came from two things the seller could have settled before listing. Nothing here predicts how long your sale will take, but the pattern is the useful part: early gaps are passed down the line.

## What you control and what you do not

| You control | You partly control | You do not control |
|---|---|---|
| Records and account ownership | Which buyers see the listing | A buyer's funding and bank timing |
| Asking price | How fast you answer | Legal advisers' schedules |
| Written handover notes | The length of exclusivity | A buyer who changes their mind |

If a quick sale matters more to you than the top price, say so to yourself before you list, and set the asking price near the lower half of your range. If the price matters more, plan for a longer timeline and keep the business running well in the meantime, because earnings that fall during a sale invite a lower offer. [When to sell an online business](/guides/when-to-sell-an-online-business/) covers timing the decision itself.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I sell an online business in a few weeks?

Possibly, if the records are ready, the price matches the figures and the buyer has funds. Each stage still has to happen. Rushing due diligence tends to bring a lower price or a failed sale.

### Which stage takes the longest?

For many sellers it is finding the right buyer or due diligence. It depends on the sale. Finding a buyer is slow when the price is high or the listing is thin, and due diligence is slow when records are incomplete.

### Does the way I sell change the timeline?

Yes. A broker, a marketplace listing and a direct sale reach buyers differently and involve different amounts of your own time. Compare them in [broker vs marketplace vs selling directly](/guides/ways-to-sell-a-digital-business/) before you choose.

### What is the best way to avoid delays?

Value the business first, collect your records into a data room, put accounts in your own name and answer buyers quickly. These steps cost little and remove the most common causes of delay.
