## Key takeaway

A valuation is only as good as the records behind it. Whether you are checking your own online business before a sale or a buyer is checking someone else's, the same short list of documents decides how much the number can be trusted: monthly profit and loss statements, payment and bank records that match them, traffic or customer data, proof of ownership of every account, and a clear list of what the owner costs the business.

This checklist groups about 30 items by category, marks which ones buyers treat as essential, and shows with a worked example how missing records turn directly into a lower price. It is written for websites, stores, newsletters, apps and small SaaS businesses, where much of the value sits in accounts and data rather than in buildings or stock.

## Why a valuation needs documents, not just a number

A valuation turns a business into a price. The U.S. Small Business Administration advises owners to "use business valuation to set a monetary value" and describes three common approaches: an income approach that "looks at projected revenue and accounts for potential risks," a market approach that "compares your business to other similar businesses that have recently sold," and an assets approach that "subtracts total business liabilities from the total value of all assets."

Every one of those approaches starts from records. The income approach needs real revenue and profit. The market approach needs figures you can compare with other sales. The assets approach needs proof that you own what you list. For small online businesses, buyers usually start from yearly profit and apply a multiple, so the profit figure and the evidence for it carry most of the weight. {{BRAND}}'s tools use the same method, described on the [methodology page](/methodology/).

## The online business valuation checklist

Use the tables below as a working list. "Essential" means almost every buyer asks for it before making a serious offer. "Helpful" means it supports a higher price or answers a question before it is asked.

### 1. Financial records

| Item | Period | Priority | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monthly profit and loss statement | 12 to 24 months | Essential | Shows revenue, costs and profit month by month |
| Revenue split by source | Same months | Essential | Shows which income would be at risk if one source stopped |
| Expense detail with invoices for large items | Same months | Essential | Lets a buyer check every cost is real and needed |
| Tax returns or filed accounts | Last 1 to 2 years | Essential | Confirms the figures you report to the tax authority |
| Bank statements for the business account | Same months | Essential | Proves the money arrived |
| Balance sheet, if you keep one | Latest | Helpful | Lists what the business owns and owes |
| List of debts, subscriptions and unpaid bills | Latest | Essential | A buyer needs to know what they take on |

How many months to prepare is covered in [how many months of data buyers want](/guides/how-many-months-of-data-do-buyers-want/). The short version is at least 12, and 24 is better.

### 2. Payment and income proof

| Item | Priority | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Payment processor payout reports (card payments, PayPal, store payouts) | Essential | Matches sales to money received |
| Affiliate network and ad network earnings reports | Essential | Proves income that never touches a store checkout |
| Marketplace seller reports, if you sell on one | Essential | Shows sales, fees and returns |
| Refund and chargeback history | Helpful | Shows how often customers ask for money back |
| Subscription export: active, cancelled, start dates | Essential for subscription businesses | Lets a buyer calculate churn and recurring revenue |

The matching of reports to bank records is the most common point where deals slow down. [How to prove your revenue to a buyer](/guides/how-to-prove-your-revenue-to-a-buyer/) shows the steps.

### 3. Traffic, customers and marketing

| Item | Priority | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Analytics access (view-only) | Essential | Lets the buyer see visits themselves |
| Search Console access (view-only) | Essential for search-led sites | Shows which searches bring visitors |
| Traffic by source, by month | Essential | Shows dependence on one channel |
| Top pages or products by revenue | Helpful | Shows how concentrated income is |
| Email list size, growth and send history | Helpful | An owned audience is less exposed to platform changes |
| Advertising account history and spend | Helpful | Shows what paid traffic cost and whether it can be repeated |

For checking the traffic side from a buyer's chair, see [how to verify website traffic](/guides/how-to-verify-website-traffic/).

### 4. Ownership and accounts

| Item | Priority | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Domain registrar login or transfer proof | Essential | The domain is usually the core asset |
| Hosting, store platform or app store account details | Essential | The business cannot run without them |
| Social media and brand accounts | Helpful | Part of the audience and brand |
| Software and tool subscriptions list | Helpful | Shows ongoing costs and what must be re-created |
| Trademarks, logos and content ownership | Helpful | Proves what is transferable |
| Contracts with suppliers, writers and developers | Essential if any exist | Shows who the business depends on |

The related list of what typically moves to a buyer is in [what is included when you buy a website](/guides/what-is-included-when-you-buy-a-website/).

### 5. Owner involvement and add-backs

| Item | Priority | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Owner's hours per week and main tasks | Essential | A business that needs 50 hours a week is worth less than one that needs 5 |
| Documented processes and handover notes | Helpful | Shortens the transition |
| Add-back list: owner pay, personal costs, one-off items | Essential | Raises profit to the real earnings a buyer would receive |
| Evidence for each add-back | Essential | See below |

BizBuySell notes that "add-backs are expenses added back to a company's bottom-line profits when determining Seller's Discretionary Earnings (SDE) or EBITDA," and warns that "some add-backs can unrealistically overvalue the company." Both sides should read that warning. A seller who cannot show an invoice, a bank line or a clear explanation for an add-back will find it removed in negotiation. The steps are in [how to calculate SDE](/guides/how-to-calculate-sde/).

## Which records move the price most

Not every item moves the number equally. Three have the most direct effect.

1. **Profit.** Profit multiplied by a multiple is the starting point for most small online sales. Any dollar of profit you cannot document is a dollar a buyer will not pay a multiple on.
2. **Concentration.** If most income comes from one traffic source, one customer or one product, a buyer will price in the risk. Showing the split early avoids the surprise.
3. **Owner dependence.** Hours per week and documented processes decide how much a buyer must pay someone else to run the business.

## Worked example: what missing records cost

This example is illustrative. It uses {{BRAND}}'s default website range of 2.5 to 4.0 times yearly profit, with a midpoint of 3.2. These are default assumptions, not market data.

A content website earns $9,000 a month in revenue. Its owner has prepared 24 months of profit and loss statements and payout reports that match the bank.

| Line | Yearly figure |
|---|---|
| Revenue ($9,000 x 12) | $108,000 |
| Business costs (hosting, writers, tools) | $48,000 |
| Documented SDE ($108,000 - $48,000; the owner takes no salary) | $60,000 |

**Documented case.** Every add-back has an invoice or bank line behind it, so the buyer accepts $60,000.

- Low: $60,000 x 2.5 = $150,000
- Midpoint: $60,000 x 3.2 = $192,000
- High: $60,000 x 4.0 = $240,000

**Undocumented case.** Suppose $6,000 of the profit comes from a sponsor paid by invoice outside the main payment account, and the owner has no record of it. The buyer leaves it out, so SDE becomes $54,000.

- Midpoint: $54,000 x 3.2 = $172,800

The gap at the midpoint is $192,000 - $172,800 = $19,200. That is a loss of $19,200 from a $6,000 record gap, because the multiple applies to every dollar of profit. A buyer who also cannot confirm the traffic is likely to ask for a lower multiple as well, which would widen the gap further.

## Common gaps that lower the multiple

These problems appear again and again when online businesses are checked, and each one can be fixed before a valuation.

- **Income paid outside the main account.** A PayPal balance, a personal bank account or cash that is never shown. Move it into the books and keep the record.
- **Costs mixed with personal spending.** If a laptop, phone and software plan are shared with personal use, split them and say how.
- **One-off items hidden in monthly figures.** A single large sponsor payment or refund in one month should be labelled so a buyer can see normal months.
- **No access to analytics.** If traffic data lives only in the owner's personal login, a buyer cannot check it without a screen share.
- **Accounts in someone else's name.** A domain registered by a former developer or a store opened by a friend creates a transfer problem.
- **Missing contracts.** Verbal deals with writers or suppliers are hard to hand over. Put them in writing.
- **Seasonality not explained.** A strong December and a weak summer look alarming without context. See [seasonality](/glossary/seasonality/) for how buyers read it.

## How to use the checklist

1. **Gather before you list or value.** Create one folder with the sections above. A buyer-ready folder is what [a data room](/guides/data-room-checklist/) means in practice.
2. **Reconcile.** Make sure monthly revenue in the profit and loss statement matches the payout reports and bank deposits.
3. **List your add-backs and attach evidence.**
4. **Write one page on the business.** Income sources, traffic sources, owner hours and the main risks.
5. **Run a valuation.** With clean numbers, a tool such as the [Website Valuation Tool](/tools/website-valuation/) gives a range you can defend.
6. **Fix the biggest gap first.** If one item would change profit by the most, work on that before anything else.

For buyers, the same list works in reverse: ask for each item, and note what is missing. The [financial due diligence checklist](/guides/financial-due-diligence-checklist/) goes deeper on the checks to run after you receive them.

## When a professional valuation is worth it

A free tool gives a starting range from your own inputs. A formal valuation by a qualified professional is a different service, and it may be needed for tax filings, legal disputes, partner buy-outs or large deals. The IRS defines fair market value in Publication 561 as "the price that property would sell for on the open market." If a valuation has a legal or tax purpose, ask an accountant or valuation professional which standard applies, and give them the same documents listed here. They will ask for them either way.

## Frequently asked questions

### What documents do I need for a business valuation?

At minimum: 12 to 24 months of profit and loss statements, tax returns, bank and payment processor records that match, traffic or customer data, a list of what you own and owe, and a list of the owner's costs and one-off items. Online businesses should add analytics access, ad account history and subscription exports.

### How long does it take to prepare for a valuation?

For a business with tidy accounts, a few days of collecting reports. If income and costs are mixed with personal spending, plan a few weeks to separate them and attach evidence. Starting early is cheaper than fixing gaps during a sale.

### Do I need an accountant?

For a quick estimate, no. For tax returns, add-backs that buyers will test or a valuation with legal use, the SBA recommends getting professional help, including an attorney to review any sales agreement. An accountant can also confirm that your profit figure follows a consistent method.

### Does a valuation checklist change for a store or a SaaS business?

Yes, slightly. Stores need supplier terms, inventory and return data. SaaS businesses need subscription exports and churn, which Stripe defines as "the percentage of total customers who discontinue using a product or service during a given period." The rest of the list is the same.

### Will a buyer accept my own spreadsheet?

A spreadsheet helps as a summary, but buyers check it against the source reports. Share the exports and the bank lines behind each number.
