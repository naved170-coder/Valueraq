## The short answer

Yes, you can sell a website with no revenue. Buyers do purchase sites that earn nothing, usually because they want a head start: an aged domain, a library of articles, search traffic or an audience they would otherwise spend months building.

What changes is how the price is set. VALUERAQ's [website valuation tool](/tools/website-valuation/) multiplies yearly profit by a multiple, and it returns no estimate when profit is zero or negative, because an earnings multiple cannot value a site that earns nothing. A no-revenue site is priced on its parts instead.

## What a buyer is paying for

The U.S. Small Business Administration says a valuation should account for intangible assets, which "can include intangible assets like brand presence, intellectual property, customer information, and projection of future revenue" ([SBA](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/manage-your-business/close-or-sell-your-business)). For a website with no revenue, that translates into four things:

- **The domain.** Age, a clean history and a name that fits a topic all matter. See [domain appraisal](/guides/domain-appraisal-explained/).
- **The content.** Original articles, tools or product pages that a buyer would otherwise have to commission.
- **The audience.** Search visitors, social followers and email subscribers. Buyers will want proof, so be ready to show how you [verify website traffic](/guides/how-to-verify-website-traffic/).
- **A clear path to earning.** A buyer pays more when you can explain, with evidence, how the site could make money.

## A worked example of the rebuild-cost check

One way to set a floor is to ask what it would cost a buyer to rebuild the site. This is an illustration with made-up inputs, not a market figure.

A site has 40 original articles. A buyer estimates 6 hours to research and write each, valued at $30 an hour, plus $400 for design and setup.

**Rebuild cost = 40 × 6 × $30 + $400 = $7,200 + $400 = $7,600.**

That number ignores traffic and the domain's age, which a buyer cannot recreate quickly. It is a starting point for a conversation, not a price. Buyers also weigh the [fair market value](/glossary/fair-market-value/) idea: what a willing buyer would actually pay on the open market.

## How to give the sale its best chance

1. **Show the traffic.** Screenshots of analytics and search reports from the last 12 months carry more weight than a description.
2. **List what is included.** Domain, content, social accounts, email list, design files and logins.
3. **Be honest about the revenue.** Say plainly that it earns nothing. A buyer who finds out later will walk away.
4. **Price on parts, then be ready to negotiate.** Expect the buyer to compare your ask with their own rebuild cost.

## When it will be hard to sell

A site with no revenue, no traffic and little content has little for a buyer to value. Sites with a Google penalty, copied content or unclear rights to images and text are harder still. Fix or disclose these before you list.

If the site earns even a small profit, price it with the tool and read [how to sell a website](/guides/how-to-sell-a-website/) for the full process.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do buyers really pay for a website that makes no money?

Some do, when the domain, content or audience would save them time and money. The price is lower than for an earning site and the buyer's questions are tougher.

### How is a no-revenue website priced?

On its assets and what it would cost to rebuild, not on a profit multiple. See [how to value a business with no profit](/guides/how-to-value-a-business-with-no-profit/).

### Should I wait until the site earns something?

Even a small, steady profit lets a buyer use a multiple and gives you a clearer story. If you can earn a few months of real revenue before listing, it usually helps.
