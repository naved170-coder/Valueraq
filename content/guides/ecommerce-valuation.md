## Key takeaway

Ecommerce valuation is the process of estimating what an online store is worth from the profit it earns, the risks attached to that profit and the stock that comes with it. The usual method is **value = annual net profit × multiple, plus inventory at cost**. Two stores with the same sales can be worth very different amounts, because margins differ far more in ecommerce than in software or content.

## Why stores are valued on profit, not revenue

A store with $500,000 in sales may keep $100,000 or $10,000, depending on product cost, shipping, returns, payment fees and advertising. A revenue multiple hides that difference. Small stores are therefore priced on annual net profit before the owner's pay, also called [SDE](/glossary/sde/). See [revenue vs profit multiples](/guides/revenue-vs-profit-multiples/).

## The factors that move the multiple

### Margins

[Gross margin](/glossary/gross-margin/) is what is left after the cost of the goods and of getting them to the customer. Net profit then also deducts advertising, software and staff. Thin margins leave little room if supplier prices or ad costs rise.

### Fulfillment

How orders are stored and shipped affects how easily the business can be handed over. Stock held with a third-party logistics provider (3PL) transfers with a change of account. An owner's own warehouse involves a lease, staff and equipment. Dropshipping needs no stock but depends on suppliers the buyer does not control.

### Channel concentration

A store that makes most of its sales through one marketplace or one advertising platform is exposed to that platform's fees, rules and account decisions.

### Repeat customers and order value

Customers who come back cost nothing to acquire the second time. Buyers look at the repeat purchase rate, [average order value](/glossary/average-order-value/) and [conversion rate](/glossary/conversion-rate/) to judge how efficiently the store turns visitors into profit. {{BRAND}}'s ecommerce tool does not adjust for these directly.

### Revenue trend, age and owner hours

Growth, a longer history and a low owner workload support a higher multiple, as with any digital business. Check the trend against the same months a year earlier, because many stores are seasonal.

## How the valuation is calculated

1. **Annual profit** = average monthly net profit × 12.
2. **Base multiple**: {{BRAND}}'s default assumption for ecommerce is 2.0× to 3.5× annual profit (midpoint 2.7×). It is a published editorial assumption, not measured market data; see [data sources](/data-sources/).
3. **Adjustments** published in the [methodology](/methodology/#adjustments):

| Factor | {{BRAND}}'s default adjustment |
|---|---|
| Third-party logistics (3PL) | +3% |
| Own warehouse | Baseline (0%) |
| Dropshipping | −8% |
| Over 60% to 80% of sales from one channel | −6% |
| Over 80% from one channel | −12% |

Revenue trend, age and owner hours are adjusted in the same way as for every other business type. The adjustments are multiplied together, and the combined effect is capped at −50% and +40%.

4. **Range** = annual profit × the adjusted low, mid and high multiples.
5. **Inventory** is added on top at cost. It is not multiplied. In practice it is usually counted at closing and paid for separately.

## Worked example

A hypothetical store selling its own branded products:

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Monthly revenue | $40,000 |
| Monthly net profit | $7,000 (annual profit $84,000) |
| Inventory at cost | $35,000 |
| Fulfillment | Third-party logistics (3PL) |
| Growth, last 12 months | 8% |
| Largest channel | 55% of sales |
| Age | 5 years |
| Owner time | 25 hours a week |

Steady growth adds 8% to the multiple, five years of history adds 5% and 3PL fulfillment adds 3%. Owner time of 25 hours a week takes off 5%. The largest channel is under 60%, so it sits in the baseline band. Multiplied together (1.08 × 1.05 × 1.03 × 0.95), the combined adjustment is about +11%, giving an adjusted multiple of roughly 2.2× to 3.9× annual profit: around $186,000 to $326,000, with a midpoint near $252,000.

Adding $35,000 of inventory gives about $221,000 to $361,000, with a midpoint near $287,000. The [Ecommerce Valuation Tool](/tools/ecommerce-valuation/) opens with these figures.

These numbers are illustrative, not market data.

## Common mistakes

- **Applying the multiple to revenue.** Profit is the base. A high-revenue, low-margin store is not worth a multiple of its sales.
- **Multiplying inventory.** Stock is added once, at cost. It should not also be inside the multiplied figure.
- **Counting stock that won't sell.** Damaged, expired or slow-moving items are usually excluded or discounted at the stock count.
- **Leaving out the owner's costs.** Returns, refunds, storage fees and the full advertising bill all belong in the profit figure.
- **Judging the trend on a seasonal peak.** Use a full 12 months and compare each period with the same period a year earlier.

## What buyers will ask for

- Store-platform and marketplace sales reports by month, by product and by channel.
- Payment-processor payouts reconciled to bank deposits.
- Supplier invoices and terms, including minimum orders and lead times.
- Advertising account statements.
- An inventory list with quantities, cost and age.
- Return and refund rates, and customer reviews.

The [due diligence guide](/guides/digital-business-due-diligence/) covers the wider checklist.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is inventory included in the price of an ecommerce business?

Normally it is paid for on top of the earnings-based price, at what the seller paid for it, after a stock count at closing. {{BRAND}}'s tool shows the estimate with and without inventory.

### What multiple does an online store sell for?

It depends on margins, trend, channel risk and the owner's workload, and prices vary from deal to deal. {{BRAND}} starts from a default assumption of 2.0× to 3.5× annual profit and adjusts. That range is an editorial assumption, not a measured market average.

### Are dropshipping stores worth less?

For the same profit, {{BRAND}}'s default takes 8% off the multiple for dropshipping, because the business depends on suppliers the buyer does not control. A dropshipping store with long, stable supplier relationships may deserve a smaller discount.
