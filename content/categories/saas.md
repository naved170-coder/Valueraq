## Buying a SaaS business

A SaaS acquisition normally includes the source code and infrastructure, the brand and domain, customer accounts and their subscription contracts, integrations, documentation and sometimes staff or contractors. Deals can be structured as an **asset sale** or as a purchase of the company that owns the product; the structure affects tax, liabilities and contract transfers, so take professional advice.

Common SaaS models listed on {{BRAND}}:

| Model | Typical customers | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| B2B SaaS | Businesses on monthly or annual plans | Contract terms, customer concentration, churn by plan |
| B2C SaaS | Consumers on low-priced plans | Churn, acquisition cost, payment failures |
| Plugins and extensions | Users of a larger platform | The host platform's policies and marketplace rules |
| API products | Developers | Usage-based revenue volatility, uptime obligations |

## Metrics to compare on every listing

- **MRR and ARR**, and how MRR has moved month by month for at least 12 months.
- **Monthly churn**, by customers and ideally by revenue.
- **Gross margin**: revenue after hosting, third-party services and payment fees.
- **Largest customer's share** of revenue.
- **Profit**, and the implied profit multiple shown on each listing.

## How SaaS businesses are valued

Small SaaS businesses are usually valued as a multiple of ARR or of annual profit. {{BRAND}}'s default assumption is 2× to 5× ARR before adjustments for growth, churn, margin, concentration, age and owner time. See the [methodology](/methodology/) or check a listing's figures in the [SaaS Valuation Tool](/tools/saas-valuation/).

## Buying tips specific to SaaS

1. **Get billing-system data, not summaries.** A read-only export from the billing platform shows MRR movements, failed payments and refunds.
2. **Review the code and infrastructure** with a developer you trust: dependencies, security practices, test coverage, hosting costs and who holds the keys.
3. **Read the terms of service and customer contracts** to confirm they can be transferred to you.
4. **Check support load.** Ticket volume and response times show how much work the product needs.
5. **Confirm third-party dependencies**: APIs, platforms and app marketplaces the product relies on.

## Frequently asked questions

### What should a small SaaS business cost?

There is no fixed price per dollar of revenue. Under {{BRAND}}'s default assumptions a SaaS business with $10,000 MRR ($120,000 ARR) would start from $240,000 to $600,000 before adjustments. Fast growth and low churn move it up; decline, high churn and heavy owner involvement move it down.

### Is a SaaS business with high churn worth buying?

It can be, if you know why customers leave and can fix it. Price that work into your offer: high churn means much of the revenue has to be replaced each year.

### Do customers need to agree to the sale?

It depends on the contracts and how the sale is structured. Some B2B contracts require consent to assignment. Check early.
