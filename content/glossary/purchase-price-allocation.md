## How it works

When a buyer purchases the assets of a business in an [asset sale](/glossary/asset-sale/), one price covers many different things. The parties agree how much of it belongs to each group of assets and write the split into the [purchase agreement](/glossary/purchase-agreement/). Typical groups for a digital business are:

- **Stock**, valued at cost.
- **Equipment**, such as computers and cameras.
- **Domain names, software, content and trademarks.**
- **Customer lists and contracts.**
- **[Goodwill](/glossary/goodwill/)**, the remainder paid for reputation, audience and earning power.

## Example

A buyer pays $250,000 for an ecommerce business.

| Asset | Allocated amount |
|---|---|
| Stock | $28,000 |
| Equipment | $4,000 |
| Domain, brand and content | $60,000 |
| Customer list | $8,000 |
| Goodwill (the rest) | $150,000 |
| **Total** | **$250,000** |

The goodwill figure is simply the total less the other items: $250,000 − $28,000 − $4,000 − $60,000 − $8,000 = $150,000. The amounts are illustrative, not typical.

## Why it matters in a valuation

The allocation does not change the price, but it can change how each side is taxed on the sale. Different asset types can be taxed differently, and the buyer and seller often prefer different splits. For that reason the allocation is usually agreed before closing, and both sides report it consistently.

Keep the allocation reasonable. Putting an unrealistic value on one asset, such as stock far above its cost, invites questions from the other side and from tax authorities. Back each figure with a stock report, an equipment list or an appraisal where one is sensible, and keep copies with the signed agreement.

Sellers should expect the buyer to ask for supporting values for stock and equipment. Buyers should confirm the split matches what the agreement says is being transferred.
