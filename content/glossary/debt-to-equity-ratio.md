## How it works

A business is paid for by two sources: money it owes (liabilities) and money that belongs to the owners (equity). The debt-to-equity ratio compares them. Both numbers come from the [balance sheet](/glossary/balance-sheet/).

**Debt-to-equity ratio = total liabilities ÷ owners' equity**

Some people use only borrowed money (loans and credit lines) on top instead of all liabilities. Always check which version a seller or lender is using before you compare two figures.

A ratio of 1.0 means the business owes as much as the owners have in it. A higher ratio means more of the business is funded by other people's money. A very low ratio means it relies mostly on the owners' own money and profits kept in the business.

## Example

An ecommerce business has the following balance sheet. The figures are made up.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Total assets | $150,000 |
| Total liabilities (supplier invoices, a bank loan, tax owed) | $90,000 |
| Owners' equity (assets minus liabilities) | $60,000 |

Check: $150,000 − $90,000 = $60,000.

**Debt-to-equity ratio = $90,000 ÷ $60,000 = 1.5**

The business owes $1.50 for every $1.00 the owners hold. If it repaid $30,000 of the loan from cash, liabilities would fall to $60,000, assets to $120,000, and the ratio to $60,000 ÷ $60,000 = 1.0.

## Why the debt-to-equity ratio matters in a valuation

- **It shows what comes with the business.** In a sale where the buyer takes over the company itself, debts may come along. In an [asset sale](/glossary/asset-sale/), they usually stay with the seller. Know which applies.
- **It shows risk.** High debt means fixed repayments that must be met even in a slow month.
- **It changes the price.** Debt is taken off when moving from [enterprise value](/glossary/enterprise-value/) to the value of the owners' share. See [enterprise value](/glossary/enterprise-value/) for how that figure is built.
- **It is not good or bad alone.** A business with steady subscription income can carry more debt than one with seasonal sales. Compare it with the business's own history.

Small online businesses often have a low ratio because they have few loans. Check for loans from the owner, unpaid tax and customer prepayments, which still count as liabilities.
