## How it works

An FBA seller buys or makes products, sends them to Amazon's fulfillment centers, and lists them on Amazon. When a customer orders, Amazon picks, packs and ships the item. In return Amazon charges fees, which the seller should check on Amazon's own seller pages, since they change.

For the seller, the work moves from packing boxes to buying stock, planning how much to send, and managing listings and advertising. The business runs through one seller account on one marketplace.

## Example

An illustration with round numbers. An FBA brand sells $20,000 of products in a month. The cost of the products is $7,000, Amazon's fees are $5,000, advertising is $3,000, and other costs are $1,000. Net profit is $20,000 − $7,000 − $5,000 − $3,000 − $1,000 = $4,000 a month, or $48,000 a year.

At {{BRAND}}'s default ecommerce range of 2.0 to 3.5 times yearly profit, the midpoint is 2.7 times, so $48,000 × 2.7 = $129,600, about $130,000. Stock the buyer takes over is normally paid for separately at cost, after a count at closing. With $20,000 of stock, the midpoint including stock is about $150,000. The multiple is a platform assumption, not market data.

## Why it matters in a valuation

Most of an FBA business's sales come through one account on one marketplace. The valuation therefore leans on how concentrated sales are: {{BRAND}}'s default takes 6% off the multiple when more than 60% of sales come from one channel, and 12% when more than 80% do. Buyers also check that the account is in good standing, that the listings and brand are in the seller's name, and that profit is worked out after all Amazon fees and returns.
