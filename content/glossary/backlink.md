## How it works

When site A links to site B, that link is a backlink for B. It does two jobs. It can send visitors, because people click it. It can also be a signal to search engines that other sites consider the page worth pointing to, which can help it appear in search results. Not all links count equally: a link from a relevant, trusted site is worth more than many links from low-quality pages.

Some links carry an attribute such as "nofollow", which asks search engines not to treat the link as an endorsement. Links that were bought or swapped only to influence rankings can lead to a page being ranked lower. Backlinks that were earned, because the content was useful, are the safest kind.

## Example

A recipe site is linked from 150 pages across 40 different websites. The count that matters most is the 40 distinct sites, not the 150 links.

Suppose 120 of the 150 links (80%) come from a single network of blogs owned by one person. Then:

- Total links: 150
- Links from the one network: 120
- Share from one source: 120 ÷ 150 = **80%**

If that network disappears or removes the links, most of the site's backlinks go with it.

## Why backlinks matter in a valuation

For a website, backlinks help explain where [organic traffic](/glossary/organic-traffic/) comes from and whether it will last. A buyer values profit, and links matter because they support the traffic that produces it.

Buyers usually ask:

- How many different sites link to the pages that bring in the most visits?
- Were links earned, or paid for or traded?
- Does the link profile depend on one source?
- Has the owner received any notice from a search engine about unnatural links?
