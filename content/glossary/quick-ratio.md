## How it works

The quick ratio asks a harsher version of the question behind the [current ratio](/glossary/current-ratio/): if the business had to pay its near-term bills soon, could it do so without selling any stock? It counts only cash and the money customers already owe, because stock can be slow to sell.

**Quick ratio = (cash + accounts receivable) ÷ current liabilities**

Some people reach the same number a second way, by subtracting stock from current assets and dividing by current liabilities. When a business holds only cash, receivables and stock, the two methods give the same answer. All the figures come from the [balance sheet](/glossary/balance-sheet/).

A ratio of 1.0 means the business holds exactly enough quick assets to cover what it owes in the near term. Above 1.0 gives a cushion. Below 1.0 means it would need sales or new funds to pay its bills on time.

## Example

An online store's balance sheet shows the following. The figures are made up.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Cash | $22,000 |
| Customer receivables | $9,000 |
| Stock | $29,000 |
| Supplier invoices (current liability) | $16,000 |
| Sales tax owed (current liability) | $3,000 |
| Customer prepayments (current liability) | $6,000 |

Quick assets = $22,000 + $9,000 = $31,000. Current liabilities = $16,000 + $3,000 + $6,000 = $25,000.

**Quick ratio = $31,000 ÷ $25,000 = 1.24**

For comparison, the current ratio counts the $29,000 of stock too: ($31,000 + $29,000) ÷ $25,000 = 2.4. The gap between 2.4 and 1.24 shows how much of the store's cushion is stock that still has to be sold.

## Why the quick ratio matters in a valuation

- **It shows who is really covered.** A store with lots of stock but little cash can look safe on the current ratio and tight on the quick ratio.
- **It tests receivables.** The ratio is only as good as the money owed by customers. Ask how old those invoices are and whether they get paid. See [accounts receivable](/glossary/accounts-receivable/).
- **It affects the deal.** Buyers often negotiate [working capital](/glossary/working-capital/) in the sale contract, so know your quick ratio before they ask.
- **It is a snapshot.** It describes one date. Read it with [cash flow](/glossary/cash-flow/) and the last twelve months of statements.

No single ratio is "good" for every business. A subscription business that collects payment in advance can run below 1.0 because customer prepayments are liabilities. Compare a business with its own history and with similar businesses.
