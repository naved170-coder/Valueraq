## How it works

**P/E ratio = price ÷ yearly earnings**

For a listed company, price is the share price and earnings are earnings per share. For a business being sold, the same idea uses the whole price and the whole year's [net income](/glossary/net-income/). A P/E of 4 means the price equals four years of earnings.

The P/E ratio is a type of [valuation multiple](/glossary/valuation-multiple/). Small online deals more often use a multiple of owner profit instead, but the logic is the same: price as a number of years of profit.

## Example

A seller lists a newsletter for $120,000. Its [normalized earnings](/glossary/normalized-earnings/) for the last twelve months were $40,000.

- **P/E = $120,000 ÷ $40,000 = 3.0**

The buyer is paying three years of earnings. If a second listing asks $150,000 for $60,000 of earnings, its P/E is 2.5, so it is cheaper per dollar of profit, even though the price is higher. The ratio makes different-sized businesses comparable.

You can turn it around. If you expect a P/E of 3.0 and a site earns $50,000, the implied price is 3.0 × $50,000 = $150,000. VALUERAQ's default range for a website is 2.5x to 4.0x profit, with 3.2x as the midpoint, so the same site would show $125,000 to $200,000. Those are starting assumptions, not market data.

## Why it matters in a valuation

- **A high P/E means buyers pay more per dollar of earnings.** That usually reflects expected growth, stable income or low risk.
- **A low P/E can mean risk or a bargain.** Check why: falling traffic, one big customer or an owner who does all the work.
- **The earnings used matter.** Net income, owner profit and earnings with adjustments all give different ratios. Always check which one a number uses.
- **It ignores debt.** Where debt matters, compare [enterprise value](/glossary/enterprise-value/) with earnings before interest instead.
