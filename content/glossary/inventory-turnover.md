## How it works

The formula is:

**Inventory turnover = cost of goods sold ÷ average inventory**

Average inventory is usually the stock value at the start of the period plus the stock value at the end, divided by two. Both figures are taken at cost, not at the selling price. [Cost of goods sold](/glossary/cost-of-goods-sold/) comes from the profit and loss statement.

A higher number means stock sells faster. A lower number means money sits on the shelf. Dividing 365 by the turnover gives the average days it takes to sell stock.

## Example

An online store has $120,000 of cost of goods sold in a year. Stock at cost was $30,000 at the start and $40,000 at the end, so average inventory is ($30,000 + $40,000) ÷ 2 = $35,000.

Inventory turnover is $120,000 ÷ $35,000 = 3.43 times a year. Days to sell stock are 365 ÷ 3.43 = about 106 days.

If the same store held $20,000 on average, the turnover would be $120,000 ÷ $20,000 = 6 times, or about 61 days. Same sales, but less cash tied up. The figures are illustrations, not benchmarks, and a healthy level differs by product.

## Why it matters in a valuation

{{BRAND}}'s ecommerce tool values a store on profit and shows inventory at cost separately, because stock is normally paid for on top of the price after a count at closing. Turnover helps a buyer judge that stock. Slow turnover can mean old or unsellable items, which the buyer should not pay full cost for. Fast turnover can mean the store needs more [working capital](/glossary/working-capital/) to keep shelves filled.

Buyers often ask for a stock list with the age of each item during [due diligence](/guides/digital-business-due-diligence/). See the [ecommerce valuation guide](/guides/ecommerce-valuation/) for how inventory is treated.
