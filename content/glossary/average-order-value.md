## Formula

**AOV = revenue in a period ÷ number of orders in that period**

Be consistent about what counts as revenue. Most stores use product sales after discounts and before shipping and sales tax. Whichever basis you choose, use the same one every month so the trend is meaningful.

AOV is measured per order, not per customer. A customer who buys three times counts as three orders.

## Example

An online store takes $48,000 in a month from 800 orders.

**AOV = $48,000 ÷ 800 = $60.**

## How AOV connects to profit

Revenue is the product of three numbers:

**Revenue = visitors × conversion rate × AOV**

With 40,000 visitors, a 2% [conversion rate](/glossary/conversion-rate/) and a $60 AOV, revenue is 40,000 × 2% × $60 = $48,000. Raising AOV to $66 with the same traffic and conversion rate lifts revenue to $52,800, a 10% increase, without acquiring a single extra visitor.

Many costs are charged per order: picking, packing, shipping and the fixed part of payment fees. A higher AOV spreads those costs over more revenue, so profit often rises faster than sales.

## Why AOV matters when buying or selling

- **Room for advertising.** AOV, together with [gross margin](/glossary/gross-margin/), sets how much the store can pay to win an order. If gross profit per order is lower than [CAC](/glossary/cac/), the store depends on repeat purchases to make money.
- **Trend.** A falling AOV alongside steady revenue can mean heavier discounting or a shift to cheaper products.
- **Mix.** An average can hide a lot. A $60 AOV might be mostly $30 orders plus a few large wholesale ones. Buyers will ask for the spread of order sizes and for AOV by channel.
- **Opportunity.** Bundles, free-shipping thresholds and add-on offers are common ways to raise AOV, and a buyer may see untried ones as upside.

## Worked example: what a higher AOV does to a store's value

All figures are invented to show the arithmetic. Assume 800 orders a month, product cost of 50% of the order, $8 of fulfilment and payment cost per order, $12 of advertising per order and $5,000 a month of fixed costs.

| | AOV $60 | AOV $66 |
|---|---|---|
| Product cost per order | $30 | $33 |
| Fulfilment and payment | $8 | $8 |
| Advertising | $12 | $12 |
| Left per order | $10 | $13 |
| Left from 800 orders | $8,000 | $10,400 |
| Less fixed costs | $5,000 | $5,000 |
| Monthly profit | $3,000 | $5,400 |
| Yearly profit | $36,000 | $64,800 |
| Value at the default 2.7x ecommerce midpoint | $97,200 | $174,960 |

A 10% rise in AOV lifted profit by 80% because fulfilment, advertising and fixed costs did not rise with it. The effect is that large only because costs stayed the same, so test it against real figures. It also assumes the same number of orders, and a higher price or an order-size threshold can lower conversion.

The 2.7x is {{BRAND}}'s default midpoint for ecommerce, its own assumption and not market data. See the [methodology](/methodology/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a higher AOV always better?

No. If the push for a higher AOV lowers conversion or needs heavy discounts, profit may not rise. Judge it by profit per order and total profit.

### Is AOV the same as customer lifetime value?

No. AOV is the average of one order. [LTV](/glossary/ltv/) adds up what a customer spends over time.

### What AOV should I show a buyer?

Show it by month for at least a year, by sales channel, and with the spread of order sizes, so a buyer can see what sits behind the average.
