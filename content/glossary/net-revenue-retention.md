## Formula

**NRR = (starting MRR + expansion − contraction − churned MRR) ÷ starting MRR**

- **Starting MRR**: recurring revenue from the customers you had at the start of the period.
- **Expansion**: extra revenue from those same customers through upgrades, added seats or add-ons.
- **Contraction**: revenue lost from downgrades.
- **Churned MRR**: revenue lost from cancellations.

Revenue from customers who joined during the period is left out. NRR is usually reported over 12 months.

## Example

A SaaS business starts the year with $50,000 of [MRR](/glossary/mrr/). Over the year, those customers add $4,000 in upgrades, downgrade by $1,000 and cancel plans worth $2,500.

**NRR = ($50,000 + $4,000 − $1,000 − $2,500) ÷ $50,000 = $50,500 ÷ $50,000 = 101%.**

The existing customer base is paying 1% more than it was a year ago, before counting any new customers.

## NRR vs gross revenue retention

**Gross revenue retention (GRR)** leaves out expansion: (starting MRR − contraction − churned MRR) ÷ starting MRR. In the example, GRR = $46,500 ÷ $50,000 = 93%. GRR can never be above 100%.

The two figures together tell a buyer more than either alone. An NRR above 100% with a low GRR means a few growing accounts are hiding a lot of cancellations.

## Why NRR matters when buying or selling

- **Above 100%**: the business grows even with no new customers. Buyers see this as durable revenue.
- **Below 100%**: new sales must first replace what was lost. The further below, the more a buyer will question the price.

NRR is most useful for products with seat-based or usage-based pricing, where customers can expand. A product with one flat plan has nothing to expand into, so its NRR will sit just below 100% and [churn](/glossary/churn/) tells the same story more simply.

For a small business, check how many customers produce the expansion. If one account's upgrade explains most of it, the figure says little about the rest.
