## How it works

Like financial debt, technical debt is a trade: the owner gets something now (a faster launch, a quick fix) and agrees to pay for it later. The "interest" is the extra time every future change takes because the code is harder to work with.

Common forms in small online businesses:

- **Outdated components**, such as a framework or plugin that no longer receives updates.
- **Quick fixes** left in place, with no tests and no notes on why they exist.
- **Knowledge held in one head**, where only the founder understands how parts of the product fit together.
- **Manual processes** that code should handle, such as billing corrections done by hand each month.

None of this stops a business earning money today. It shows up when someone new has to maintain or extend the product.

## Example

A buyer is looking at a SaaS product with **$48,000** of annual recurring revenue. In due diligence, a developer the buyer hired estimates that replacing an unsupported payments module will take **120 hours**. At **$75 an hour**, that is:

**Cost to fix = 120 hours × $75 = $9,000.**

The buyer asks for the price to come down by that amount, or for it to be held back until the work is done. The figures are invented to show the arithmetic; real estimates depend on the code.

## Why it matters in a valuation

{{BRAND}}'s valuation tools do not model code quality. The [methodology](/methodology/) lists technical quality among the things not modelled, so an estimate from the tools assumes a product a new owner can maintain without large rewrites. Buyers who open the code often price technical debt in one of three ways: a lower offer, a holdback released after the fix, or a longer [transition period](/glossary/transition-period/) with the seller explaining the system.

For a seller, a short document describing how the product is built, where it is hosted and what is known to be fragile makes the review quicker. For a buyer, the [SaaS buying guide](/guides/how-to-buy-a-saas-business/) explains what to ask for.
