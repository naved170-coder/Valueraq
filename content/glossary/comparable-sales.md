## How it works

The U.S. Small Business Administration describes the market approach to valuation as one that "compares your business to other similar businesses that have recently sold." Comparable sales are the raw material for it.

1. Find sales of businesses like yours: same type, similar size, similar way of earning.
2. Turn each price into a [multiple](/glossary/valuation-multiple/): price ÷ yearly profit, or price ÷ yearly revenue.
3. Apply the typical multiple from those sales to your own figures.

## Example

Three content websites sold recently:

| Site | Yearly profit | Price | Multiple |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | $40,000 | $120,000 | 3.0 |
| B | $25,000 | $85,000 | 3.4 |
| C | $60,000 | $168,000 | 2.8 |

The middle multiple is 3.0. A similar site with $50,000 of yearly profit would come out at about $50,000 × 3.0 = $150,000.

## Why it matters in a valuation

Comparable sales show what buyers actually paid, not what a model says they should pay. When good comparables exist, they are the most persuasive evidence in a negotiation.

## Why they are hard to use for small online businesses

- **Most sales are private.** Prices and profit figures are rarely published.
- **The figures behind a price differ.** One seller's "profit" includes their own pay, another's doesn't.
- **Terms change the price.** A sale paid partly later is not comparable with one paid in full at closing.
- **Few businesses are truly alike.** Traffic sources, age and owner workload all move the price.

This is why {{BRAND}} doesn't present its default multiples as market data. They are published assumptions, set out in the [methodology](/methodology/), and the tools show how each adjustment changes the result.

## Where comparables for online businesses come from

| Source | What you get | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Sold listings on marketplaces | Sale price and sometimes yearly profit | Listed profit may include or exclude the owner's pay |
| Benchmark reports from listing sites | A spread of multiples across many sales | They may lean toward larger, broker-sold businesses |
| Offers you receive | What real buyers will pay for yours | Not a sale until it closes |

One public benchmark page from BizBuySell describes "Data on website and ecommerce business sales from 2021 to 2025, covering 1,661 sold listings." It shows a spread, but covers one platform, so treat it as one input.

## Worked example: adjust before you compare

Suppose comparable site A sold for $120,000 on $40,000 of yearly profit, but that profit was before the owner's pay of $10,000. Your own profit figure is after the owner's pay, so the two are not comparable yet.

| Step | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Comp profit, same basis as yours | $40,000 − $10,000 | $30,000 |
| Comp multiple on that basis | $120,000 ÷ $30,000 | 4.0 |
| Your profit | given | $50,000 |
| Rough value from this one comp | $50,000 × 4.0 | $200,000 |

Compare that with the $150,000 from the unadjusted version above. The same sale gives a very different answer once the profit is put on the same basis. That is why a spread of several comps matters more than one.

## How to use comparables well

Use them as a check, not the only answer. Look at the spread rather than one sale, and compare like with like: profit before or after the owner's pay, and price paid in full or over time.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are comparable sales the same as a valuation multiple?

No. Comparable sales are the evidence. A [valuation multiple](/glossary/valuation-multiple/) is the number you work out from that evidence and then apply to your own figures.

### Why do my comps give a different answer from a calculator?

Calculators use set assumptions, and comps reflect specific deals. Use the [Website Valuation Tool](/tools/website-valuation/) for a starting range and comps as a check on it.
