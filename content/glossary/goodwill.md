## How it works

**Goodwill = purchase price − the value of identifiable net assets.** Identifiable net assets are the things that can be named and valued one by one, such as equipment, stock, cash and a registered trademark, minus the debts that come with them.

Whatever is left is goodwill. It stands for what the buyer is paying to get a business that already works: its reputation, loyal customers, steady traffic, supplier relationships and the know-how to run it. A business with a strong brand and a proven record of earning has more goodwill than one that has only just started.

Goodwill is not a cash item. It does not appear as money in the bank, and it only becomes visible when someone buys the business for more than its assets are worth.

## Example

A buyer agrees to pay $200,000 for an online store. The store's identifiable assets and debts on the day of sale are:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Inventory | $30,000 |
| Equipment | $5,000 |
| Cash handed over | $10,000 |
| Supplier bills outstanding | −$5,000 |
| **Identifiable net assets** | **$40,000** |

**Goodwill = $200,000 − $40,000 = $160,000.** So 80% of the price ($160,000 ÷ $200,000) is for the business as a going concern rather than for its stock and equipment.

## Why goodwill matters in a valuation

Most small digital businesses hold very little in physical assets, so most of what a buyer pays is goodwill. A website, a newsletter or a software product is mainly traffic, an audience, a brand and a stream of earnings. This is also why an asset-based valuation, which only counts the identifiable assets, tends to give a low number for them. The U.S. Small Business Administration describes the assets approach as one that "subtracts total business liabilities from the total value of all assets," and notes that intangible assets can include brand presence, intellectual property and customer information.

Goodwill rests on things that can disappear. If the owner is the brand, if one channel brings most of the customers or if profit is falling, the goodwill a buyer is willing to pay for shrinks. Buyers test it in [due diligence](/glossary/due-diligence/), and price protection such as an [earnout](/glossary/earnout/) can be used when the two sides disagree about how lasting it is.

How a purchase price is split between assets and goodwill can affect tax and the accounts of both sides, particularly in an [asset sale](/glossary/asset-sale/). Ask an accountant; this page does not give tax advice.
