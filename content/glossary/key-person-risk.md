## How it works

In many small online businesses one person writes the content, runs the ads, answers customers and holds the passwords. The business works well, but its income is tied to that person's time and knowledge. If they stop, so might the income.

Buyers look for signs of key person risk:

- **High weekly hours.** The more hours the owner puts in, the more work a buyer must replace.
- **Undocumented processes** that exist only in the owner's memory.
- **Personal relationships** with sponsors, suppliers or top customers.
- **A personal brand**, where the audience follows the founder, not the business.

## Example

A website earns **$60,000** a year in profit and the owner works **45 hours a week**. At {{BRAND}}'s default midpoint multiple for a website of 3.2x, the base value is:

**$60,000 × 3.2 = $192,000.**

In the default rules, an owner working over 40 hours a week lowers the multiple by 10%: 3.2 × 0.90 = 2.88. The adjusted value is:

**$60,000 × 2.88 = $172,800.**

That is $19,200 lower. If the owner cut their weekly hours to under 10 through documentation and hired help, the same rules would raise the multiple by 5% instead. The multiples are the website's own assumptions, not market data.

## Why it matters in a valuation

A buyer is paying for income they can keep. If that income needs the seller, the buyer carries the risk of the handover failing. Common answers are a longer [transition period](/glossary/transition-period/), an [earnout](/glossary/earnout/) tied to results after the sale, or a lower price. The rules behind the adjustment are in the [methodology](/methodology/#adjustments).

Sellers can reduce the risk before listing: write down the weekly routine, move accounts to shared or company-owned logins, train a helper, and introduce key partners to a second contact.
