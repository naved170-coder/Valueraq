## How it works

Operating income measures what the business itself earns, before anything to do with how it is financed or taxed.

**Operating income = gross profit − operating expenses**

Or, written from the top:

**Operating income = revenue − cost of goods sold − operating expenses**

[Gross profit](/glossary/gross-profit/) is revenue minus the direct cost of what was sold. [Operating expenses](/glossary/operating-expenses/) are the running costs such as advertising, software, contractors and depreciation. Interest, income taxes and one-off gains or losses outside the normal business are not part of operating income.

On most simple profit and loss statements, operating income is the same number as [EBIT](/glossary/ebit/). Some statements show EBIT after one-off items, so check how the statement defines it.

## Example

A hypothetical newsletter business had these results for the last twelve months.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Revenue | $120,000 |
| Cost of goods sold (paid tools and delivery) | $20,000 |
| Gross profit | $100,000 |
| Operating expenses | $55,000 |

**Operating income = $100,000 − $55,000 = $45,000.**

If it also paid $3,000 of loan interest and $7,000 of income tax, its net profit was $45,000 − $3,000 − $7,000 = $35,000. The operating income is higher than net profit because it comes before those two items. The figures are an illustration only, not market data.

## Why it matters in a valuation

Buyers look at operating income because it shows whether the core business makes money, separate from decisions the owner made about debt and tax. A business with a healthy operating income and a heavy loan can look weak at the bottom line, and a buyer who is not taking on that loan will want to see the earlier figure.

Some things to check:

- **Is the owner paid?** Operating income includes whatever the owner took as a salary and leaves out nothing for an unpaid owner. Compare it with [SDE](/glossary/sde/) and [EBITDA](/glossary/ebitda/) so you know which costs are inside each figure.
- **Are there one-off items?** A large one-time cost or gain inside operating expenses can distort the year. Ask what it was.
- **Does it match the records?** Check the figure against the statements, as shown in [how to read a profit and loss statement](/guides/how-to-read-a-profit-and-loss-statement/), and against bank or payment records.
