## How it works

When a buyer pays more for a business than its separately identifiable assets are worth, the extra amount is recorded as [goodwill](/glossary/goodwill/) on the buyer's [balance sheet](/glossary/balance-sheet/). Goodwill stands for things like reputation, customers and the ability to keep earning.

Goodwill is not cash and it is not used up like equipment. But if the business that created it starts earning much less than expected, the amount on the books may no longer be justified. When the buyer decides the business is worth less than the carrying amount, it records an impairment: a one-time charge that lowers goodwill and lowers reported profit for that period.

**Impairment charge = carrying amount of the business − its current value (limited to the goodwill recorded)**

The exact tests, and how often they apply, depend on the accounting rules a company follows and its size. Some smaller companies may spread goodwill out over time through [amortization](/glossary/amortization/) instead. Ask an accountant which rules apply to you.

## Example

An owner buys an online store for $200,000. Its identifiable net assets are $40,000, so $160,000 is recorded as goodwill. The figures are made up.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Price paid | $200,000 |
| Identifiable net assets | $40,000 |
| Goodwill recorded | $160,000 |

A year later a supplier problem cuts the store's profit sharply, and a review values the whole business at $120,000, while its carrying amount is $200,000.

| Step | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Shortfall | $200,000 − $120,000 | $80,000 |
| Goodwill after impairment | $160,000 − $80,000 | $80,000 |

The business records an $80,000 charge. No cash leaves the business at that moment, because the cash was spent when the store was bought. The charge only records that the purchase is now worth less.

## Why it matters in a valuation

Impairment is a signal. It tells a reader of the accounts that an earlier purchase did not perform as hoped.

- **When you buy:** if a seller has recorded an impairment on an acquisition, ask why. It may point to lost customers, a weaker brand or an overpayment, which are exactly the risks a buyer wants to understand before agreeing a price.
- **When you compare earnings:** an impairment lowers reported profit but does not use cash. Buyers usually add one-off non-cash charges back when they work out [normalized earnings](/glossary/normalized-earnings/), and should note the reason.
- **When you set a price:** a business that has lost value after a sale is a reminder to test the price against [fair market value](/glossary/fair-market-value/).
