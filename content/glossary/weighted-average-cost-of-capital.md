## How it works

A business is funded by debt (loans) and equity (owners' money). Each costs something: lenders want interest, owners want a return. WACC averages the two, weighted by the share of each:

**WACC = (equity share × cost of equity) + (debt share × cost of debt × (1 − tax rate))**

The tax term is there because interest is usually deductible, so debt costs less after tax. The Corporate Finance Institute notes that in a DCF the discount rate is "typically the weighted average cost of capital." That is WACC's main job: it gives the [discount rate](/glossary/discount-rate/) for valuing the whole business, for example when discounting [free cash flow to firm](/glossary/free-cash-flow-to-firm/).

## Example

A business is funded 70% by owners and 30% by a loan. Owners want a 20% return, the loan costs 8% and the tax rate is 25%. The figures are made up.

| Part | Calculation | Contribution |
|---|---|---|
| Equity | 0.70 × 20% | 14.0% |
| Debt, after tax | 0.30 × 8% × (1 − 0.25) = 0.30 × 6% | 1.8% |
| **WACC** | | **15.8%** |

A buyer using this rate would divide each future year's cash by 1.158 for one year away, 1.158² for two years, and so on. With less debt the rate rises, because owners' money costs more than debt.

## Why it matters in a valuation

A lower WACC gives a higher value and a higher WACC gives a lower one, so the rate is the biggest lever in a [discounted cash flow](/glossary/discounted-cash-flow/) model. For small online businesses WACC is rarely used. There is usually no market price for the owner's equity, debt is small or personal, and the owner's required return is a judgment about risk. Buyers more often choose a rate by risk, or skip it and use a [profit multiple](/glossary/profit-multiple/); the [capitalization rate](/glossary/capitalization-rate/) is the flat-income version. {{BRAND}} uses published default multiple ranges, as the [methodology](/methodology/) explains, and does not calculate WACC. See [net present value](/glossary/net-present-value/) for how a rate turns into a price check.
