## How it works

A success fee ties the payment to the result. If the sale does not close, nothing is owed under that fee. If it closes, the fee is paid, usually out of the proceeds at completion. A [business broker](/glossary/business-broker/), an adviser or a [marketplace](/glossary/marketplace/) may charge one.

The agreement should state:

- **The rate**: a flat percentage, or a sliding scale that changes with the price.
- **What it applies to**: the cash paid at closing only, or the whole price including deferred parts such as an [earnout](/glossary/earnout/).
- **When it is due**: at closing, or in stages as money arrives.
- **The tail**: how long after the contract ends the fee still applies if the business is sold to a buyer the adviser introduced.

A success fee is sometimes paired with an upfront or monthly fee. Ask whether one applies.

## Example

A seller agrees a success fee of 8% and sells a business for $250,000, of which $200,000 is paid at closing and $50,000 is an earnout paid a year later if targets are met.

- If the fee applies to the whole price: $250,000 × 8% = **$20,000**.
- If it applies only to cash at closing: $200,000 × 8% = **$16,000**.

The 8% is an invented rate for illustration. The difference of $4,000 comes from one line in the agreement, which is why it pays to check what the percentage is applied to.

## Why it matters when selling

A success fee lowers what the seller keeps, so compare offers on the amount received after fees, not on the headline price. It also aligns the adviser's interest with a completed sale, though it can also give them a reason to push for a quick close over the best price. A seller should keep an independent view of what the business is worth, for example from a free [valuation estimate](/tools/).

Fees differ widely between brokers and between marketplaces, and some platforms charge nothing to list. Read each service's own published terms rather than relying on a general figure.
