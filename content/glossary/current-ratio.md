## How it works

Current assets are cash and things expected to turn into cash within about a year, such as [accounts receivable](/glossary/accounts-receivable/) and stock for sale. Current liabilities are what the business must pay within about a year, such as [accounts payable](/glossary/accounts-payable/) and deferred revenue. Both come from the [balance sheet](/glossary/balance-sheet/).

**Current ratio = current assets ÷ current liabilities**

A ratio of 1.0 means the two are equal. Above 1.0, the business holds more short-term resources than short-term bills. Below 1.0, it owes more than it holds.

## Example

An online store's balance sheet shows the following. The figures are made up.

| Current assets | Amount | Current liabilities | Amount |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash | $22,000 | Supplier invoices | $16,000 |
| Customer receivables | $9,000 | Sales tax owed | $3,000 |
| Stock | $29,000 | Customer prepayments | $6,000 |
| **Total** | **$60,000** | **Total** | **$25,000** |

**Current ratio = $60,000 ÷ $25,000 = 2.4**

The store holds $2.40 of near-term resources for every $1 it owes soon. Its [working capital](/glossary/working-capital/) is $60,000 − $25,000 = $35,000.

## Why the current ratio matters in a valuation

- **It tests day-to-day safety.** A buyer wants to know the business can pay its bills after the sale without new cash.
- **It depends on what is counted.** Stock may be slow to sell. If $29,000 of stock took a year to sell, the real cushion is smaller. Look at cash and receivables on their own too.
- **A low ratio is not always bad.** Businesses that collect cash up front, such as subscriptions, may run below 1.0 because customer prepayments are liabilities. Read the ratio with [cash flow](/glossary/cash-flow/), not alone.
- **It guides the deal.** Working capital is often discussed in the sale contract, so know your ratio before negotiating.

There is no single "good" ratio that applies to every business. Compare a business with its own past figures and with similar businesses.
