## How it works

NPV answers one question: after allowing for the time it takes to get your money back and the risk, is this purchase worth its price? You convert each future year's cash into today's money with a [discount rate](/glossary/discount-rate/), add the results, then subtract what you pay now. This is the same method as [discounted cash flow](/glossary/discounted-cash-flow/), with the price taken off at the end. The Corporate Finance Institute describes the method as one "used to value investments by discounting the estimated future cash flows."

**NPV = (cash flow in year 1 ÷ (1 + rate)) + (year 2 ÷ (1 + rate)²) + ... − price paid**

A positive NPV means the investment earns more than the rate you chose. A negative NPV means it earns less.

## Example

A buyer is offered a small business for $90,000. They expect $40,000 of cash a year for three years and want a 25% yearly return. The figures are made up.

| Year | Cash flow | Calculation | Value today |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $40,000 | ÷ 1.25 | $32,000 |
| 2 | $40,000 | ÷ 1.5625 | $25,600 |
| 3 | $40,000 | ÷ 1.953 | $20,480 |
| **Total** | | | **$78,080** |

NPV = $78,080 − $90,000 = **−$11,920**. At a 25% required return the price is too high. At a 15% rate, the same cash is worth about $91,300 today (40,000 ÷ 1.15 + 40,000 ÷ 1.3225 + 40,000 ÷ 1.5209), so NPV turns slightly positive at about +$1,300. The decision flips on the rate alone.

## Why it matters in a valuation

NPV turns "is this a good price?" into a number a buyer can defend. It also shows how sensitive a price is to the buyer's chosen rate, which is why two buyers can value one business differently. For small online businesses, earnings are often too uneven to forecast for years, so many buyers use a [profit multiple](/glossary/profit-multiple/) instead, and {{BRAND}} uses published default multiple ranges as its [methodology](/methodology/) explains. NPV still helps as a cross-check on a price. See also [payback period](/glossary/payback-period/) for a simpler view.
