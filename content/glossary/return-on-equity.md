## How it works

**ROE = net income ÷ owners' equity**

[Net income](/glossary/net-income/) comes from the profit and loss statement for a period, usually a year. Owners' equity is what is left on the [balance sheet](/glossary/balance-sheet/) after liabilities are subtracted from assets, which is the same as the business's [book value](/glossary/book-value/). The result is shown as a percentage.

Many analysts use average equity over the year, the opening and closing figures added and divided by two, because equity changes during the period.

## Example

An online business earned net income of $60,000 last year. At the start of the year its equity was $180,000 and at the end it was $220,000.

- Average equity: ($180,000 + $220,000) ÷ 2 = $200,000
- **ROE = $60,000 ÷ $200,000 = 0.30, or 30%**

Now compare a second business with the same $60,000 of profit but $600,000 of equity. Its ROE is $60,000 ÷ $600,000 = 10%. The two earn the same profit, but the first uses far less capital to do it.

## Why it matters in a valuation

ROE shows how efficiently a business turns the owners' money into profit. Buyers of small online businesses often care about it for three reasons:

- **Asset-light businesses score high.** A site or software product needs little equity, so ROE can be very high. It says little on its own about quality.
- **Debt changes the picture.** Borrowing reduces equity and can push ROE up while adding risk. Check the [debt-to-equity ratio](/glossary/debt-to-equity-ratio/) beside it.
- **Equity can be small or negative.** If equity is close to zero, ROE becomes huge or meaningless. Small deals are usually priced on profit multiples instead.

For a comparison that ignores how the business is financed, see [return on investment](/glossary/return-on-investment/). VALUERAQ's default valuation uses profit or recurring revenue, not ROE, as explained in the [methodology](/methodology/).
