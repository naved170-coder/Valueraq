## How it works

RPM turns a site's income into a figure that can be compared from month to month and from site to site. Take the revenue for a period, divide it by the number of visits in the same period, and multiply by 1,000.

**RPM = revenue ÷ visits × 1,000**

Be clear about which revenue and which visits you count. Some ad networks report "page RPM", based on page views, while analytics tools count sessions. A page RPM and a session RPM for the same site can differ a lot, so a buyer will ask how yours is calculated. Use the same definition every time you compare.

## Example

A blog earns $4,200 in a month from ads and affiliate links and has 120,000 visits in the same month. The numbers below are an illustration, not a market figure.

- **RPM** = $4,200 ÷ 120,000 × 1,000 = **$35**

If traffic grows to 150,000 visits and RPM stays at $35, revenue becomes 150,000 ÷ 1,000 × $35 = $5,250. If RPM drops to $28 on the same 150,000 visits, revenue is only $4,200, so growth in traffic did not add income.

## Why it matters in a valuation

Buyers of content sites price on profit, so they want to know whether revenue will hold. RPM helps answer two questions:

- **Is the income steady?** A stable RPM over 12 months suggests the monetization is not luck. A sharp swing needs an explanation, such as a seasonal rise in ad rates.
- **Where could it grow?** A site with high traffic and a low RPM may have room to improve its layout or offers. A high RPM on thin traffic depends on a few pages.

RPM is not a price on its own. VALUERAQ's [website valuation tool](/tools/website-valuation/) multiplies annual profit by a multiple (its default range is 2.5x to 4.0x, explained in the [methodology](/methodology/)), so RPM is best used to check that the revenue behind the profit is sound.
