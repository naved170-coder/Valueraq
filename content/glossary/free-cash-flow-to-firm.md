## How it works

FCFF measures the cash a business produces for everyone who funds it, both lenders and owners, before either is paid. Because it ignores how the business is financed, it lets you compare two businesses with different amounts of debt.

A common way to calculate it:

**FCFF = EBIT × (1 − tax rate) + depreciation and amortization − capital spending − increase in working capital**

[EBIT](/glossary/ebit/) is earnings before interest and income taxes. The tax step applies the tax rate to that operating profit. Depreciation and amortization are added back because they are accounting charges, not cash leaving the business. Capital spending (equipment, software builds, other long-lived purchases) and any increase in [working capital](/glossary/working-capital/), such as more inventory, are subtracted because they use cash.

## Example

The figures are made up to show the arithmetic.

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| EBIT | $100,000 |
| Tax at 25% | −$25,000 |
| Operating profit after tax | $75,000 |
| Add back depreciation and amortization | +$10,000 |
| Capital spending | −$20,000 |
| Increase in working capital | −$5,000 |
| **FCFF** | **$60,000** |

Operating profit after tax is $75,000, but only $60,000 is free cash, because $25,000 went back into the business.

## Why it matters in a valuation

FCFF is the cash flow that goes into the enterprise version of a [discounted cash flow](/glossary/discounted-cash-flow/) model: each year's FCFF is converted to today's money and added up, which gives the value of the whole business before debt is taken off. That is the idea behind [enterprise value](/glossary/enterprise-value/).

The Corporate Finance Institute describes DCF as a method "used to value investments by discounting the estimated future cash flows," and notes the rate used is typically the weighted average cost of capital.

For small online businesses, FCFF is rarely used. Most sell on a multiple of yearly profit, and the owner's earnings, not a forecast of future cash, set the price. {{BRAND}}'s tools use multiples for that reason (see the [methodology](/methodology/)). FCFF becomes useful when a business spends heavily on equipment or inventory, because profit alone can hide that spending. Two businesses with equal profit can have very different free cash.
