## How it works

**Book value = total assets − total liabilities.** Assets are what the business owns, such as cash, stock, equipment and money owed to it. Liabilities are what it owes, such as supplier bills, loans and taxes due. Both figures come from the business's balance sheet. It is also called net book value or net asset value.

Two points to keep in mind. First, "recorded value" is an accounting figure, often based on what was paid for an asset less wear over time, not on what it would sell for today. Second, book value usually leaves out things the accounts do not record, such as a loyal audience, a well-known brand or a website that ranks well in search.

## Example

A small online business shows these figures on its balance sheet.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Cash | $15,000 |
| Inventory | $25,000 |
| Equipment and software | $8,000 |
| **Total assets** | **$48,000** |
| Supplier bills due | −$9,000 |
| Loan outstanding | −$6,000 |
| **Total liabilities** | **−$15,000** |

Book value is $48,000 − $15,000 = $33,000.

The same business earns $60,000 a year in profit. At a multiple of 3 times yearly profit, a buyer would pay $60,000 × 3 = $180,000. The difference between that price and the book value, $180,000 − $33,000 = $147,000, is [goodwill](/glossary/goodwill/).

### Book value, market value and fair market value

| Measure | What it tells you | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Book value | What the accounts say is left after debts | The balance sheet |
| Market value | What buyers are paying for similar businesses | Comparable sales and offers |
| Fair market value | What a willing buyer and seller would agree. The IRS describes it as "the price that property would sell for on the open market." | Appraisal or negotiation |

### An online business with almost no assets

A newsletter holds $4,000 in cash and $1,000 of software, so its assets are $5,000. It owes $1,000 to a contractor, so its book value is $5,000 − $1,000 = $4,000. It earns $30,000 a year in profit. At a multiple of 3 times profit, a buyer would pay $30,000 × 3 = $90,000, which is 22.5 times its book value ($90,000 ÷ $4,000). The rest is audience and earning power, which the balance sheet does not record.

## Why it matters in a valuation

Book value sets a floor for a business that holds a lot of stock and equipment, and it is the basis of the asset-based method of valuation. The U.S. Small Business Administration describes the assets approach as one that "subtracts total business liabilities from the total value of all assets." That is simple and easy to check, which is why it is used when a business is closing or has little profit.

For most websites, newsletters, apps and software businesses, book value is a poor guide. They hold little on the balance sheet and earn their value from future profit, so a buyer pays far more than the accounts show. Earnings-based methods, such as a profit multiple, fit them better. See [fair market value](/glossary/fair-market-value/) for the price a willing buyer and seller would agree.

Book value is still worth knowing. It tells a buyer how much of the price is backed by assets and how much depends on the business continuing to earn. In an [asset sale](/glossary/asset-sale/), the treatment of cash, stock and unpaid bills is usually agreed separately, as described under [working capital](/glossary/working-capital/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Can book value be negative?

Yes. If a business owes more than it owns, its book value is below zero.

### When is book value useful?

When a business holds much stock or equipment, is closing, or makes little profit. For earnings, see the [business valuation formula](/guides/business-valuation-formula/).
