## How it works

A multiple turns one figure about a business into a price:

**Value = base figure × multiple**

The base figure is usually one of three things:

- **Yearly profit**, most often [SDE](/glossary/sde/) for a small owner-run business. This gives a [profit multiple](/glossary/profit-multiple/).
- **Yearly revenue**, or annual recurring revenue for subscription software. This gives a [revenue multiple](/glossary/revenue-multiple/).
- **EBITDA**, for larger businesses with a management team. See [SDE vs EBITDA](/guides/sde-vs-ebitda/).

A multiple means nothing until you know which base figure it applies to. "Three times" profit and "three times" revenue are very different prices for the same business.

## Example

A content website keeps $4,000 a month after all costs, before the owner's own pay.

- Yearly profit: $4,000 × 12 = $48,000
- Multiple: 3.2 (the midpoint of {{BRAND}}'s default range for websites, before adjustments)
- **Value = $48,000 × 3.2 = $153,600**

The multiple also works in reverse. If the same site is listed at $180,000, the asking price is $180,000 ÷ $48,000 = **3.75 times** yearly profit. The [business multiple calculator](/calculators/business-multiple-calculator/) does this sum for any asking price.

## Why it matters in a valuation

The multiple is where a buyer's view of risk shows up. A business with a long history, steady growth and little need for its owner earns a higher multiple than one that is new, shrinking or dependent on one customer or traffic source. Two businesses with equal profit can therefore sell for very different prices.

{{BRAND}}'s tools start from a default range for each business type, for example 2.5 to 4.0 times yearly profit for a website, and adjust it with fixed rules. Both are published in the [methodology](/methodology/). They are editorial assumptions, not averages from a database of sales.

## Limits

A multiple is a shortcut. The Corporate Finance Institute points out that multiples compress complex information into a single value and reflect only one period of time. A multiple can't tell you whether the profit figure is accurate, whether last year was unusual, or what the business will earn under a new owner. Check the base figure first, then argue about the multiple.
