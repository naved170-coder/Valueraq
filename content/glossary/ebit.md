## How it works

EBIT stands for earnings before interest and taxes. It shows what the business earns from its operations, before the choices about how it is financed and how it is taxed.

**EBIT = revenue − cost of goods sold − operating expenses (including depreciation and amortization)**

You can also work backwards from the bottom line:

**EBIT = net profit + interest + income taxes**

The two routes give the same answer. Interest is left out because it depends on how much the owner borrowed, and taxes are left out because they depend on the owner's own tax position. What remains is a cleaner view of the operating business.

## Example

A hypothetical online store reports the following for the last twelve months.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Revenue | $400,000 |
| Cost of goods sold | $200,000 |
| Operating expenses (ads, software, contractors) | $110,000 |
| Depreciation of equipment | $10,000 |

**EBIT = $400,000 − $200,000 − $110,000 − $10,000 = $80,000.**

If the store paid $5,000 of interest on a loan and $15,000 of income tax, its net profit was $80,000 − $5,000 − $15,000 = $60,000. Adding the interest and tax back to $60,000 returns the same $80,000. The figures are an illustration only, not market data.

## Why it matters in a valuation

EBIT sits between two figures buyers often meet. [EBITDA](/glossary/ebitda/) adds depreciation and amortization back to EBIT, so EBITDA is always at least as high as EBIT. In the example, EBITDA would be $80,000 + $10,000 = $90,000. [SDE](/glossary/sde/) goes the other way for owner-run businesses: it also adds back the owner's pay and personal costs.

A few things to keep in mind:

- **Which profit has the multiple been applied to?** A multiple of EBIT is not comparable with a multiple of SDE or EBITDA. Always check which figure a quoted multiple refers to.
- **EBIT counts the owner's pay only if the owner is paid.** If the owner takes no salary, EBIT can look larger than the business would earn under a hired manager.
- **Depreciation is a real cost over time.** Leaving it in, as EBIT does, is more cautious than EBITDA for a business that must replace equipment.

For smaller online businesses, buyers more often see SDE. See [SDE vs EBITDA](/guides/sde-vs-ebitda/) for how to choose between them, and [net profit](/glossary/net-profit/) for the figure after interest and tax.
