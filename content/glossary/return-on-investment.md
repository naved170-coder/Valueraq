## How it works

**ROI = (gain − cost) ÷ cost × 100**

If you spend $1,000 and get back $1,300, the gain is $300 and the ROI is 30%. The result depends on the period you measure, so state it: 30% over one year is a different thing from 30% over five.

For a business purchase, "gain" usually means the profit the business produces. When the profit is measured per year, the annual ROI is a simple division of that profit by the price paid.

## Example

A hypothetical buyer pays $120,000 for a website that earns $40,000 of net profit a year, after the cost of any help the buyer must hire.

**Annual ROI = $40,000 ÷ $120,000 × 100 = 33.3%.**

The price is 3 times annual profit, and the ROI is the same fact upside down: 1 ÷ 3 = 33.3%. Over three years, profit of $40,000 × 3 = $120,000 would repay the price. That is the [payback period](/glossary/payback-period/), three years. The figures are an illustration only. They do not predict results, and earnings can rise or fall after a sale.

## Why it matters in a valuation

ROI gives a buyer a way to compare very different purchases, such as a website, a store and an app, on the same scale. It also connects directly to the [profit multiple](/glossary/profit-multiple/): a higher multiple means a lower annual ROI for the same profit.

Some points to keep in mind.

- **Use the right profit.** If the figure is [SDE](/glossary/sde/), it leaves out the owner's pay. A buyer who must hire someone should deduct that cost first.
- **Count every cost of buying.** Fees, stock, transition help and any money needed to fix problems all belong in the cost side.
- **ROI does not show risk.** Two businesses can show the same ROI while one depends on a single traffic source or a single customer. Compare the risks as well as the percentage.
- **It looks backwards.** The profit used is usually from the past twelve months. It is not a forecast.
