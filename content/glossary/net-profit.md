## How it works

**Net profit = revenue − all costs.** The costs include the cost of goods or services sold, advertising, software, contractors, fees, interest and taxes. What remains is the net profit, also called net income or the bottom line.

[Gross margin](/glossary/gross-margin/) stops earlier: it deducts only the direct cost of making or delivering the product. Net profit goes all the way down.

## Example

A website earns $120,000 in a year.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Revenue | $120,000 |
| Hosting and software | −$6,000 |
| Writers and contractors | −$30,000 |
| Advertising | −$14,000 |
| Payment and platform fees | −$4,000 |
| **Net profit** | **$66,000** |

Net profit is $120,000 − $54,000 = $66,000, or 55% of revenue.

## Why it matters in a valuation

Most small digital businesses are priced as yearly profit multiplied by a [multiple](/glossary/profit-multiple/), so the profit figure decides the price. Buyers rarely use net profit as it stands. They adjust it:

- **For the owner.** If the owner takes no pay, a buyer must pay someone to do the work. Profit before the owner's pay is called [SDE](/glossary/sde/).
- **For one-off or personal costs.** Valid [add-backs](/glossary/add-backs/) raise the figure; BizBuySell warns that "some add-backs can unrealistically overvalue the company."
- **For tax and interest.** Profit before interest, tax, depreciation and amortization is [EBITDA](/glossary/ebitda/).

Because tax rules differ by country, check with an accountant how your profit is calculated for tax before comparing it with a listing.

## Net profit vs revenue

Revenue is the money coming in. Net profit is what stays. Two businesses with the same $120,000 of revenue can have very different net profit: one might keep $66,000 and the other $20,000, because their costs differ. That is why buyers of small digital businesses ask for profit records and not only sales figures, and why a listing should show both.
