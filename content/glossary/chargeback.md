## How it works

A customer who does not recognize a charge, never received an order, or is unhappy with it can ask the card issuer to reverse it instead of asking the seller for a refund. The bank then asks the seller's payment processor for the money back. The seller can reply with evidence, such as proof of delivery, order records or messages, and the issuer decides who wins.

The money usually leaves the seller's balance at the start of the dispute. If the seller loses, it does not come back, and the processor may add a fee for the dispute. A refund is different: the seller chooses to send the money back, and no dispute process is involved.

The **chargeback rate** is chargebacks divided by the number of payments in the same period. Payment processors watch it, and a high rate can lead to extra checks or to the account being limited. The exact limits differ by processor, so read your own processor's rules.

## Example

An online store takes 2,000 card payments in a quarter and receives 10 disputes. The chargeback rate is 10 ÷ 2,000 = 0.5%.

Say the average order is $60 and the dispute fee is $15 (an assumed figure for this example). Losing all 10 costs 10 × ($60 + $15) = $750, plus the cost of the goods that were already shipped. If the store has a net profit of $12,000 a quarter, the direct loss is about 6% of that profit ($750 ÷ $12,000). The figures are an illustration, not a benchmark.

**Chargeback rate = chargebacks ÷ card payments in the period × 100.**

## Chargeback or refund?

| | Refund | Chargeback |
|---|---|---|
| Who starts it | The seller | The customer, through their bank |
| Fee | Usually none beyond fees already paid | Often a dispute fee |
| Seller can contest | Not applicable | Yes, with evidence |
| Effect on the account | None | Counted by the processor |

## Why it matters in a valuation

A buyer who looks at a store or a software subscription business will ask for processor reports and the dispute history for the last 12 months or more. Chargebacks matter in three ways:

- **Profit.** Disputed sales that are lost should be counted as a cost. If the seller's profit figure leaves them out, [net profit](/glossary/net-profit/) is overstated.
- **Account risk.** A processor that limits or closes the account can stop sales on the day of a handover. Buyers want to know the account is in good standing and whether it can be moved.
- **Signs of trouble.** A rising rate can point to product quality, unclear billing descriptions, fraud or poor customer service.

Sellers can help their case by keeping order records, delivery proof and customer messages in one place. See [how to verify website revenue](/guides/how-to-verify-website-revenue/) and the [ecommerce valuation guide](/guides/ecommerce-valuation/).

## What a buyer checks

- Processor reports of disputes by month for the last 12 months or more.
- How many disputes were won and how many lost.
- Whether lost disputes are inside the profit figure.
- Any warning or limit the processor has placed on the account.
- Whether the billing name on card statements is one customers recognize.

## How sellers lower chargebacks

Use a clear billing descriptor, send order and shipping confirmations, answer customer messages quickly, and keep delivery proof. These steps also make a dispute easier to win.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I win a chargeback?

Sometimes. You send evidence to your processor, and the cardholder's bank decides. Records of delivery, order and messages help.

### Do chargebacks affect what my store sells for?

They can. They reduce profit, and a high or rising rate suggests account risk and customer problems, so buyers may offer less.

### Is a chargeback the same as fraud?

No. Some are true fraud, some are mistakes, and some are disputes about the product. The processor report shows the reason given.
