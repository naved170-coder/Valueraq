## How it works

Reported profit is shaped by events that may not repeat and by choices the current owner made. Normalizing removes that noise. You start from net profit and adjust in both directions:

- **Add back one-off costs**, such as a one-time legal bill or a failed product launch.
- **Add back owner-specific costs**, such as personal expenses run through the business.
- **Remove one-off gains**, such as a single unusually large order that is unlikely to repeat.
- **Adjust for pay that is too low or too high**, so any hired role is paid a fair rate.

The result is the profit a buyer can reasonably expect in a normal year. Sellers who also add back their own pay get [SDE](/glossary/sde/). Buyers who charge a fair salary for the owner's job get an [EBITDA](/glossary/ebitda/)-style figure.

## Example

A store reports $60,000 net profit for the year.

- Add back a one-time legal bill: **+$4,000**.
- Add back the owner's personal phone paid by the business: **+$1,200**.
- Remove a one-off bulk order that will not repeat: **−$6,000**.

**Normalized earnings = $60,000 + $4,000 + $1,200 − $6,000 = $59,200.**

At the midpoint ecommerce multiple of 2.7 in {{BRAND}}'s default range, that is $59,200 × 2.7 = $159,840 for the business. Had the one-off order been left in, the figure would be $6,000 × 2.7 = $16,200 too high. The default range is a platform assumption, not market data.

## Why normalized earnings matter in a valuation

A multiple only means something when it is applied to a profit figure everyone agrees is typical. Sellers like to adjust upward and buyers adjust downward, so the number gets argued over. The best defense is a list with a receipt or contract for each line.

BizBuySell warns that [some add-backs can unrealistically overvalue the company](https://www.bizbuysell.com/learning-center/article/add-backs-sde/). Adjustments that are real, documented and clearly one-off are usually accepted. Normal advertising, regular wages and recurring software costs relabeled as "growth" usually are not.
