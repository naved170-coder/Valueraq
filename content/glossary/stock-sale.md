## How it works

Many online businesses are run through a company. In a stock sale, the seller does not hand over the website, the app or the store account piece by piece. The seller transfers the shares of the company that owns them, and the buyer becomes the new owner of that company.

Because the company stays the same legal entity, almost everything stays where it is:

- Domains, code, brand names and content remain owned by the company.
- Bank accounts, payment processor accounts and ad accounts stay in the company's name.
- Contracts with suppliers, advertisers and customers continue without being re-signed, unless a contract says a change of owner needs consent.
- Past debts, tax matters and legal claims also stay inside the company, which the buyer now owns.

The [purchase agreement](/glossary/purchase-agreement/) sets out the price, the number of shares sold and the statements the seller makes about the company.

## Example

A seller owns 100% of a company that runs a newsletter business. The business earns $120,000 a year in profit and the parties agree a price of $360,000.

**Price = $360,000 for 100% of the shares.**

A buyer who wanted only 60% of the company would pay a share of that value, **$360,000 × 60% = $216,000**, and the seller would stay as a part owner. A minority share is usually priced differently, since the holder cannot control decisions, so this is only the simple arithmetic.

## Why it matters in a valuation

A stock sale does not change what the business earns, but it changes what the buyer is taking on. In an [asset sale](/glossary/asset-sale/), the buyer picks the assets and usually leaves past liabilities behind. In a stock sale the buyer inherits the company as it is, including anything unknown. For that reason buyers often carry out deeper [due diligence](/glossary/due-diligence/) before a stock sale, and may ask for a [holdback](/glossary/holdback/) or [escrow](/glossary/escrow/) to cover claims.

The structure can also change how a deal is taxed for each side. That varies by country and by business structure, so the same headline price can leave different amounts after tax.

Most small websites, apps and stores are sold as asset sales. A stock sale is more common when the company has many contracts, employees or licences that are hard to transfer one by one.
