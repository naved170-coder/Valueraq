## How it works

**Bounce rate = bounced sessions ÷ total sessions × 100**

A session is one visit. What counts as a "bounce" depends on the analytics tool, and this is where most confusion comes from.

- **Older definition (Universal Analytics):** a visit that viewed only one page and did nothing else that the tool recorded.
- **Google Analytics 4:** bounce rate is the share of sessions that were not engaged, and engagement rate is its opposite. A session is normally counted as engaged when it lasts long enough, includes a conversion event or views more than one page. Check Google's Analytics Help for the current thresholds, since the tool can change them.

The same site can therefore show quite different bounce rates in two tools. Compare figures only from the same tool and the same definition.

## Example

A hypothetical blog had 8,000 sessions last month. Of these, 3,200 were not engaged.

**Bounce rate = 3,200 ÷ 8,000 × 100 = 40%.**

Engagement rate is the other side: 100% − 40% = 60%. If the next month brings 10,000 sessions with 5,500 not engaged, bounce rate is 55%. Whether that is a problem depends on why it moved. The figures are an illustration only, not a benchmark.

## Why it matters in a valuation

Buyers of websites look at bounce rate as a clue, not as a price input. [VALUERAQ's tools](/guides/website-valuation/) value a site on its profit, not on its bounce rate. But a buyer reading a traffic report may ask:

- **Is the page type the reason?** A page that answers a question in one screen, such as a definition, can have a high bounce rate and still do its job. A store with a high bounce rate on product pages may have a problem.
- **Did it change suddenly?** A jump can come from a new traffic source, a tracking change or a change in the tool's definition, as well as a drop in quality.
- **Is the traffic real?** Very high or very low bounce rates can point to bots or broken tracking. See [how to verify website traffic](/guides/how-to-verify-website-traffic/).
- **How does it link to sales?** Read bounce rate next to [conversion rate](/glossary/conversion-rate/) and the source of [traffic](/glossary/traffic/). A low bounce rate that never turns into sales is worth less than it looks.

Avoid quoting a "good" bounce rate. Without a published source, any single benchmark is a guess, because the right level varies with the type of page and the tool.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a high bounce rate bad?

Not always. It is a problem when visitors were meant to go on to another page or buy and did not. For a page that answers a question in full, leaving after reading it can be a good result.

### Why did my bounce rate change when I switched to Google Analytics 4?

Because GA4 defines it as the share of sessions that were not engaged, which is different from the old single-page-visit rule. The new number is not directly comparable with older reports.
