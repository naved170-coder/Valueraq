## How it works

Most spending is an [operating expense](/glossary/operating-expenses/): rent, software subscriptions, ads, supplies. It is used up now and counted in the same period. CapEx is different. It buys something that will help the business for more than a year, so the cost is recorded as an asset and spread over its life through [depreciation](/glossary/depreciation/).

**CapEx = spending on long-lasting assets in the period**

You can often find it on the [cash flow statement](/glossary/cash-flow-statement/), in the investing section. Examples are a warehouse shelf, a new server, a camera for a video channel, or the cost of building an app feature that will be used for years.

## Example

An online store has $60,000 of yearly profit before depreciation. During the year it buys a $9,000 packing machine and pays a developer $6,000 to build a new checkout feature. The figures are made up.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Profit before depreciation | $60,000 |
| CapEx: packing machine | −$9,000 |
| CapEx: checkout feature | −$6,000 |
| **Cash left after CapEx** | **$45,000** |

CapEx for the year is $9,000 + $6,000 = $15,000, or $15,000 ÷ $60,000 = 25% of that profit. If the machine lasts five years, depreciation is $9,000 ÷ 5 = $1,800 a year.

## Why CapEx matters in a valuation

- **It shows the true cash profit.** Measures such as [EBITDA](/glossary/ebitda/) add depreciation back and ignore CapEx. A business that must keep buying equipment keeps less cash than its EBITDA suggests.
- **It separates one-off from regular.** A single large purchase may not repeat. Regular replacement is a cost the next owner will carry.
- **Light businesses need less.** Many websites, newsletters and software businesses spend little on CapEx, one reason buyers find them attractive.
- **It supports free cash flow.** See [free cash flow to firm](/glossary/free-cash-flow-to-firm/), which subtracts CapEx from operating cash.
- **Buyers will ask.** Expect questions on what was bought, when, and what must be replaced soon.
