## How it works

The formula is short:

**Value = annual recurring revenue (ARR) × multiple.**

If you track monthly figures, [MRR](/glossary/mrr/) × 12 gives [ARR](/glossary/arr/). Only revenue that repeats counts. One-off setup fees, consulting and single sales are left out, as the [recurring revenue](/glossary/recurring-revenue/) entry explains.

Recurring revenue is priced as a multiple of revenue rather than profit because many subscription businesses reinvest heavily in growth, so profit understates what the customer base is worth. Buyers still check profit as a sanity test.

## Example

A software product has $10,000 in MRR.

- **ARR:** $10,000 × 12 = $120,000.
- **At 2.0 times:** $120,000 × 2.0 = $240,000.
- **At 3.5 times:** $120,000 × 3.5 = $420,000.
- **At 5.0 times:** $120,000 × 5.0 = $600,000.

Those three multiples are {{BRAND}}'s default range for a SaaS business: 2.0 low, 3.5 midpoint, 5.0 high. They are platform assumptions, not market data, and the [methodology](/methodology/) explains them.

## Why it matters in a valuation

Two businesses with the same ARR can deserve very different multiples. What usually pushes the number up or down:

| Factor | Pushes the multiple up | Pushes it down |
|---|---|---|
| Customer churn | Low, stable | High or rising |
| [Net revenue retention](/glossary/net-revenue-retention/) | Customers spend more over time | Customers shrink or leave |
| Growth | Steady and explained | Flat or falling |
| Concentration | Many small customers | One customer is a large share |
| Owner dependence | Runs without the founder | Founder does the key work |

The multiple is a starting point for negotiation, not a verdict. A buyer will usually ask for a customer list with start dates, monthly revenue by customer and a record of cancellations, then check that the ARR you quote matches payments received. If the figures do not tie, expect the multiple, or the price, to come down. Sellers who prepare these records before listing usually negotiate from a stronger position.
