## How it works

The store lists products and takes payment. When an order arrives, the seller passes it to a supplier, pays the supplier's price, and the supplier packs and ships the item straight to the customer. The seller never touches the product.

That has two effects. The seller needs little money up front, because there is no stock to buy. But the seller also controls less: the supplier decides price changes, stock levels, packing quality and shipping speed.

In the accounts, the supplier's price and shipping are the [cost of goods sold](/glossary/cost-of-goods-sold/). What is left from each sale is [gross margin](/glossary/gross-margin/), and advertising, platform fees and software come out of that.

## Example

Take a store with $30,000 of monthly revenue and $4,000 of monthly net profit, so $48,000 a year ($4,000 × 12).

Using {{BRAND}}'s default ecommerce range of 2.0 to 3.5 times yearly profit, the midpoint is 2.7. The default dropshipping adjustment is −8%, which gives 2.7 × 0.92 = 2.48 times. The estimated midpoint value is 2.48 × $48,000 = $119,040, shown as about $119,000.

The same store with the same profit and its own warehouse stays at 2.7 times: 2.7 × $48,000 = $129,600, about $130,000. The example is an illustration, not market data.

## Why it matters in a valuation

A buyer of a dropshipping store is buying a customer-facing brand and a set of supplier relationships it does not own. If a supplier raises prices, runs out of stock or stops trading, profit can fall quickly. That is why {{BRAND}}'s default range takes 8% off the multiple. See the [ecommerce valuation guide](/guides/ecommerce-valuation/) for every adjustment, and the [methodology](/methodology/) page for how defaults are set.

Sellers can narrow the gap by showing more than one supplier for the main products, written terms with each, and a record of delivery times and refunds. Buyers should ask for those during [due diligence](/guides/digital-business-due-diligence/).
