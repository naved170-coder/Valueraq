## How it works

When one company owns more than half of another, it controls it and reports the subsidiary's results in full on its own accounts, even if it owns less than 100%. The part owned by others is shown separately as minority interest, also called non-controlling interest. It appears on the [balance sheet](/glossary/balance-sheet/) as the outside owners' share of the subsidiary's equity, and in the income statement as the share of profit that belongs to them.

The word "minority" refers to the outside owners' share being less than half. It does not mean the owners are small.

## Example

A parent company owns 80% of a subsidiary that runs an online store. The store earns $100,000 of [net income](/glossary/net-income/) in the year. The figures are made up.

- Reported in full in the group accounts: **$100,000**
- Minority interest share: 20% × $100,000 = **$20,000**
- Share that belongs to the parent: 80% × $100,000 = **$80,000**

The same split applies to the subsidiary's net assets. If they are worth $500,000, the minority interest on the balance sheet is 20% × $500,000 = $100,000.

## Why it matters in a valuation

A group's figures include 100% of the subsidiary, but the parent's owners only own part of it. To compare like with like, valuers add minority interest when they work out [enterprise value](/glossary/enterprise-value/), because enterprise value covers everything the group controls. They then subtract it again when moving from enterprise value to the value that belongs to the parent's own owners ([equity value](/glossary/equity-value/)).

For a small online business the term matters when someone sells a share rather than the whole thing. If you buy 60% and a partner keeps 40%, the partner holds a minority stake in your business. Ask who controls decisions, how profit is shared and what happens if the partner wants to sell, and have the terms written into the agreement.
