## How it works

The Corporate Finance Institute describes revenue run rate as a measure that takes "current revenue in a certain period (a week, month, quarter, etc.) and converts it to an annual figure."

The simple versions are:

- **Monthly figure × 12**
- **Quarterly figure × 4**

Run rate looks forward from the latest period. [Trailing twelve months](/glossary/trailing-twelve-months/) looks back over a full year of real results.

## Example

An app earned $8,000 in September.

**Run rate = $8,000 × 12 = $96,000 a year**

Over the last 12 months it actually earned $62,000, because it was much smaller a year ago. Both numbers are true. They answer different questions: $62,000 is what happened, and $96,000 is what will happen if every coming month matches September.

## Why it matters in a valuation

A [multiple](/glossary/valuation-multiple/) applied to the run rate gives a higher price than the same multiple applied to the last 12 months whenever a business is growing. On the example above, at three times yearly earnings, the difference is $288,000 against $186,000.

Sellers of fast-growing businesses therefore quote run rate, and buyers prefer trailing figures. A common middle ground is to value on trailing results and pay extra later if the growth holds, through an [earnout](/glossary/earnout/).

For subscription software, [ARR](/glossary/arr/) is a kind of run rate: current [MRR](/glossary/mrr/) × 12. It is more dependable than most, because the revenue is contracted to repeat. It still assumes customers don't cancel.

## The risks

The Corporate Finance Institute lists three:

- **It assumes nothing changes.** Run rate takes for granted that conditions stay as they are.
- **Seasons distort it.** A store's December multiplied by 12 says little about its year.
- **One-off events get multiplied.** A launch, a promotion or a single large order becomes 12 of them.

Two checks help. Ask for every month of the last year, not the latest one. Then ask what happened in the chosen month that might not repeat.
