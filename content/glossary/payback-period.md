## How it works

**Payback period = amount invested ÷ cash earned per year** (or per month, to get months).

It answers a plain question: how long until I have my money back? It uses cash earned, not revenue, and it ignores anything that happens after the money is recovered.

Two uses come up often:

- **For a buyer:** the price of the business divided by its yearly profit.
- **For customer acquisition:** the cost of winning a customer ([CAC](/glossary/cac/)) divided by the monthly gross profit that customer brings in.

## Example

A buyer pays $194,400 for an ecommerce business that earns $72,000 a year in profit.

- **Payback period:** $194,400 ÷ $72,000 = 2.7 years.

That equals the 2.7 multiple used to set the price, because a profit multiple and a payback period are the same calculation. A buyer paying 3.5 times profit expects a 3.5-year payback if profit stays flat.

For customer acquisition: if it costs $300 to win a customer who brings in $50 of gross profit a month, the payback is $300 ÷ $50 = 6 months.

## Why it matters in a valuation

Payback period turns a price into a time frame a buyer can judge. It also shows the limit of the method: it assumes profit stays the same, and it says nothing about what the business earns after payback or what it could be resold for. A business with falling profit takes longer to pay back than the simple sum suggests.

Use it as a quick check next to a [valuation multiple](/glossary/valuation-multiple/), not as the only measure. A short payback period is attractive, but only if the profit behind it is real, so a buyer should check the records before trusting the sum.

## Related terms

A [profit multiple](/glossary/profit-multiple/) is the price divided by yearly profit. [Customer lifetime value](/glossary/ltv/) looks at the whole relationship with a customer rather than the time to recover the cost of winning them.
