## Formula

**EBITDA = net profit + interest + taxes + depreciation + amortization**

or, starting from the top:

**EBITDA = revenue − operating expenses (including market-rate salaries), excluding depreciation and amortization**

## Example

An online business reports:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Net profit | $180,000 |
| Interest on a loan | $10,000 |
| Income taxes | $45,000 |
| Depreciation of equipment | $5,000 |
| Amortization of acquired software | $20,000 |

**EBITDA = $180,000 + $10,000 + $45,000 + $5,000 + $20,000 = $260,000.**

## EBITDA vs SDE

The key difference is the owner's pay. [SDE](/glossary/sde/) adds back the owner's salary and personal benefits, because a buyer of an owner-run business will do that work themselves. EBITDA assumes everyone, including management, is paid a market-rate salary.

Use **SDE** for small businesses where one owner does most of the work. Use **EBITDA** for businesses with a management team, where a buyer expects to own rather than operate.

## Why EBITDA matters for valuation

Larger businesses are often valued as a multiple of EBITDA. Because EBITDA is after management salaries, EBITDA multiples are not directly comparable with SDE multiples for the same business. {{BRAND}}'s tools use SDE, which suits the owner-run businesses most of them are built for.

## Limitations

EBITDA ignores capital spending and changes in working capital, so it can make a business that needs constant reinvestment look more profitable than the cash it produces.
