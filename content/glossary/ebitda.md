## Formula

**EBITDA = net profit + interest + taxes + depreciation + amortization**

or, starting from the top:

**EBITDA = revenue − operating expenses (including market-rate salaries), excluding depreciation and amortization**

## Example

An online business reports:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Net profit | $180,000 |
| Interest on a loan | $10,000 |
| Income taxes | $45,000 |
| Depreciation of equipment | $5,000 |
| Amortization of acquired software | $20,000 |

**EBITDA = $180,000 + $10,000 + $45,000 + $5,000 + $20,000 = $260,000.**

## EBITDA vs SDE

The key difference is the owner's pay. [SDE](/glossary/sde/) adds back the owner's salary and personal benefits, because a buyer of an owner-run business will do that work themselves. EBITDA assumes everyone, including management, is paid a market-rate salary.

Use **SDE** for small businesses where one owner does most of the work. Use **EBITDA** for businesses with a management team, where a buyer expects to own rather than operate.

## Adjusted EBITDA example

Buyers often adjust EBITDA for one-off items so it reflects normal earnings. An online store has EBITDA of $260,000, as above. Its books also show a one-time legal bill of $12,000 and a founder salary of $20,000, where a market-rate manager would cost $60,000.

- One-off legal bill added back: +$12,000
- Salary raised to market rate: −$40,000 ($60,000 − $20,000)
- **Adjusted EBITDA = $260,000 + $12,000 − $40,000 = $232,000**

The adjusted figure is lower, because a buyer must pay a manager a fair wage. Always check which version a quoted multiple uses.

## When do buyers use EBITDA instead of SDE?

There is no fixed rule, but a pattern. If the owner does most of the work and a buyer will step into that job, [SDE](/glossary/sde/) is the usual measure. If the business has managers and the buyer will own it rather than run it, EBITDA is more common. A buyer may ask for both. Sellers should state which figure they are quoting.

## Why EBITDA matters for valuation

Larger businesses are often valued as a multiple of EBITDA. Because EBITDA is after management salaries, EBITDA multiples are not directly comparable with SDE multiples for the same business. {{BRAND}}'s tools use SDE, which suits the owner-run businesses most of them are built for.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is EBITDA the same as profit?

No. EBITDA leaves out interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, so it is usually higher than net profit.

### Is EBITDA the same as cash flow?

No. It ignores spending on equipment and changes in working capital, so cash can be lower than EBITDA suggests.

### Which should a small online seller quote, SDE or EBITDA?

SDE, if you run the business yourself. If a manager runs it day to day, show EBITDA too. See the [profit multiple](/glossary/profit-multiple/) page for how multiples are applied.

## Limitations

EBITDA ignores capital spending and changes in working capital, so it can make a business that needs constant reinvestment look more profitable than the cash it produces.
