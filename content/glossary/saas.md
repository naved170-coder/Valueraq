## How the SaaS business model works

The provider hosts the software, maintains it and releases updates. Customers pay a recurring fee, usually monthly or annually, for access. Pricing may be per user, per usage tier, or flat.

Because customers keep paying as long as the product is useful to them, SaaS revenue repeats, which is its main economic attraction. The trade-off is that revenue has to be re-earned continually through product quality and support.

## Key SaaS metrics

| Metric | What it shows |
|---|---|
| [MRR](/glossary/mrr/) / [ARR](/glossary/arr/) | Size of the recurring revenue base |
| Growth rate | How fast recurring revenue is increasing |
| [Churn](/glossary/churn/) | How much revenue or how many customers are lost |
| [Gross margin](/glossary/gross-margin/) | Revenue left after hosting and direct costs |
| [CAC](/glossary/cac/) and [LTV](/glossary/ltv/) | Cost of winning customers against their value |

## Example

A tool that helps small restaurants manage bookings charges $49 a month. With 300 paying restaurants, it has $14,700 MRR and $176,400 ARR. If 2% cancel each month, it loses about 6 customers a month and must win at least that many to stay level.

## How SaaS businesses are valued

Small SaaS businesses are usually valued as a multiple of ARR or annual profit, with the multiple rising for faster growth, lower churn and higher margins. See [how to value a SaaS business](/guides/saas-valuation/) or use the [SaaS Valuation Tool](/tools/saas-valuation/).

## SaaS vs other online businesses

SaaS differs from content websites and online stores mainly in the predictability of its revenue and in what a buyer takes on: code, infrastructure and ongoing support obligations. See [website vs SaaS valuation](/guides/website-vs-saas-valuation/).
