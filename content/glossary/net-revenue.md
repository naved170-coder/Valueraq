## How it works

The formula is:

**Net revenue = gross revenue − refunds − returns − discounts and allowances.**

Gross revenue is every sale at its full price before anything is taken off. Net revenue is what is left once the sales that were reversed or discounted are removed. It is still a sales figure: costs such as stock, ads and software have not been subtracted yet, so it sits above [gross profit](/glossary/gross-profit/) and [net profit](/glossary/net-profit/).

Be careful with the word "net". Some businesses say "net revenue" to mean sales after payment fees or marketplace fees as well. Whichever meaning is used, state it and keep it the same in every month.

## Example

An online store reports one month of figures. The numbers are made up to show the arithmetic.

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Gross revenue | $20,000 |
| Refunds | −$1,200 |
| Returns | −$600 |
| Discounts and allowances | −$400 |
| **Net revenue** | **$17,800** |

The arithmetic is $20,000 − $1,200 − $600 − $400 = $17,800. The store lost 11% of its gross sales to reversals and discounts ($2,200 ÷ $20,000).

## Why it matters in a valuation

A buyer who prices a business on revenue should use net revenue. If a listing quotes gross sales while 11% of them come back as refunds, a revenue multiple applied to the gross figure overstates the price. The same multiple applied to $20,000 and to $17,800 gives different answers, so the buyer will ask which one you mean.

A rising refund rate is also a signal. It can point to product problems, unclear descriptions or disputes, and it often links to [chargebacks](/glossary/chargeback/). Sellers who show net revenue month by month, matched to payouts, as described in [how to prove your revenue to a buyer](/guides/how-to-prove-your-revenue-to-a-buyer/), answer the question before it is asked.
