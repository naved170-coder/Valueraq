## How it works

The IRS puts it simply in Publication 561: "FMV is the price that property would sell for on the open market." The standard idea behind it has three parts:

- **A willing buyer and a willing seller.** Neither is under pressure to act.
- **Both know the relevant facts.** No hidden problems, no inside knowledge.
- **An open market.** The business is offered widely, not to one chosen buyer.

Fair market value is a legal and tax term. Courts, tax authorities and lenders use it when they need a value that doesn't depend on who happens to be buying.

## Example

A website earns $40,000 a year in profit. Comparable sites have sold at around 3 times yearly profit, so its fair market value is about $120,000.

- A competitor who can merge it with their own site pays $150,000. That is a **strategic price**, above fair market value, because this buyer gains more than most would.
- The owner must sell within two weeks and accepts $90,000. That is a **forced sale**, below fair market value.

Neither price changes the fair market value. Both reflect circumstances that the definition rules out.

## Why it matters in a valuation

- **Tax and legal uses.** Gifts, estates, partner buy-outs and disputes usually need fair market value, prepared by a qualified appraiser.
- **Negotiation.** Knowing fair market value tells you whether an offer is strategic, fair or distressed.
- **Calculator results.** {{BRAND}}'s tools give an estimate from your figures and published assumptions. That is a useful guide to fair market value, not a formal appraisal of it.

## Not the same as asking price

An asking price is what a seller hopes to get. Fair market value is what an informed open market would pay. The gap between the two is usually where negotiation happens.
