## How it works

Some businesses are paid on the day of the sale. Others invoice customers and wait, for example "pay within 30 days." Until the invoice is paid, the amount is an asset on the [balance sheet](/glossary/balance-sheet/) called accounts receivable. Revenue is usually recorded when the work is done, but cash only arrives when the customer pays.

Two simple checks show how healthy receivables are:

**Days to collect = accounts receivable ÷ revenue for the period × days in the period**

and an aging list, which groups unpaid invoices by how overdue they are (current, 30 days late, 60 days late, and so on). Old invoices are less likely to be paid.

Many online businesses have little or none, because customers pay by card at checkout. Agencies, ad networks, affiliate programs and businesses that sell to other businesses often have a lot.

## Example

A web agency invoices $90,000 in a quarter (about 90 days). At quarter end, customers still owe $30,000, and $6,000 of that is more than 60 days overdue. The figures are made up.

| Step | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Days to collect | $30,000 ÷ $90,000 × 90 | 30 days |
| Share that is badly overdue | $6,000 ÷ $30,000 | 20% |

A 30-day average is reasonable if invoices are due in 30 days. The $6,000 that is long overdue needs a closer look: it may never be paid.

## Why accounts receivable matter in a valuation

Receivables are real value, but only if customers pay. They matter in three ways when a business is sold:

- **Earnings quality.** Revenue that is booked but not collected is not cash. Buyers compare revenue with cash received, as covered in our [cash flow](/glossary/cash-flow/) entry.
- **Working capital.** Receivables are part of [working capital](/glossary/working-capital/). Many deals expect a normal amount to be left in the business, so how receivables are handled is agreed in the sale terms.
- **Deal structure.** In an [asset sale](/glossary/asset-sale/), the contract states whether the buyer takes over the unpaid invoices or the seller keeps and collects them, and a buyer may discount the old ones.

Read the sale contract with a lawyer and ask an accountant how receivables are treated in your deal.
