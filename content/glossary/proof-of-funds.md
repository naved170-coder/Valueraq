## How it works

Before a seller spends weeks answering questions and sharing records, they want to know the buyer can actually pay. A buyer shows this with proof of funds. Common forms include:

- A recent **bank or brokerage statement** showing the available balance.
- A **letter from a bank or lender** confirming that funds or a loan are available.
- A **mix of cash and financing**, such as part cash and part approved loan, with documents for each.

Buyers usually send it with or soon after their offer, often alongside a [letter of intent](/glossary/letter-of-intent/). On {{BRAND}}, you can attach files to messages, so a buyer can share a proof of funds with a seller privately in the conversation.

Buyers often black out account numbers and anything not needed, and share only what the seller reasonably requires.

## Example

A buyer offers $150,000 for a website. They plan to pay $100,000 from savings and $50,000 from a loan. They send a statement showing $120,000 available and a lender's letter confirming the loan.

**Cash shown + loan = $120,000 + $50,000 = $170,000**, which covers the $150,000 price with $20,000 to spare for costs such as an [escrow](/glossary/escrow/) fee and legal advice. The seller can see the offer is backed.

## Why it matters in a valuation

Proof of funds does not change what a business is worth. It changes how seriously an offer is taken. A strong offer from a buyer who cannot show the money may be less useful to a seller than a slightly lower one that is backed.

It also affects the deal structure. If the buyer can show only part of the price, the sides may agree on [seller financing](/glossary/seller-financing/) for the rest, and the seller then needs to judge the buyer's ability to pay later too.

Sellers should not treat proof of funds as a guarantee. A balance on a statement can change before closing, and the document is not a promise to pay. Paying through escrow is still the safer route. See [buyer safety](/buyer-safety/).

## Take advice

Share only what is needed, and send financial documents through a channel you trust. For large amounts, ask an accountant or lawyer what to include.
