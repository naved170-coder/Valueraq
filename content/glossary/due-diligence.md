## What due diligence covers

| Area | Typical checks for an online business |
|---|---|
| Financial | Profit and loss statements reconciled to bank and payment-processor records |
| Revenue | Reports from affiliate programmes, ad networks, billing systems, app stores |
| Traffic and usage | Read-only analytics, search-console data, active users, list statistics |
| Operations | Owner workload, processes, contractors, suppliers |
| Legal | Ownership of domain, content, code and trademarks; contracts; compliance |
| Technical | Code quality, infrastructure, third-party dependencies |

## Example

A seller lists a website with $5,000 monthly profit. During due diligence, the buyer checks affiliate dashboards and bank deposits, which confirm revenue; reviews 12 months of analytics, which show traffic falling in the last three months; and finds the content was written by contractors under agreements that assign copyright. The buyer proceeds, but offers a lower multiple because of the recent traffic decline.

## Good practice

- Verify figures at their source through read-only access, not screenshots.
- Look at recent months as well as 12-month averages.
- Keep a written list of questions and answers.
- Use professional help (accountant, lawyer, developer) in proportion to the price.

## Due diligence and valuation

Due diligence often changes the price. Evidence that confirms stable earnings supports the asking price; problems lead to a lower price, different terms (such as an earn-out), or walking away. The full [due diligence checklist](/guides/digital-business-due-diligence/) covers every area in detail.
