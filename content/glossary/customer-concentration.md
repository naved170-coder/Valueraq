## How to measure it

**Largest customer share = revenue from the largest customer ÷ total revenue × 100**

Also look at the **top five customers' share**, since several large customers together can be as risky as one.

For online businesses, "customer" can mean an affiliate programme, an ad network, a newsletter sponsor or a wholesale buyer, whoever pays the business.

## Example

A newsletter earns $84,000 a year. Its largest sponsor pays $25,200.

**Largest customer share = $25,200 ÷ $84,000 = 30%.**

If that sponsor stopped, the newsletter would lose nearly a third of its revenue overnight.

## What counts as a customer online

| Business | The "customer" to measure | Example of concentration |
|---|---|---|
| Content or affiliate site | One affiliate program or ad network | One program pays most of the commissions |
| Newsletter | A sponsor | One sponsor buys most of the ad slots |
| SaaS | A client account | One company pays for a large share of seats |
| Ecommerce | A wholesale buyer or marketplace | One reseller orders most of the stock |
| Agency | A retainer client | One client is a quarter of billings |

A heavy reliance on one traffic source is a related risk, covered under [platform risk](/glossary/platform-risk/).

## Risk table and the effect on price

{{BRAND}}'s tools use these steps, described in the [methodology](/methodology/):

| Largest customer's share of revenue | Effect on the multiple |
|---|---|
| 15% or less | No change |
| Over 15% up to 30% | Lowered by 6% |
| Over 30% | Lowered by 12% |

**Worked example.** The newsletter above has $36,000 of yearly profit (made up). At a 3.2× multiple, its value is $36,000 × 3.2 = $115,200. Its largest sponsor is 30% of revenue, which falls in the "over 15% up to 30%" row, so the multiple is lowered by 6%: 3.2 × 0.94 = 3.008, and $36,000 × 3.008 = $108,288. If the sponsor were 31%, the 12% row would apply: 3.2 × 0.88 = 2.816, and $36,000 × 2.816 = $101,376. The step costs $6,912 in value ($108,288 − $101,376). Buyers do not follow a table, so treat this as a rough guide to the direction and size of the effect.

**Top five.** If the five largest sponsors pay $25,200, $14,000, $9,000, $6,000 and $4,000, they add up to $58,200, or $58,200 ÷ $84,000 = 69% of revenue. No single sponsor looks dangerous, but the group is a large share.

## Why it matters for valuation

When a large share of revenue depends on one relationship, a buyer is partly buying that relationship, which could end with the sale or at any time after. Buyers reduce what they'll pay, ask for contracts to be assigned, or request an earn-out tied to the customer staying.

{{BRAND}}'s tools lower the multiple by 6% when one customer provides more than 15% of revenue, and by 12% above 30%. The thresholds are published in the [methodology](/methodology/).

## Reducing concentration before a sale

- Win additional customers or sponsors so the largest share falls.
- Put long-term contracts in writing with key customers.
- Diversify affiliate programmes or ad networks.
- Document the relationship history, so a buyer can judge how stable it is.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a high customer concentration?

There is no universal line. {{BRAND}}'s tools start lowering the multiple above 15% from one customer and again above 30%. Contracts, how long the customer has stayed and how easily it could be replaced all matter too.

### How do you reduce customer concentration?

Win more customers or sponsors so the biggest share falls, and put long contracts in writing with the ones you keep. The list above has more steps.

### Does a long contract remove the risk?

It lowers it, but a buyer will still ask whether the customer would renew. Check that the contract can be assigned to a new owner.

A related risk is [platform risk](/glossary/platform-risk/): depending on one channel or marketplace instead of one customer.
