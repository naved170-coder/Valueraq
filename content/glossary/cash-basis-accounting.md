## How it works

Cash basis accounting is the simplest way to keep books. When money lands in the bank, record income. When money leaves, record the expense. There is nothing to track for unpaid invoices or unpaid bills, because they do not exist in the books until cash moves.

Many one-person websites, newsletters and small online stores keep their books this way because it matches the bank statement and the payment processor reports.

**Profit (cash basis) = cash received in the period − cash paid out in the period**

## Example

An ecommerce store has this month. All figures are made up.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Cash from customers this month | $20,000 |
| Supplier invoice received this month, due next month | $8,000 |
| Supplier invoice paid this month, from last month's stock | $6,000 |
| Other costs paid this month | $4,000 |

On a cash basis: $20,000 − $6,000 − $4,000 = **$10,000 profit**. The $8,000 invoice does not count until it is paid.

On an accrual basis, the stock actually used this month would count: $20,000 − $8,000 − $4,000 = **$8,000 profit**.

The $2,000 difference is only timing. Next month the cash basis catches up, and it will look $2,000 worse than the accrual figure.

## Why cash basis accounting matters in a valuation

- **Single months can mislead.** Annual fees paid early, or bills paid late, move profit from one month to another. Look at a full 12 months, not one month.
- **It hides debts.** Unpaid bills are not on the books. Ask for a list of what the business owes at closing.
- **Buyers may ask for a restatement.** A buyer may want figures on an accrual basis so that income and costs line up. Offering this early saves time in [due diligence](/glossary/due-diligence/).
- **It is easy to verify.** Cash records tie directly to bank and payment statements, which is a strength.

The method a business may use depends on the country and on its size. Ask an accountant.
